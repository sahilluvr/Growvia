Most agents don't have a lead problem. They have a system problem. Leads arrive from Zillow, open houses, a cousin's coworker and a website form, then sit in five different places until they go cold. Meanwhile, the agent down the street with fewer leads closes more deals because every one of them gets a reply, a plan and a reason to stay in touch.

This guide lays out an all-in-one AI marketing system for real estate agents and small teams. It covers what changed after the NAR settlement, how to win your sphere and your neighborhoods, how to follow up without sounding like a robot, where video fits, how to stay inside Fair Housing rules, and which tools to use for which job.

You'll finish with a 90-day plan and a short list of numbers to track. Use AI to do the drafting, the reminders and the reporting. Keep the relationships, the advice and the judgment for yourself.

## What changed after the NAR settlement

The practice changes from the National Association of REALTORS settlement took effect on **August 17, 2024**. Two changes matter most for marketing:

- **Written buyer agreements before touring.** MLS participants working with a buyer need a written agreement in place before touring a home with that buyer. The agreement has to spell out the compensation the agent will receive.
- **No offers of buyer-broker compensation on the MLS.** Compensation can still be negotiated, but it can no longer be advertised through the MLS.

NAR's own summary is on its [facts about the settlement page](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts). Your state law, your brokerage and your MLS may add their own requirements, so check those too. This guide is not legal advice.

For marketing, this means you now explain your value before the first tour, in writing, to someone who may have found you an hour ago. That rewards agents who have already earned trust through content, reviews and consistent follow-up.

On the listing side, sellers ask sharper questions about what they're paying for. A clear marketing plan for their home, backed by real video and real local knowledge, is now a stronger listing tool than it was.

**The short version:** after August 2024, trust has to come before the first showing. Your marketing is how a buyer decides you're worth signing with.

## The growth system at a glance

An all-in-one AI marketing system for real estate agents has five connected parts. Each one feeds the next.

::flow How an agent's marketing system works | Get found locally | Capture the lead | Follow up for months | Win and serve the client | Turn them into reviews and referrals

## Start with your sphere and past clients

Your sphere of influence and past clients are the cheapest source of business you have. They already know you. The job is to stay remembered without being annoying.

A simple sphere system looks like this:

- **One list, cleaned up.** Export contacts from your phone, email and old CRM. Tag each person as past client, sphere, vendor or lead. Delete duplicates.
- **A monthly email that's worth opening.** A short local market note, one useful tip (property tax appeal deadlines, a contractor you trust) and one personal line.
- **Quarterly personal touches.** A call, a handwritten card or a coffee for your top 50. No AI here. This is the part that makes people refer you.
- **Home anniversaries.** Each year on the closing date, send a note with a rough value range and an offer to run a proper analysis.
- **A clear referral ask.** Once or twice a year, tell people plainly that referrals are how you grow, and what kind of client you help best.

::check Sphere marketing basics to set up this week | One clean contact list with tags | Monthly local market email | Home anniversary reminders for every past client | Top 50 list for quarterly personal calls | A short referral ask written in your own words | Review link saved and ready to send

AI can draft the monthly email and flag who you haven't spoken to in 90 days. It can't make the call.

## Hyperlocal SEO and neighborhood content

National portals win the broad searches like "homes for sale in Austin". You can't outspend them, and you don't need to. You win on the questions portals answer badly: what it's like to live on the east side of a specific neighborhood, which streets flood, how the two nearby school attendance zones differ, what sold last month and why.

That's hyperlocal SEO. It means:

- **Neighborhood guides** written from real experience, with honest pros and cons, commute notes, amenities and recent sales context.
- **Monthly market updates** for the three to five areas you actually work, with your own read on the numbers.
- **Answers to real client questions** ("Can I build an ADU in this county?", "What does an HOA transfer fee cover here?").
- **Consistent business details** across your site, your Google profile, your brokerage page and directories.

Our guide to [hyperlocal real estate SEO](/blog/hyperlocal-real-estate-seo) goes deep on neighborhood page structure, IDX trade-offs, schema markup and local links. Read it if your website gets traffic but no leads, or no traffic at all.

One warning: neighborhood content is where Fair Housing problems sneak in. Describe the property and the amenities. Don't describe who lives there or who "would fit in". More on this below.

## Google Business Profile for agents

Google's [Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) allow public-facing individual practitioners, including real estate agents, to have their own profile, with specific rules when you share an office with your brokerage. Read the practitioner section carefully before you create one. If you meet clients at an office during staffed hours, you can show that address. If you work from home and meet clients elsewhere, set yourself up as a service-area business and hide the address.

Google says local results depend mainly on [relevance, distance and prominence](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091). For agents, that translates into a few practical habits:

- **Pick the right primary category.** "Real estate agent" is usually correct. Don't stack unrelated categories.
- **Use your real name and brokerage as required.** Don't stuff keywords like "Best Realtor Miami Homes" into the business name. That violates Google's guidelines and can get the profile suspended.
- **Add real photos.** You, your listings, neighborhoods you work. Update them every month or two.
- **Post weekly.** New listings, open houses, market notes and short videos.
- **Get reviews steadily.** A steady trickle of recent reviews beats a burst from 2022.

Follow the full setup in our [Google Business Profile optimization guide](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-guide), then build a review habit with [how to get more Google reviews within the rules](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews). The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews and buying reviews, and Google bans asking only happy clients. Ask everyone you closed with.

Google Local Services Ads are also available for real estate agents in many US markets. They're pay-per-lead and require screening. They can work for some agents, but they're a separate budget decision, and Growvia doesn't buy or manage ads for you.

## Lead follow-up that actually converts

A lead comes in on a Sunday night. The agent replies Tuesday. By then the person has talked to two other agents, or gone quiet.

Good follow-up has two speeds:

- **Fast.** Reply within minutes during business hours with a real, specific message. Set an instant auto-reply for after hours that says when you'll call.
- **Slow and long.** Most online leads are months from buying or selling. Keep them on a useful nurture for a year or more, with a human check-in every few weeks.

The numbers below are an illustration of how a typical month can look. They aren't a benchmark.

::funnel Example: one month of online leads for a solo agent (illustrative) | New leads: 120 | Replied to a first message: 48 | Had a real conversation: 20 | Booked a consult or tour: 8 | Signed agreement: 3

Notice the biggest drop is between "new lead" and "replied". Speed, a human-sounding first message and a second attempt the next day are what move that number.

Post-NAR, the conversation about the written buyer agreement belongs early, not at the front door of the first showing. Explain it plainly in your first call and in a short email. Our [real estate lead follow-up system](/blog/real-estate-lead-follow-up-system) has the scripts, a 12-month email cadence, how to handle the buyer agreement conversation, and what to track.

On texting: many agents rely on text, and that's fine when it's personal and replying to a lead who contacted you. Automated marketing texts are different. They need prior express written consent under the TCPA, respect quiet hours (8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the recipient's time), and 10DLC registration for local numbers. ## Video and social media

Video is the closest thing to meeting you before you meet. A buyer who has watched you walk a neighborhood for two minutes calls you differently than one who has only seen your headshot.

Three kinds of video earn their keep:

- **Listing videos.** A real walkthrough with your voice explaining the layout, light and trade-offs. Sellers notice.
- **Neighborhood tours.** Coffee shops, parks, commute routes, new construction. These keep working on YouTube for years.
- **Short answers.** 30 to 60 second clips answering one question ("What's a buyer agreement?", "Should I sell before or after I buy?").

A recent phone, a cheap lavalier mic and natural light cover most of it. Drone footage is great, but commercial drone use requires an FAA Part 107 certificate, so hire a licensed pilot or get certified.

Our [real estate video marketing guide](/blog/real-estate-video-social-media) covers listing video shot lists, YouTube SEO for neighborhood tours, reels, AI-assisted scripts, and Fair Housing-safe captions.

A simple weekly rhythm: one listing or sold post, one neighborhood post, one educational clip and one personal post.

## Email newsletters that people read

A good real estate newsletter is short, local and useful. It is not a list of your listings with a stock photo of a house.

A format that works:

1. **One headline number with your read on it.** "Median days on market in our area rose this month. Here's what that means if you're selling this spring."
2. **One useful thing.** A tax deadline, a rebate program, a contractor recommendation, a permit change.
3. **One home or story.** A recent listing, a sale, or a client's (anonymized, permission-based) situation.
4. **One soft call to action.** "Reply if you want a value check on your home."

Keep CAN-SPAM in mind: honest From and subject lines, your physical mailing address, and a working unsubscribe that's honored within 10 business days. The [FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) is short and worth reading once.

Send from your own mailbox and domain, not a no-reply address. Replies are where the business comes from.

## Fair Housing advertising rules

The federal [Fair Housing Act](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/fair_housing_equal_opp) bars discrimination in housing, including advertising that indicates a preference, limitation or discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status or disability. Many states and cities protect more classes, such as source of income, age, marital status, sexual orientation or gender identity. Check your state and local rules.

AI makes this more urgent, not less. AI writing tools often reach for phrases like "perfect for young professionals" or "great family neighborhood" because those phrases are common online. They can still create Fair Housing risk. Always read and edit AI copy before it goes out.

> Warning: describe the property, not the people. Edit every AI-written listing description, caption and neighborhood guide for words that suggest who should or shouldn't live there.

| Instead of this | Write this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect for young families | 3 bedrooms, fenced yard, near two parks | Familial status is protected. Describe features. |
| Walking distance to St. Mary's church | Walking distance to shops, parks and places of worship | Naming one religion's institution can signal preference. |
| Safe, quiet neighborhood | Cul-de-sac street, low traffic | "Safe" is subjective and often coded. Stick to facts. |
| Ideal for empty nesters | Single-level living, low-maintenance yard | Describes the home without targeting an age group. |
| Exclusive community | Gated community with HOA amenities | "Exclusive" can imply exclusion. Name the actual feature. |
| No wheelchairs (or ignoring accessibility) | Step-free entry, wide doorways (if true) | Disability is protected. Describe accessibility features accurately. |

For school information, link to the official district boundary tools and let buyers research for themselves rather than ranking schools as "good" or "bad".

Paid social ads for housing have their own guardrails. Meta, for example, requires housing ads to run in a special ad category with limited targeting options. Don't try to target around it.

## Where AI helps and where it doesn't

AI is good at volume and first drafts. It's bad at knowing your market, your clients and the law. Use it accordingly.

::compare Agent marketing with and without a system | Without: leads spread across portals, inbox and phone; replies when you remember; newsletter every few months; no idea which source closes | With: one pipeline for every lead; instant auto-reply and next-day follow-up; monthly AI-drafted newsletter you edit; reports by lead source

| Task | Good use of AI | Keep human |
|---|---|---|
| Listing descriptions | First draft from your notes and photos | Accuracy, Fair Housing edit, final voice |
| Market updates | Summarizing your MLS stats into plain English | Your interpretation and advice |
| Follow-up emails | Drafting a 12-month nurture sequence | First call, buyer agreement conversation |
| Social posts | Captions, hashtags, repurposing a video into 5 posts | What you film and say on camera |

| Reviews | Drafting a reply to a review | Deciding what to say about a real client |
| Reporting | Pulling numbers into one weekly summary | Deciding what to change |

## Your 90-day plan

This plan assumes a solo agent or small team with a few hours a week for marketing. Adjust the pace to your reality.

::steps 90-day real estate marketing plan | Days 1–14 — clean your contact list, fix your Google Business Profile, set up lead auto-replies | Days 15–30 — write two neighborhood guides and send your first monthly newsletter | Days 31–45 — build a 12-month nurture sequence and start a weekly review ask | Days 46–60 — film one listing video and two neighborhood tours | Days 61–75 — run an SEO audit and fix the top five issues | Days 76–90 — review lead sources and double down on what closed

A few details that make the plan stick. In week one, put an instant auto-reply on every lead form and portal and save your review link where you can paste it in seconds. When you film in weeks 7 and 8, cut each video into three short clips so one shoot feeds a month of posts. In the final two weeks, judge each lead source by signed agreements and closings, not by lead count, and cut what didn't produce.

## The numbers to track

You don't need a dashboard with 40 metrics. Track a handful every month.

| Metric | What it tells you | How to measure |
|---|---|---|
| First response time | Whether leads hear from you while they're still interested | CRM or pipeline timestamps |
| Lead-to-conversation rate | Whether your first messages sound human | Conversations divided by new leads |
| Signed buyer agreements and listing agreements | Whether conversations turn into clients | Count per month |
| Closings by lead source | Where your business really comes from | Tag every lead with its source |
| Reviews per month | Whether your review habit is running | Google Business Profile |
| Newsletter replies | Whether your sphere is engaged | Count replies, not just opens |

For response time, here's a simple way to grade yourself:

::scale How fast do you reply to new leads? | Good: within 5 minutes in business hours, with an after-hours auto-reply | Needs work: same day | Poor: next day or later

Treat email opens as a weak signal. Replies, booked calls and signed agreements matter more.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best all-in-one AI marketing tool for real estate agents?

There isn't one tool that does everything well. Most agents pair a real estate CRM such as Follow Up Boss (for calling, texting and portal leads) with a marketing tool such as Growvia (for SEO, Google Business Profile, email, social and reporting).

### How did the NAR settlement change real estate marketing?

Since August 17, 2024, MLS participants need a written buyer agreement before touring a home with a buyer, and buyer-broker compensation can't be offered on the MLS. That puts more weight on earning trust before the first showing through content, reviews and follow-up.

### Can I use AI to write listing descriptions?

Yes, for a first draft. Always check facts against the listing and edit for Fair Housing issues, since AI tools often use phrases that describe ideal buyers instead of the property.

### Can real estate agents have their own Google Business Profile?

Google's guidelines allow public-facing individual practitioners such as real estate agents to have their own profile, with specific rules when sharing an office with a brokerage. If you don't meet clients at an office, set up as a service-area business and hide your address.

### How often should a real estate agent send a newsletter?

Monthly is a good default for your sphere and past clients. Keep it short and local, and measure replies rather than opens.

### How long should I follow up with a real estate lead?

At least 12 months for most online leads, since many are months away from moving. Combine an automated email nurture with a personal check-in every few weeks.
