A residential lawn route is a good business. A route that also includes an HOA, two office parks and a shopping center is a much better one. Commercial accounts pay on schedule, cover more ground per stop and renew year after year when the work is consistent. They also give you leverage with suppliers and a reason to keep crews employed all season.

This guide walks through who to target, how to find the person who signs, and how to write a proposal that's easy to approve.


## Why commercial and HOA work is worth pursuing

Residential lawn routes are dependable until they aren't. Homeowners move, cancel when budgets tighten, switch to a neighbor's kid or decide to mow themselves in a cool spring. Each cancellation leaves a gap on the route that has to be refilled with marketing.

Commercial and association accounts behave differently. An HOA's common areas need mowing, edging and bed care every week of the season, and the board votes on vendors once a year rather than week by week. A property manager with several apartment communities wants one landscaper across all of them. An office park needs its entrance to look sharp for tenants every Monday morning. These accounts fill large blocks of crew time at one stop, which means less driving, fewer trailer loads and better margins per hour.

Commercial work also brings natural add-ons: seasonal color, mulch refreshes, irrigation repairs, tree trimming, leaf removal and, in colder regions, snow removal. When you already maintain the property, you're the obvious choice for each of these extras, and they can add up to a meaningful share of the account's value.

The trade-off is competition and paperwork. Commercial bids are compared closely, boards ask questions and property managers expect reporting. Companies that make all of that easy win and keep these accounts.

## Timing matters

Many HOAs and property managers review vendor contracts in late autumn and winter for the next season. Reach out then with a proposal, and again in early spring for sites that need help fast.

::steps Commercial landscaping calendar | Oct–Dec: contact managers before budgets are set | Jan–Feb: walk sites and send proposals | Mar: start new contracts with a strong first clean-up | Mid-season: send a short photo report | Autumn: renew and upsell seasonal color or irrigation

## The proposal that gets approved

Boards and managers compare bids side by side. Make yours the easiest to understand.

1. **A site map or photo** showing what's included.
2. **A task schedule:** mowing frequency, edging, bed maintenance, pruning, leaf removal.
3. **Clear exclusions** so there are no surprise invoices.
4. **One monthly price** for the season, with optional add-ons priced separately.
5. **A named account manager** and how quickly you respond to issues.

::compare What makes a bid stand out | Typical bid: one-line price; vague scope; no contact person | Your bid: photo map; task calendar; clear add-ons; named manager

## Keeping the account

Send a short monthly note with two or three photos of the property. Managers forward them to owners and boards, and it reminds everyone the work is happening. Fix complaints within a day. Renewals are decided by the small things.

## Sample outreach email

> **Subject:** Grounds at Maple Ridge for next season
>
> Hi Alicia — we maintain grounds for two communities near Maple Ridge. Before you set next year's budget, would a free site walk and a written scope be useful? Our proposals list every task by month, so the board can compare bids line by line.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Bidding blind.** Always walk the site.
- **Underpricing irrigation and clean-ups.** Price them separately so the base bid stays fair.
- **Different crews every week.** Consistency is what residents notice.

## Your first site walk with a manager

1. What does the board or owner complain about most?
2. Which areas matter most to residents, tenants or customers?
3. What's included in the current contract, and what's billed as extra?
4. Are there irrigation problems or drainage issues we should know about?
5. When is the budget set and when do contracts renew?
6. How do you want to receive updates and photos?
7. Who approves the contract: you, the board or an owner?

Walk the property with them if possible, and take photos as you go. Afterwards, your proposal should reflect every concern they mentioned, so they can see you listened.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We're under contract." | There's a renewal date. | Ask when it ends and when bids are reviewed; set a reminder. |
| "The board picks the lowest bid." | Price pressure. | Offer a base bid plus optional add-ons so the board can compare fairly. |
| "Our current vendor is fine." | Not dissatisfied enough. | Offer to be on file for extras like seasonal color or irrigation repairs. |
| "Send a price." | They want a number without a walk. | Explain that a site walk prevents surprises and keeps the bid accurate. |
| "We need insurance documents." | Standard requirement. | Send certificates the same day. |

## Getting your company ready for commercial buyers

Property managers and boards check credentials before they vote. Keep liability insurance and workers' compensation certificates ready to send, and list any applicator licenses your state requires for treatments. Build a commercial page on your website with photos of properties you maintain, your services by season and how you report to managers. Make sure your trucks and crews look professional on site, because board members will notice. A short list of references from current commercial clients often decides close votes.

## Add-ons that grow account value

**Seasonal color.** Annual flower rotations at entrances and signs make properties look cared for. Offer two or three rotations a year at a set price.

**Irrigation management.** Start-up in spring, shut-down in autumn, monthly checks and repairs. Water waste and dead patches are common complaints that you can solve.

**Tree and shrub care.** Pruning, hazard checks near walkways and periodic trimming keep properties safe and tidy.

**Mulch and bed refreshes.** A yearly refresh is one of the most visible improvements a property can get.

**Snow and ice removal.** In colder regions, a winter contract keeps crews employed and makes you indispensable to managers.

Present these as clearly priced options alongside the base bid. Boards often approve one or two add-ons once they trust you.

## A worked example

Here's how an association account might come together. The details are illustrative.

In early November, you email a community association management firm that oversees several townhome communities near your routes. The manager responds to your follow-up about side-by-side bid formats because her board has struggled to compare proposals. You walk one community with her, note patchy turf, overgrown beds at the entrance and an irrigation zone that floods a walkway.

Your proposal includes a site map, a month-by-month task calendar, irrigation repairs as a separate line, an optional seasonal color package and the name of your account manager. The board votes for you in January, partly because your format made comparison easy. You start with a thorough spring clean-up and send the manager monthly photo updates.

By late summer, the board approves seasonal color and mulch refreshes. The manager mentions your monthly photos at her firm's meeting, and you're invited to bid on two more communities the following season.

## Presenting to an HOA board

Many association contracts are decided at a board meeting, and some boards invite bidders to present. Treat it as a short, focused conversation. Bring printed copies of your proposal with the site map and task calendar, and summarize the three things that matter most to residents: consistent mowing and edging, tidy beds at entrances and quick responses to complaints. Explain how you'll communicate with the community manager and how often you'll send photo updates.

Expect questions about price, crew consistency, insurance and what happens if residents complain. Answer directly and refer to the written proposal. Keep your presentation to ten minutes, leaving time for questions. Boards are made up of volunteers who want the decision to be simple and defensible; a clear, organized bid that's easy to compare gives them exactly that.

## Handling resident complaints

Residents notice everything, and complaints often go to the board before they reach you. Agree with the community manager on a single channel for complaints, respond within a day and fix the issue promptly. Keep a simple log of complaints and resolutions, and mention trends in your monthly update. When boards see that complaints are handled quickly and transparently, renewals become routine.

## Managing crews across many commercial sites

As commercial accounts grow, scheduling becomes the hardest part. Group sites by area and day, assign a lead to each route and keep site notes on gate codes, irrigation zones, special requests and areas to avoid. Use a short checklist at each stop so nothing is missed. Track time per site to check that each account is priced correctly. When an account consistently takes longer than planned, it's time to adjust the scope or the price at renewal.

## Using photos to prove value

Photos are the simplest proof that work is happening. Take before-and-after photos of key areas each month: entrances, beds, problem spots and completed extras. Share a few with the manager in your monthly update. Boards and owners who never walk the property themselves see the results, and renewals become an easy decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do landscapers get commercial contracts?

Target property managers, HOA management companies and office parks. Contact them before budgets are set, walk each site and send a clear, itemized proposal.

### When do HOAs choose landscaping vendors?

Many review contracts in late autumn or winter before the next season. Reaching out then gives you time to walk the site and propose.

### What should a landscaping proposal include?

A site map, the task schedule, exclusions, a monthly price and the person responsible for the account.

### Is commercial landscaping more profitable than residential?

It often brings steadier, denser work with less driving per dollar, but bids are competitive. Profit comes from accurate scoping and efficient routes.

### Do landscapers need special licenses for commercial work?

Requirements vary by state. Many require licenses for pesticide or fertilizer application and certain irrigation work. Check your state's rules and list your licenses in proposals.

### How do I price snow removal for commercial clients?

Common approaches are per push, per event or a seasonal contract. Seasonal contracts give both sides predictability; define what triggers a visit and what's included.

### Should landscapers send photo updates to clients?

Yes. Short monthly photo updates show managers and boards that work is happening and make renewals easier.

### How many commercial accounts should a small landscaping company take on?

As many as your crews can serve consistently. Adding accounts along existing routes is the most profitable way to grow.

### How do I find out when an HOA's landscaping contract renews?

Ask the community manager. Many associations renew annually and review bids before budgets are approved.

### Should landscapers offer multi-year contracts?

Some associations prefer them for stability. Include clear terms for price adjustments and performance reviews.

### How do landscapers handle irrigation in commercial bids?

List routine checks in the base price and price repairs separately, so the base bid stays predictable.

### What do property managers expect in monthly reports?

A short summary of work completed, photos and any issues or recommendations.

### How do I win back an account I lost?

Stay in touch politely, and reach out before the next renewal with a fresh proposal.

### How many photos should landscapers send in monthly updates?

A handful of clear photos of key areas is usually enough to show progress without overwhelming the reader.

### How should landscapers handle extra work requests?

Quote extras in writing before starting, and get approval from the manager or board.

### Do HOAs require landscapers to attend meetings?

Some invite vendors to present or answer questions; be prepared with your proposal and references.

### What insurance do commercial landscapers need?

Most property managers and boards expect general liability insurance and workers' compensation where required, and some ask for commercial auto coverage. Keep certificates current and send them with every proposal so the paperwork never slows a decision.
