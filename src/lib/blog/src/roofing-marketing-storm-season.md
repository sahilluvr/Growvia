After a big hailstorm, every roofer in the region is busy, and so are a few dozen trucks with out-of-state plates. Homeowners get door knocks, flyers and calls for weeks. Some of those visitors do honest work. Others disappear with a deposit or leave a leaking roof behind. Homeowners know this, which is why trust is the real currency in **roofing marketing**.

The roofers who grow year after year don't win by knocking on more doors after a storm. They win because homeowners already know their name, can see dozens of reviews with real photos, and find a clear, honest explanation of how the insurance process works on their website. When the storm comes, those roofers get the call first.

This guide shows you how to build that position. ## How roofing demand really works

Roofing demand comes in two very different shapes, and your marketing needs to serve both.

| Demand type | Trigger | What the homeowner wants | How you win |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storm-driven | Hail, high wind, hurricanes, falling trees | A trustworthy inspection, help understanding insurance, quick tarping | Local reputation, reviews with photos, fast response, clear claim content |
| Planned replacement | Age, curling shingles, a leak, selling the house | Fair price, quality materials, a clean job | Service pages, financing, comparisons, referrals |
| Repairs and maintenance | Leaks, missing shingles, flashing, gutters | Someone who will take a small job | Map Pack, quick scheduling, honest repair-vs-replace advice |

Storm work can be huge, but it's unpredictable. Planned replacements and repairs keep crews working in the months between storms. A roofer who only markets after storms rides a roller coaster. One who markets all year stays busy and gets the storm work on top.

## Ethical storm response: how not to look like a storm chaser

Homeowners and insurers have learned to be wary of "storm chasers," the out-of-area crews that canvass hit neighborhoods, pressure people into signing and leave once the work dries up. The FTC's [home repair scams guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/pass-it-on/home-repair-scams) warns consumers about unsolicited contractors, pressure tactics and large upfront payments. Your marketing should make it obvious you're the opposite.

### What ethical storm response looks like

- **Lead with your local history.** Years in business, your office or yard location, local crews, local references.
- **Offer inspections with photo documentation,** not instant verdicts. Show the homeowner what you found, on their roof, in photos.
- **Don't promise a free roof.** Whether insurance covers a replacement is the insurer's decision. Say so plainly.
- **Never offer to waive or cover the deductible.** It's prohibited in several states and can amount to insurance fraud.
- **Don't pressure people to sign on the spot.** Give them time and a written estimate.
- **Explain cancellation rights.** The FTC's [Cooling-Off Rule](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buyers-remorse-ftcs-cooling-rule-may-help) gives buyers three business days to cancel many sales of $25 or more made at their home. Some states add longer or more specific rights for storm-related roofing contracts.

### State contractor rules to check

Rules vary a lot by state, and some changed after major storms. Common areas to check:

- **Licensing or registration.** Some states license roofers statewide; others leave it to cities or counties. Many cities require contractor registration and permits for re-roofing.
- **Deductible rules.** Texas Insurance Code Chapter 707, for example, prohibits contractors from paying, waiving or rebating an insured's deductible.
- **Solicitation and inducements.** Florida law (section 489.147) restricts roofing contractors from offering gifts or incentives to prompt a claim and from interpreting insurance policies for homeowners unless properly licensed.
- **Public adjusting.** In most states, only a licensed public adjuster or attorney can negotiate a claim on the homeowner's behalf. Roofers can document damage and meet the adjuster, but shouldn't act as the homeowner's negotiator.
- **Door-to-door rules.** Some cities require solicitor permits or ban canvassing at certain hours.

> **Not legal advice:** These are examples, and laws change. Check your state's current rules with your licensing board, state insurance department and an attorney before running storm campaigns.

## Insurance-claim education content

Most homeowners have never filed a roof claim. They don't know whether to call the insurer or a roofer first, what an adjuster does, or what "depreciation" means. The roofer who explains this clearly, without overpromising, earns trust before the first phone call.

### Pages and posts worth creating

- **"What to do after a hailstorm: a homeowner's checklist."** Safety first, photograph damage from the ground, check gutters and soft metals, call your insurer, get a local inspection.
- **"How a roof insurance claim works, step by step."** Filing, adjuster visit, the estimate, approval, deductible, depreciation and final payment. Keep it general and tell readers to check their own policy.
- **"Hail damage vs normal wear: what adjusters look for."** With your own photos.
- **"Should I repair or replace my roof?"** Your honest rules of thumb by age and damage.
- **"How to check a roofer's license and insurance"** in your state. It positions you as transparent, and it quietly filters out fly-by-night competitors.

::steps A homeowner's roof claim journey (explain it on your site) | Document damage — photos from the ground and a local inspection | Contact the insurer — file the claim and get a claim number | Adjuster visit — your inspector can attend if the homeowner wants | Review the estimate — compare scope with your findings | Schedule the work — permit, install, final inspection and invoice

### Rules for claim content

Don't tell readers their claim will be approved, don't advise them to exaggerate damage and don't present yourself as their insurance representative. Describe the general process and recommend they read their policy and talk to their insurer. This keeps you within the rules and builds more trust than a "we'll get you a free roof" headline ever could.

## Google Business Profile for roofers

Most roofing searches end in the Map Pack. Your Business Profile is often the first thing a homeowner sees.

### Setup that avoids trouble

Use **Roofing contractor** as your primary category. If you don't receive customers at your office, Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say to hide your address and set a service area. Don't create a temporary listing at a hotel or virtual office in a storm-hit city. Google treats that as a fake location, and it's a classic storm-chaser signal to homeowners too.

### Photos do the selling

Roofing is visual. Before-and-after photos, aerial shots, crew photos and close-ups of hail damage and new installs show homeowners what they're buying. Post new photos weekly. Avoid showing house numbers or anything that identifies a customer's home without permission.

## Local Services Ads for roofers

Roofing is an eligible Local Services Ads category in the US. LSA puts you at the top of results with a **Google Verified** badge, and you pay per lead instead of per click. Screening typically includes background checks, license checks where required and proof of insurance; requirements vary by state, as Google's [screening and verification page](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/12174778?hl=en) explains.

LSA can be especially useful after storms, when homeowners look for trustworthy names fast. A few tips:

- Respond fast. Responsiveness affects ranking, and post-storm leads go to whoever calls back first.
- Select only job types you want (inspection, repair, replacement, gutters) and turn off what you don't do.
- Dispute leads that are clearly invalid (wrong trade, out of area).
- Raise your budget only if your crews can handle more work. A surge of leads you can't serve hurts your reputation.

## Reviews with photos

For roofers, a review with a photo of the finished roof is worth several text-only reviews. It shows real work on a real house and makes it far harder for a homeowner to mistake you for a fly-by-night crew.

### How to ask

- Ask at the final walkthrough, once the homeowner has seen the cleanup and the finished roof.
- Send the review link the same day by email or from your project software.
- Encourage customers to add a photo, and send them the best after-photo from your crew so they can use it.
- Ask every customer, not only the happiest. Google bans review gating, and the FTC's [Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/rule-consumer-reviews-testimonials) bans fake reviews, paid reviews and suppressing negative ones.

Reply to every review, naming the job type and town where the customer mentioned them ("Thanks for trusting us with your roof replacement in Plano"). For more scripts, see [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews).

::scale How trustworthy does your roofing company look online? | Good: dozens of recent reviews with photos, local address or service area, license shown, claim guide on site | Needs work: some reviews, few photos, license not visible | Poor: handful of old reviews, stock photos, no license or insurance details, "free roof" promises

## Financing: make big jobs possible

A full roof replacement is one of the largest repairs a homeowner faces. When insurance doesn't cover it, or only covers part, financing can decide whether the job happens.

- **Create a financing page** that explains your options, typical terms and how to apply. Link it from every replacement page.
- **Mention it early** in the estimate conversation, not as a last resort.
- **Follow the advertising rules.** If you advertise specific terms like a monthly payment or a rate, federal Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z) generally require additional disclosures. Your financing partner should give you approved language. Use it.
- **Be careful with "0% interest" claims.** Deferred-interest promotions must be explained clearly, or customers feel tricked later.

## The 30-day post-storm playbook

When a storm hits your area, use a plan you wrote in advance rather than improvising.

1. **Day 0 to 1:** Check that your phones and forms work. Post safety tips and your availability on your Business Profile and social channels. Update your hours.
2. **Day 1 to 3:** Email past customers in the affected area with a short note: how to check for damage safely, and how to book an inspection. Prioritize emergency tarping for active leaks.
3. **Day 3 to 14:** Publish or update your "what to do after a hailstorm" page. Run inspections with photo reports. Adjust LSA budget to crew capacity.
4. **Day 14 to 30:** Follow up on every inspection with a written estimate and a timeline. Ask for reviews on finished jobs. Keep homeowners informed about scheduling.

## Common roofing marketing mistakes

- **Marketing only after storms,** then scrambling for work for the rest of the year.
- **Promising free roofs or deductible help.** It breaks laws in several states and erodes trust.
- **Stock photos instead of real jobs.**
- **Hiding your license and insurance.** Homeowners look for them.
- **Fake locations** in storm-hit towns to rank in Maps.
- **No follow-up after inspections.** Many homeowners need a nudge and a written estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do roofers get more leads?

Build a strong Google Business Profile with photo reviews, run Local Services Ads, publish helpful insurance-claim and repair-vs-replace content and keep in touch with past customers. Market all year, not only after storms.

### Is door-to-door canvassing after a storm legal?

It depends on your state and city. Many cities require solicitor permits or limit hours, and some states have specific rules for storm-related roofing sales. Check your local rules and follow the FTC Cooling-Off Rule.

### Can a roofer help with an insurance claim?

A roofer can inspect, document damage and meet the adjuster if the homeowner wants. In most states, only a licensed public adjuster or attorney can negotiate the claim on the homeowner's behalf, so check your state's rules.

### Can I offer to pay a customer's insurance deductible?

No in many states. Texas, for example, prohibits contractors from paying, waiving or rebating a deductible, and it can be considered insurance fraud. Check your state's current rules.

### Are Local Services Ads good for roofing companies?

They can be, especially after storms when homeowners want a verified local name quickly. Results depend on fast response, good reviews and matching your budget to crew capacity.
