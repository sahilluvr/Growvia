Immigration law has two very different markets. Family and humanitarian cases come from individuals, often through community networks and word of mouth. Business immigration comes from employers: companies hiring international talent, startups bringing in founders, universities supporting scholars. Business clients bring repeat matters, predictable timelines and long relationships.

This guide focuses on building that business side through outreach to employers and professional partners. For the broader picture, read [AI marketing for lawyers](/blog/ai-marketing-for-lawyers) and our guide to [lawyer advertising rules by state](/blog/lawyer-advertising-rules-by-state).


## Why business immigration work is worth building

Family and humanitarian immigration matters are deeply meaningful work, but they tend to be one-time engagements driven by individual circumstances. Business immigration is different. A growing company that sponsors one international hire usually sponsors more. A hospital that brings in clinicians does so every year. A university hosts visiting scholars every semester. Each employer relationship brings a stream of matters, from initial petitions to extensions, changes and permanent residence processes.

Employer clients also value predictability, which suits law firms. They plan hiring cycles, budget for legal fees and want a reliable partner who can explain options and timelines to managers and candidates. A firm that becomes that partner can grow with the client's workforce.

There's also a strategic benefit. Business immigration practices often get referrals from HR consultants, accountants, relocation firms and corporate attorneys, all of whom serve many employers. One strong relationship with a referral partner can introduce you to several companies a year.

Finally, business work tends to be less dependent on individual advertising, where rules on solicitation and client vulnerability require extra care. Outreach to organizations about their planning needs is easier to keep professional and compliant, though it still needs to follow your bar's rules.

## Ethics first

Lawyer marketing rules differ by state. Outreach to businesses about their needs is generally treated differently from contacting individuals who may need legal help, but rules on solicitation, advertising labels and filing requirements vary. Check your state bar's rules before starting, especially on:

- How communications must be labeled.
- Whether certain messages must be filed or kept on record.
- Restrictions on paying for referrals (generally prohibited).
- Claims about results or specialization.

Our [lawyer advertising rules by state](/blog/lawyer-advertising-rules-by-state) guide is a starting point, not a substitute for your bar's current rules.

## Your first conversation with HR

1. How often do you hire candidates who need work authorization?
2. Which roles are hardest to fill locally?
3. Who manages immigration matters now, and how is it going?
4. What does your team find most confusing about the process?
5. How do you budget for immigration costs?
6. How do you prefer to receive status updates?

Avoid giving specific legal advice in a general conversation. Offer to review specific situations under an engagement, and keep any general information clearly educational.

## Preparing your firm

Employers will research you before replying. Make sure your website has a clear business immigration page explaining who you serve, the types of matters you handle, how engagement works and how fees are structured. Keep attorney bios current. Prepare a plain-English planning guide and a short briefing outline. Review all outreach templates against your state bar's advertising rules, and keep records as required. Make sure your intake process can respond to employer inquiries quickly.

## Ways to provide value to employers

**Planning guides.** Short, plain-English guides on options and timelines for hiring managers.

**Hiring manager briefings.** Virtual or in-person sessions on the basics, including what managers shouldn't promise candidates.

**Annual planning calls.** A yearly conversation about upcoming hiring needs and expiring authorizations.

**Status dashboards.** Clear, regular updates on each matter, so HR can answer questions from managers and employees.

**Partner webinars.** Joint sessions with HR consultancies or chambers of commerce on changes and planning.

## A worked example

Here's how an employer relationship might begin. The details are illustrative.

You email the head of people at a growing software company, offering a planning guide for international hires. She replies to your follow-up because her team is about to make an offer to a candidate abroad and isn't sure where to start. You schedule a short call, explain the general process and propose an engagement for that candidate.

You handle the first matter with clear timelines and regular updates. HR appreciates being able to answer the hiring manager's questions confidently. A few months later, they ask you to run a briefing for all hiring managers and engage you for two more candidates.

At their annual planning meeting, the head of people introduces you to an HR consultant who advises several local startups. That consultant becomes a referral partner, introducing you to new employers over the following year.

## Keeping relationships strong over time

Schedule an annual planning call with each employer client to review upcoming hires and expiring authorizations. Send short updates when significant changes affect their workforce, following your bar's rules on communications. Keep fees predictable and explain any changes early. Thank referral partners and keep them informed, with client consent, about how introductions went.

## Communicating with HR, managers and candidates

Business immigration matters involve several people: HR, hiring managers, the candidate or employee and sometimes their family. Each needs different information. HR wants timelines, costs and status updates. Hiring managers want to know when the person can start and what they should avoid saying. Candidates want to understand their options and what to expect.

Agree with the employer at the start who receives which updates, and respect confidentiality between parties where appropriate. Use plain-English summaries and avoid jargon. Regular, predictable updates, even when there's nothing new, reduce anxious emails and make HR's job easier. Employers remember firms that make complex processes feel manageable, and they bring those firms back for every future hire.

## Planning ahead with employer clients

Proactive planning sets business immigration firms apart. Offer an annual review with each employer client covering upcoming hires, expiring authorizations, extensions and any changes in the employer's structure, such as new locations or acquisitions. Track key dates for every employee you represent and contact the employer well before deadlines. This approach reduces emergencies, spreads work more evenly through the year and shows employers that you're looking out for their workforce.

## Building partner referrals carefully

HR consultants, relocation companies and accounting firms meet employers that need immigration help. Introduce yourself with a short summary of your practice and the types of employers you serve. Offer educational briefings for their clients. Keep partners informed, with client consent, about how introductions went. Never offer or accept anything of value for referrals, and review any joint marketing against your bar's rules. Partners who see their clients handled well keep making introductions.

## Supporting employees and their families

Business immigration affects people's lives deeply. Employees often worry about timelines, travel, family members and their future in the country. While the employer is often the client, treating employees with clarity and respect strengthens the relationship with the company. Provide plain-language explanations of the process, set realistic expectations and respond promptly to questions within the scope of your representation. Employers notice when their employees feel supported, and positive feedback from staff makes HR more likely to rely on you again.

## Managing fees and budgets

Employers budget for immigration costs, and surprises damage trust. Explain fees clearly at the start, separating legal fees from government fees and other costs. Discuss possible additional work, such as responses to requests for evidence, and how it's priced. Send invoices that are easy to match to each matter. Predictable billing makes it easier for HR to plan and for finance teams to approve.

## Keeping knowledge current

Immigration rules and processes change often. Set aside time to follow updates from official sources and professional associations, and share relevant changes with employer clients in short, plain-language notes, within your bar's rules on communications. Employers value counsel who keeps them ahead of changes rather than reacting after problems arise.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do immigration lawyers get business clients?

Build relationships with employers that hire internationally and with referral partners such as HR consultancies, accounting firms and relocation companies. Offer useful planning guides and briefings.

### Can immigration lawyers email businesses?

Generally yes, within your state bar's advertising and solicitation rules and US email law. Check requirements on labeling and record-keeping before sending.

### Who refers business immigration clients?

HR consultants, accounting firms, relocation companies, universities and other attorneys who don't practice immigration law.

### What should immigration attorneys never promise?

Specific outcomes or approval timelines. Explain options and typical processes instead.

### Do immigration law firms need to label marketing emails?

Some states require specific labels or disclaimers on lawyer advertising. Check your state bar's current rules before sending.

### What do employers want from immigration counsel?

Clear timelines, predictable fees, regular status updates and plain-English explanations they can share with managers and candidates.

### Can immigration attorneys offer free briefings to employers?

Educational briefings are common. Keep them general and avoid giving specific legal advice outside an engagement.

### How do immigration firms build referral partners?

By serving referred clients well, keeping partners informed with consent and offering useful resources, without paying for referrals.

### How do immigration firms price business matters?

Many use flat fees for common filings, with separate government fees and clear terms for additional work.

### What should employers know before offering a job to an international candidate?

That options and timelines vary, and that speaking with immigration counsel before making promises can prevent problems.

### Can immigration attorneys represent both the employer and employee?

This depends on the matter and ethics rules. Explain the relationship clearly and follow your jurisdiction's rules on joint representation.

### How do business immigration firms track deadlines?

With case management systems that track key dates for each employee and send reminders well in advance.

### Should immigration firms offer webinars for HR teams?

Educational webinars on planning and process are common and helpful, as long as they follow bar advertising rules.

### What's the best way to start working with a new employer?

Begin with a planning conversation and a single matter handled well, then expand to annual planning.

### Who is the client in business immigration matters?

Often the employer, though the employee is closely involved. Explain the relationship and any joint representation clearly.

### How do immigration firms help employees understand the process?

With plain-language explanations, realistic expectations and prompt answers within the scope of representation.

### Should immigration firms separate legal and government fees?

Yes, clearly separating them helps employers budget and approve costs.

### What happens if a case needs additional work?

Explain upfront how additional work, such as responding to requests for evidence, is handled and priced.

### How can firms keep HR informed?

With regular status updates, even when there's no change.

### How do immigration firms keep clients informed about changes?

With short, plain-language updates on relevant changes, sent when they affect the client.

### How do immigration firms choose which employers to target?

By focusing on industries and sizes that hire internationally and fit the firm's experience.

### What makes HR teams choose one immigration firm over another?

Clear timelines, predictable fees, responsive communication and plain-English explanations they can share with managers and candidates.
