Occupational health is a business-to-business service wearing a medical coat. The patient walks in, but the customer is an employer: a construction company that needs pre-employment physicals, a trucking firm that needs DOT exams, a warehouse that needs a clinic ready when someone gets hurt on shift.

Employers choose a clinic once and stick with it when it works. That makes outreach to local employers one of the highest-return growth activities for occupational health and urgent care clinics. This guide covers which employers to target, how to reach them and how to make signing up painless. See also our [medical practice marketing guide](/blog/ai-marketing-for-medical-practices).


## Why employer accounts are the core of occupational health

Occupational health clinics serve patients, but employers make the buying decision. A construction company decides where its new hires go for physicals. A trucking firm decides where drivers get DOT exams. A warehouse decides where an injured worker is sent on the night shift. Once that decision is made, it usually stays made until something goes wrong.

That gives employer accounts unusual value. One relationship with a staffing agency can mean a steady flow of pre-placement exams every week. A logistics company with hundreds of drivers needs certifications renewed on a cycle. A manufacturer with injury risk needs a clinic ready every day. Each account brings repeat visits without repeat marketing.

Employer accounts also help clinics plan. Pre-employment exams and screenings can be scheduled into quieter times, staffing can be matched to predictable volume and billing becomes simpler with a handful of large accounts rather than many individual payers. For urgent care centers, occupational health accounts can fill weekday mornings that walk-in traffic leaves empty.

The flip side is that employers judge you on speed and paperwork. If a new hire can't start because results are late, or an injury report is unclear, the account moves elsewhere. Clinics that make the employer's job easier keep accounts for years.

## The offer: make it easy to say yes

Employers need a process more than a price list:

::flow How an employer gets started | Short call with HR or safety lead | One-page service menu and pricing | Account set up with an authorization form | Employees walk in or book | Results sent to the employer contact

Include on your one-pager: services, turnaround times, hours, walk-in policy, how results are delivered and who to call.

## Privacy boundaries matter

Employers receive only what they're entitled to, such as work-status or clearance results; detailed medical information stays protected. Make this clear in your agreements and train front-desk staff on what can be shared. ## Mistakes to avoid

- **Marketing to employees instead of employers.** The employer chooses the clinic.
- **Slow results.** A new hire who can't start costs the employer money.
- **Unclear billing.** Invoice employers consistently and on time.

## Your first conversation with an employer

1. How many people do you hire in a typical month, and in peak season?
2. Which services do you need: pre-employment exams, drug testing, DOT physicals, injury care?
3. How quickly do new hires need to start after an offer?
4. Who should receive results, and in what format?
5. What happens today when someone is injured on a night or weekend shift?
6. How is billing handled with your current provider?
7. What frustrates you about your current clinic?

Answer each frustration with a specific process. If they wait days for results, explain your turnaround. If injured workers wait hours, explain your triage for employer accounts.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We already use a clinic." | Existing account. | Offer to be a backup site for locations further from their current clinic. |
| "Your prices are higher." | Comparing service menus line by line. | Show turnaround times and the cost of a new hire waiting to start. |
| "We need you open late." | Shift work. | Share your hours and any after-hours options; be honest about limits. |
| "Corporate decides." | Regional or national policy. | Ask for the corporate contact and offer to support local sites. |
| "We'll call when we hire." | Low urgency. | Offer to set up the account now so the first hire goes smoothly. |

## Preparing your clinic

Employers check credentials. Make sure your website has a clear employer services page listing services, hours, walk-in policy, turnaround times and how to set up an account. Keep a one-page service menu and pricing sheet ready, plus an account setup form and an authorization form employees can bring. Train front-desk staff to recognize employer patients, follow each account's protocols and send results to the right contact. Confirm which examiners are certified for DOT physicals and keep that information current.

## Services that win specific employers

**DOT physicals and testing programs.** Carriers need certified examiners and organized testing processes. Clear scheduling and fast paperwork make you valuable.

**Pre-placement exams for staffing agencies.** High volume, quick turnaround and walk-in capacity matter most. Consider dedicated morning slots.

**Injury care and return-to-work.** Employers want clear work status reports, follow-up scheduling and communication with their safety lead.

**Health screenings for healthcare and education.** TB testing, titers and vaccinations, scheduled in batches for new staff.

**On-site events.** Flu shot clinics or screening days at employer sites build goodwill and introduce your clinic to employees.

## A worked example

Here's how an account might start. The details are illustrative.

You email the HR manager at a regional distribution center ten minutes from your clinic. She replies to your follow-up about same-day results; their current clinic often takes two days, and new hires lose interest while waiting. You send a service menu and set up an account in a day.

During their next hiring wave, twenty new hires come in over two weeks. Results go to HR the same afternoon for most tests. When a worker is injured on a Saturday shift, the supervisor uses your checklist and sends him to your clinic, and HR gets a clear work status report on Monday morning.

A month later, the HR manager introduces you to the safety lead at the company's second site across town, and asks about a flu shot clinic for both locations in the autumn.

## Keeping accounts healthy in year two

Review each account every few months: volume, turnaround times, any complaints and upcoming hiring. Ask whether services have changed, such as new safety-sensitive roles or new sites. Send a short annual summary of services provided, in aggregate and without individual details. Employers appreciate a clinic that thinks ahead about their needs, and these reviews often lead to new services or new locations.

## Making employer onboarding painless

Employers want to start using your clinic without paperwork headaches. Create a simple account setup process: a short form for company details, billing information, authorized contacts and the services they need. Provide authorization forms employees bring to appointments, and explain how results will be delivered. Offer a quick call to walk HR through the process and answer questions.

Train front-desk staff on employer accounts, including how to verify authorizations, which services each employer has approved and who receives results. Mistakes at the front desk, such as wrong tests or results sent to the wrong person, damage trust quickly. A smooth first visit is the best way to keep the account.

## Reporting that employers value

Employers need clear, timely information without unnecessary medical detail. Provide standardized work-status reports after injuries, with restrictions and follow-up dates. Send pre-employment results promptly with clearance status. For accounts with regular volume, consider a periodic summary of services provided, in aggregate. Keep billing consistent and easy to reconcile. Employers who receive clear reports and accurate invoices rarely look for another clinic.

## Building relationships with safety managers

Safety managers care about injury prevention and return-to-work programs. Offer to discuss their injury trends (in aggregate), recommend prevention measures and explain how your clinic supports return-to-work goals. Invite them to tour the clinic. Safety managers who see you as a partner in reducing injuries and lost time become strong advocates within their companies, and they often move between employers, bringing you new accounts.

## Partnering with workers' compensation stakeholders

Workplace injuries involve employers, insurers and sometimes case managers. Clinics that communicate clearly with all parties, within privacy rules, make the process smoother. Provide timely reports, follow up on restrictions and coordinate return-to-work planning. Insurers and case managers notice clinics that handle injuries efficiently, and they may recommend you to employers they work with.

## Seasonal hiring support

Many employers hire in waves: retailers before holidays, warehouses during peak shipping and landscaping companies in spring. Contact employers before their peak seasons, offer extended hours or dedicated slots for pre-employment exams and confirm turnaround times. Being ready for seasonal surges makes you invaluable when employers need you most.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do occupational health clinics get employer clients?

Target nearby employers that hire often or have workplace injury risk: logistics, construction, warehouses and staffing agencies. Send a clear service menu, set up accounts quickly and deliver fast results.

### Who decides which occupational health clinic an employer uses?

Usually HR, a safety manager or the owner in smaller businesses.

### What should an occupational health service menu include?

Services offered, turnaround times, hours and walk-in policy, how results are delivered, pricing and a contact person.

### What information can employers receive?

Generally only what's needed for employment decisions, such as clearance or work status. Detailed medical information stays protected; confirm your obligations with your compliance advisor.

### Can urgent care centers offer occupational health services?

Many do. It can fill quieter weekday hours, but it requires clear employer processes, certified examiners for some services and reliable result delivery.

### How do occupational health clinics price services?

Usually per service, with a menu for employers. Some offer account-based pricing for high-volume clients. Keep pricing clear and billing consistent.

### What makes employers switch occupational health providers?

Slow results, long waits for injured workers, unclear reports and billing errors are common reasons.

### Should clinics offer on-site services at employer locations?

Flu shot clinics and screening days can strengthen relationships and introduce employees to your clinic.

### What is a work-status report?

A report after an injury or evaluation that states whether the employee can work, any restrictions and follow-up plans.

### How do occupational health clinics handle drug testing?

By following applicable regulations and employer policies, with proper collection procedures and confidential result handling.

### Can occupational health clinics help with return-to-work programs?

Yes. Clear restrictions, follow-up visits and communication with employers support safe return to work.

### What should be on an employer authorization form?

Employer details, the employee's name, authorized services and the contact for results and billing.

### How do clinics keep employer accounts long term?

Fast results, accurate billing, clear reports and responsive communication.

### Should occupational health clinics offer on-site services?

On-site screenings or flu shot clinics can strengthen relationships and introduce employees to your services.

### How do occupational health clinics work with insurers?

By providing timely, accurate reports and following up on care and restrictions, within privacy rules.

### Can clinics handle seasonal hiring surges?

Yes, with planning: extended hours, dedicated slots and clear turnaround commitments.

### What do case managers want from clinics?

Clear reports, timely follow-up and coordinated return-to-work plans.

### What is a fitness-for-duty evaluation?

An assessment of whether an employee can safely perform their job duties, often after an injury or illness, with results reported to the employer as work status.

### How should clinics price employer services?

Many publish a clear menu of per-service prices, with volume pricing for large accounts, and invoice employers consistently each month.

### How do clinics stay ready for injured workers?

With triage procedures for employer patients, staff trained on account protocols and clear communication with the employer's contact.

### How do clinics introduce themselves to safety managers?

With a short note, a service menu and an offer to tour the clinic or discuss injury prevention.
