A one-star review lands on your Google profile at 9 p.m. It's unfair, half of it is wrong, and you know exactly what happened in that appointment, consultation or job. Your first instinct is to reply with the facts. For a dentist, doctor or lawyer, that instinct can turn a bad review into a regulatory problem.

Knowing how to respond to negative reviews is different for professionals. A restaurant owner can say "we checked the receipt and you ordered the fish." A dentist who writes "we reviewed your chart" has just confirmed someone is a patient, which has led to federal settlements with practices. A lawyer who corrects a former client's account may be revealing confidential information.

This guide gives you a process that works across professions, the rules that apply to each, copy-ready templates by scenario, and a clear path for fake reviews. It isn't legal advice, so check with your licensing board, bar or counsel for your situation.

## Why your reply matters more than the review

Most people who read a negative review also read your reply. They aren't judging whether the reviewer was right. They're judging how you behave when something goes wrong, because that's what they'd experience if something went wrong for them.

A calm, short, professional reply tells prospective clients three things: you read feedback, you take it seriously, and you'd handle their problem like an adult. An angry or defensive reply tells them the opposite, even if every fact in it is correct.

::compare Two replies to the same complaint | Defensive: argues the timeline; reveals details of the visit or case; blames the client; long and emotional | Professional: thanks them for the feedback; shares no private details; states your standard; offers a direct contact to resolve it

The reply is also permanent and public. Write it for the hundred future clients who'll read it, not for the one person who wrote the review.

## The rules that change how professionals respond

Every business has to follow Google's policies and the FTC's rules. Healthcare and legal professionals have extra confidentiality duties on top. Here's how they stack up.

### HIPAA: dentists, doctors, therapists and clinics

If you're a HIPAA-covered healthcare provider, a review reply that confirms someone is your patient, or mentions their treatment, can be an impermissible disclosure of protected health information. It doesn't matter that the patient posted first. Their decision to share their own information doesn't give you permission to share it.

This isn't theoretical. The HHS Office for Civil Rights has settled with dental practices over review replies.

::stats HHS OCR settlements over review replies | $10,000 | Elite Dental Associates, Dallas (2019) | $23,000 | New Vision Dental, California (2022)

According to HHS, [Elite Dental Associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/elite/index.html) disclosed patients' information while responding to Yelp reviews. New Vision Dental settled in December 2022 for disclosing PHI in Yelp review replies. The lesson for every healthcare practice: **never confirm someone is a patient, and never mention treatment, in a review reply.** Our guide to [dental reviews and HIPAA](/blog/dental-office-google-reviews-hipaa) goes deeper with dental-specific examples.

### Bar rules: lawyers

Lawyers have a duty of confidentiality that covers far more than privileged communications. The ABA's [Formal Opinion 496](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/aba-formal-opinion-496.pdf), issued in January 2021, addresses responding to online criticism. It concludes that a negative online review alone doesn't let a lawyer reveal confidential client information in response, and it suggests responses such as stating that professional considerations prevent a detailed reply.

States adopt their own versions of the rules and some have their own ethics opinions, so check your state bar's guidance. Our guide to [how lawyers get Google reviews ethically](/blog/lawyer-reviews-ethics-google) covers asking for reviews and responding within the rules.

### Contractors and other service businesses

Plumbers, electricians, roofers and HVAC companies don't have HIPAA or bar confidentiality duties. You have more room to discuss the job. Still, avoid posting a customer's address, payment history, or anything else that would embarrass them or make you look vindictive. Stick to your process and what you'll do to make it right.

### The FTC review rule and Google's policies apply to everyone

The FTC's [Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) (16 CFR Part 465) took effect October 21, 2024. Among other things, it bans review suppression, meaning using threats or intimidation to stop or remove negative reviews, and it bans fake and purchased reviews. Separately, the [Consumer Review Fairness Act](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-review-fairness-act-what-businesses-need-know) makes form-contract clauses that bar customers from posting honest reviews generally void.

In practice, that means:

- Don't threaten legal action in a reply.
- Don't make a refund conditional on deleting or changing the review. Offer to fix the problem, and let the customer decide what to do with the review.
- Don't bury negative reviews under purchased positive ones.
- Don't write fake reviews about competitors.

## A five-step process for every negative review

The process is the same whatever your profession. The difference is what you leave out.

::flow Handling a negative review | Pause for a few hours | Check if it's real or fake | Investigate privately | Post a short, safe reply | Follow up offline

### 1. Pause before replying

Don't reply angry. Wait a few hours, or overnight. Let someone else on your team read the review and your draft. Google lets you edit replies later, but people may see the first version, and screenshots last forever.

### 2. Decide whether it's real

Look for a real person with a real experience, even a bad one. Fake reviews have telltale signs: no record of the person, details that don't match your business, a burst of similar reviews at once, or a reviewer who clearly meant a different business. Fake reviews get a different path, covered below.

### 3. Investigate privately

Find out what happened. Talk to the staff involved. Check your records, privately. Healthcare and legal professionals can do this inside their own systems, because the problem is disclosing information publicly, not looking at it internally.

### 4. Post a short, safe reply

Keep it to three or four sentences. Thank them, acknowledge the concern in general terms, state your standard, and give a direct way to reach a named person. Don't argue facts in public.

### 5. Follow up offline

If you can identify the person and contact them through your normal channels, do it. Many complaints come from miscommunication that a phone call fixes. If you resolve it, don't ask them to delete the review. They may update it on their own.

::check Before you post any review reply | No confirmation they're a client or patient (healthcare and legal) | No treatment, case, job address or billing details | No blame, sarcasm or legal threats | No offer tied to removing the review | A named contact and direct phone or email | Read by a second person

## Templates by scenario

Copy these, then adjust the wording so they sound like you. Avoid posting the same reply word for word on every review, since readers notice.

### Healthcare: complaint about wait time or front desk

"Thank you for your feedback. We work hard to respect everyone's time and to treat every person who calls or visits with courtesy. We'd like to understand what happened. Please call our office manager, Maria, at (555) 555-0123."

Notice what's missing: no "we're sorry your appointment ran late," no "your visit," no "our records show." Each would confirm a patient relationship.

### Healthcare: complaint about treatment or outcome

"We take all feedback seriously. Privacy laws prevent us from discussing anyone's care online, but we'd welcome the chance to talk directly. Please contact our practice administrator at (555) 555-0123."

### Healthcare: complaint about billing or insurance

"Thank you for reaching out. Billing questions can be confusing, and we want to help. Our billing coordinator can be reached at (555) 555-0123 or billing@example.com, and we'll be glad to look into this privately."

### Law firm: unhappy former client

"We're committed to treating every person who contacts our firm with respect. Our professional obligations prevent us from responding in detail online. If you'd like to discuss your concerns, please contact our managing partner at (555) 555-0123."

### Law firm: review from the opposing party

People on the other side of a case sometimes review the opposing lawyer. Don't confirm the case, the client or the outcome.

"We take feedback seriously. Our professional obligations prevent us from discussing any matter publicly. We remain committed to representing our clients ethically and professionally."

You can also report the review to Google if it doesn't describe a genuine customer experience, since Google's policies cover reviews that aren't based on a real experience with the business.

### Contractor: job ran late or cost more than expected

"Thanks for the feedback, and I'm sorry the project didn't go the way you expected. Schedules and change orders should always be clear up front, and we'd like to make this right. Please call me directly at (555) 555-0123. Dave, owner."

Contractors can be a little more specific, like mentioning "the project," but still skip the address, the price and the back-and-forth.

### Contractor: workmanship complaint

"We stand behind our work and want to fix this. Please call our service manager at (555) 555-0123 so we can schedule a time to come back out and take a look."

::chat Following up offline with an unhappy client | you: Hi, this is Dave from Ridgeline Roofing. I saw your review and wanted to call directly. | them: Your crew left nails all over my driveway. | you: That's not acceptable, and I'm sorry. Can I send someone tomorrow morning with a magnetic sweeper and check the gutters too? | them: Tomorrow works. | you: Done. I'll text you the crew lead's name tonight.

## Fake reviews and when to flag them

Some negative reviews aren't from real customers. They may be from competitors, ex-employees, or people who meant a different business. Google's [fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) prohibits content that doesn't reflect a genuine experience.

### How to report one

1. In your Google Business Profile, find the review and choose the option to report it.
2. Pick the policy reason that fits, such as fake engagement, conflict of interest or off-topic.
3. Keep a record: screenshots, dates and why you believe it's fake.
4. If Google doesn't remove it, you can follow up through Business Profile support.

Removal isn't guaranteed. Google decides based on its policies, not on whether the review is fair.

### Should you reply to a fake review?

Usually yes, briefly and calmly, because readers see it while Google decides. For healthcare and legal professionals, don't say "this person was never our patient." That's still a statement about who is or isn't a patient. Use something neutral:

"We take all feedback seriously, but we're unable to match this review to our records. If you'd like to discuss it, please call our office at (555) 555-0123."

> Never respond to a suspected fake review by revealing who you think wrote it, or by posting fake positive reviews to bury it. Both create bigger problems than the review itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you respond to a negative review professionally?

Wait a few hours, then post a short reply that thanks the reviewer, acknowledges the concern in general terms, and gives a direct contact to resolve it. Don't argue facts publicly or share private details.

### Can a dentist or doctor respond to a negative review?

Yes, but without confirming the person is a patient or mentioning any treatment. HHS has settled with dental practices, including Elite Dental Associates for $10,000 in 2019, over review replies that disclosed patient information.

### What does ABA Formal Opinion 496 say about lawyers responding to reviews?

It says a negative online review alone doesn't allow a lawyer to reveal confidential client information in a reply. A lawyer can respond without confidential details, for example by saying professional considerations prevent a detailed response. Check your state bar's rules too.

### Can I offer a refund if the customer removes their review?

Don't make a refund or discount conditional on removing or changing a review. Offer to fix the problem on its own merits. Pressuring customers to remove negative reviews can raise problems under the FTC's review rule.

### How do I get a fake Google review removed?

Report it from your Google Business Profile and choose the policy reason that fits, such as fake engagement or conflict of interest. Keep screenshots and notes. Google decides based on its policies, and removal isn't guaranteed.

### Should I reply to every negative review?

Generally yes. A calm reply shows future clients how you handle problems. Keep it brief and private-detail-free, and move the real conversation offline.
