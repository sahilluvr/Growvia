Most real estate leads aren't lost to a better agent. They're lost to silence. Someone fills in a form on a Sunday night, gets a generic auto-reply, hears nothing useful for two days, and by Wednesday has either talked to someone else or decided they're "just looking."

The fix isn't more leads. It's a follow-up system that does two things well: replies fast and like a human, then stays useful for months while the person gets ready. This guide gives you that system, with first-message scripts, a seven-day sprint, a 12-month email cadence, how to handle the written buyer agreement conversation after the NAR settlement, and the handful of numbers that tell you it's working.

## Why real estate lead follow up breaks down

Agents rarely ignore leads on purpose. Follow-up breaks for boring, fixable reasons:

- **Leads live in too many places.** Portal leads in one app, website forms in your inbox, Instagram DMs on your phone, open house sign-ins on paper.
- **There's no next step.** After the first call, nobody decided what happens on day 3, day 10 or month 4.
- **The messages are about you.** "Just checking in!" gives the lead nothing. They stop opening.
- **Timelines are long.** Many online leads are months from moving. Agents give up after a few weeks, right before the person gets serious.
- **Nobody measures it.** Without response times and conversion by source, you can't tell what's broken.

A system fixes all five. One place for leads, a written plan for each stage, messages that help, a long enough runway, and a short weekly review.

## Step 1: Put every lead in one pipeline

Before you write a single script, decide where leads live. Every source should end up in the same pipeline with the same fields.

::flow Where every lead should end up | Portal, website, social and open house leads | One pipeline with source tagged | Instant auto-reply plus a task for you | Nurture track by buyer, seller and timeline

At minimum, capture for each lead:

- Name, email and phone
- Source (Zillow, website, open house, Instagram, referral, sign call)
- Buyer, seller or both
- Rough timeline (0–3 months, 3–6, 6–12, 12+)
- Area and price range if known
- Next action and date

If you're on a team, add an owner field. Unowned leads are the ones nobody calls.

## Step 2: Reply fast, and sound like a person

When a lead raises their hand, their interest is highest in the first minutes. That's not a statistic, just common sense: they're on their phone, looking at houses, right now. Reply while they're still there.

Set two things up today:

1. **An instant auto-reply** on every form and portal that sounds like you, says when you'll call, and asks one easy question.
2. **A task for you** to call or text personally within minutes during business hours, and first thing the next morning for after-hours leads.

A good instant auto-reply for a buyer lead:

"Hi Maria, thanks for asking about 412 Oak Street. I'm Jen, a local agent with Lakeside Realty. I'll call you within the hour (or by 9 a.m. tomorrow if it's after 8 p.m.). Quick question so I can be useful: are you mainly looking in this neighborhood, or open to nearby areas too?"

It names the property, sets an expectation and invites a one-line reply. Compare that with "Thank you for your inquiry. An agent will contact you shortly."

Here's how the first conversation might go once the lead replies. This is an illustrative script, not a transcript.

::chat First conversation with a portal buyer lead (illustrative) | them: Saw the house on Oak St. Is it still available? | you: It is. It has a showing request for Saturday already. Are you hoping to see it soon, or still getting a feel for the area? | them: Just starting. Lease is up in March. | you: That's good timing. I'll send you the 4 homes that sold near Oak St recently, so you know what things really go for. Want to talk for 10 minutes this week about what you need? | them: Sure, Thursday works.

Notice what the agent did. Answered the question, asked about timeline, gave something useful, and asked for a small next step. No pressure, no "are you pre-approved?" in the first line.

## Step 3: The first seven days

If they don't reply, you haven't failed. Most people don't answer the first message. A short, structured sprint over the first week catches the people who were busy, not uninterested.

::steps Seven-day follow-up sprint for a new lead | Day 0 — instant auto-reply, then a personal call or text within minutes | Day 1 — second call attempt plus a short email with one useful link | Day 2 — text or DM a specific question about their search | Day 4 — email recent sales near the home they asked about | Day 7 — final sprint message, then move to the long nurture

A few rules for the sprint:

- **Vary the channel.** Call, then email, then text or DM. Some people never answer unknown numbers.
- **Every message gives something.** Recent sales, a new listing that fits, an answer to a question they asked.
- **Don't guilt-trip.** "I haven't heard back from you" makes people feel bad and stop opening.
- **Respect texting rules.** Personal replies to someone who contacted you are normal. Automated marketing texts need prior express written consent under the TCPA, quiet hours of 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the recipient's time zone, and 10DLC registration for business texting from local numbers. Our [SMS marketing, TCPA and 10DLC guide](/blog/sms-marketing-tcpa-10dlc-guide) covers the details. Growvia doesn't send texts, so use your CRM or phone for this part.

The day 7 message can be simple: "I'll stop filling your inbox. I'll send a short local market note once a month, plus anything that matches what you're looking for. Reply any time and I'll pick up where we left off."

## Step 4: The 12-month nurture

After the sprint, a lead who hasn't engaged moves to a long nurture. This is where most agents give up too early. Someone whose lease ends in nine months, or who is waiting for a child to finish the school year, is a real client. They just aren't ready yet.

A long nurture mixes automated emails with occasional personal touches. Here's a cadence you can adapt.

| When | Buyer nurture | Seller nurture | Personal touch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 2 | "How buying works here" in 5 steps | "What your home might sell for, and why" | None |
| Week 4 | Recent sales in their target area | Recent sales on or near their street | Short call to check timeline |
| Month 2 | What a written buyer agreement covers | What sellers typically fix before listing | None |
| Month 3 | Mortgage pre-approval explained simply | Pricing strategy and days on market | Text or email with one question |
| Month 4 | Neighborhood guide for their top area | Staging on a budget | None |
| Month 6 | Mid-year market update | Mid-year market update | Call: "Has anything changed?" |
| Month 8 | Inspection and appraisal basics | Prep timeline: 60 days before listing | None |
| Month 10 | New construction vs resale locally | Buying and selling at the same time | Handwritten note or call |
| Month 12 | Annual market recap | Annual market recap | Call to re-check plans |

Between scheduled emails, send anything genuinely relevant: a new listing that fits, a price drop on a home they viewed, a local rule change.

### Writing nurture emails people open

- **Short subject lines that describe the content.** "4 homes sold near Oak St in August" beats "Market Update!!"
- **Plain text, from your own address.** It reads like a note from a person, and replies come straight to you.
- **One idea per email.** Then one soft question ("Want me to set up a search for this area?").
- **Your own read on the numbers.** Anyone can paste a chart. Your opinion is the reason to read.

AI is useful here. Give it your notes and local stats, ask for a 150-word draft in your voice, then edit. Do not let AI invent market figures. Every number in a nurture email should come from your MLS or a public source.

Keep the legal basics in place: honest From and subject lines, your physical mailing address, and an unsubscribe link honored within 10 business days, as the [FTC's CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) explains. And set up your domain properly so your emails land in the inbox; our [email deliverability guide](/blog/email-deliverability-spf-dkim-dmarc-guide) walks through SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

> Key takeaway: a 12-month nurture isn't about volume. One useful email every few weeks, plus a real call every quarter, keeps you in mind until the lead is ready.

## Step 5: The buyer agreement conversation after the NAR settlement

Since **August 17, 2024**, MLS participants working with buyers need a written agreement in place before touring a home with that buyer, and buyer-broker compensation can no longer be offered through the MLS. NAR summarizes the changes on its [facts about the settlement page](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts). Your state, your brokerage and your MLS may have added rules, so check those. This is not legal advice.

For follow-up, the practical shift is timing. The agreement should come up early and calmly, not at the front door of the first showing when the buyer feels cornered.

Here's a way to bring it up on the first real call:

"Before we tour anything, there's one piece of paperwork every buyer signs now: a written agreement that says what I'll do for you and how I'm paid. It's a national change from 2024, not just me. I'll send it today so you can read it before we meet, and I'm happy to walk through any part of it. Some people like a short agreement covering one home to start. Does that sound reasonable?"

Then follow with a short email that explains, in plain English:

- What you'll do for them (search, showings, negotiation, inspection and closing support)
- How compensation works, and that it's negotiable
- How long the agreement lasts and how to end it
- Who to ask if they have questions (you, and their own attorney if they want one)

::check Before you send a buyer agreement | Explain it on a call first, not only by email | Use your brokerage's current approved form | State compensation clearly and say it's negotiable | Offer a shorter or single-property option if your brokerage allows it | Invite questions and suggest an attorney if they want legal advice | Save the signed copy in the lead's record

Done well, this conversation builds trust. Buyers who understand what they're signing are more committed, not less.

## Seller leads need a different rhythm

Seller leads move on a different clock. A homeowner who asks "what's my home worth?" may be curious, may be planning a move in two years, or may need to sell in 60 days.

Ask about timeline early, then match the follow-up:

- **0–3 months:** in-person listing appointment as soon as possible, with a real comparative market analysis.
- **3–12 months:** a pricing conversation now, a prep checklist, and quarterly sales updates for their street.
- **12+ months:** home value updates twice a year and your monthly newsletter.

Seller leads often decide based on who seems to know their neighborhood best. That's where content helps. ## Step 6: Track what matters

You need a short weekly review, not a complicated dashboard. Five numbers tell you almost everything.

| Metric | What it shows | Target to aim for |
|---|---|---|
| Median first response time | Whether leads hear from you while interested | Minutes in business hours |
| Lead-to-conversation rate | Whether your first messages work | Rising month over month |
| Conversations to signed agreements | Whether you explain your value well | Track by source |
| Leads still in nurture at month 6 | Whether people stay subscribed | Low unsubscribe rate |
| Closings by original source | Where your business really comes from | Review quarterly |

Tag every lead with its original source and keep that tag when they close, even if they closed 11 months later. Without it, you'll credit the last email instead of the source that produced the lead.

Here's an illustration of why that matters. These are made-up numbers for one agent over a year, not benchmarks.

In this example, portal leads might produce the most volume and the most work, while the sphere produces the most closings. You can't see that without source tagging.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking for pre-approval in the first message.** It feels like a gate. Ask after you've helped.
- **"Just checking in" emails.** Give a reason every time.
- **Stopping after two weeks.** Most leads aren't ready for months.
- **Letting AI write unchecked.** It can invent numbers and slip in phrases that cause Fair Housing problems, like "perfect for young families." Describe homes, not people.
- **No owner on team leads.** Every lead needs one person responsible.
- **Ignoring past clients.** The best follow-up system also keeps your past clients on a monthly newsletter and a yearly home anniversary note.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast should I follow up with a real estate lead?

As fast as you can, ideally within minutes during business hours. Use an instant auto-reply that sounds like you for after-hours leads and call first thing the next morning.

### How long should I keep following up with a lead?

At least 12 months for most online leads. Run a seven-day sprint first, then move non-responsive leads to a monthly nurture with a personal check-in every few months.

### When should I bring up the buyer agreement?

On your first real call, before any tour. Since August 17, 2024, MLS participants need a written agreement before touring with a buyer, so explain it early and send it for review ahead of the first showing.

### Can I send automated texts to real estate leads?

Automated marketing texts require prior express written consent under the TCPA, must respect quiet hours, and need 10DLC registration for local business numbers. Personal one-to-one replies to someone who contacted you are a different situation, but check current rules.

### What should a real estate nurture email include?

One useful idea, such as recent sales in their area or how a step in the process works, your own read on it, and one soft question. Keep it short, plain text and sent from your own address.
