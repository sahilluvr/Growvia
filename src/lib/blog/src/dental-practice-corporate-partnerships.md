Most dental marketing waits for patients to search. That works, but it puts you in a race with every practice in the Map Pack. Partnerships take a different route: they put your practice in front of groups of people who already trust the organization introducing you.

A local employer with 120 staff, a private school with 400 families, a gym with 900 members: each is a doorway to new patients who'd otherwise never compare you to anyone. This guide shows how to find those partners and what to offer them. For search-driven growth, see our [dental SEO guide](/blog/dental-seo-guide).


## Why partnerships are a smart growth channel for dental practices

Most dental marketing competes in the same places: the Map Pack, paid search, social ads and insurance directories. Every practice in town fishes in those waters, costs rise and patients compare offices on price and convenience. Partnerships open a channel competitors rarely use well.

When a local employer, school or gym introduces your practice, the introduction carries their credibility. Employees hear about you from their HR team, parents from the school, members from the gym they visit every week. That trust shortens the decision. Many people put off finding a dentist for months simply because choosing feels like a chore; a trusted recommendation removes the chore.

Partnerships also bring patients in groups and from specific places. An office of a hundred people two blocks away can become a steady source of patients who book lunchtime cleanings. A school can bring whole families. A gym brings health-conscious adults who value prevention. Because these patients come from nearby, they're more likely to stay for years and keep their recall appointments.

Finally, partnerships build community presence. Talks, health fairs and school visits make your practice a familiar name locally, which supports every other channel, from reviews to word of mouth.

## What to offer

Keep it helpful and simple. Good options:

- **A short "lunch and learn"** at the office on topics like sensitivity, whitening myths or teeth grinding.
- **Priority scheduling** for staff, such as early morning or lunchtime slots.
- **A staff information sheet** explaining what common insurance plans usually cover for preventive care, and how to check their own benefits.
- **Sports mouthguard nights** for gyms and school teams.

Before offering any discount, check your state dental board's advertising rules and your agreements with insurers; some arrangements are restricted. Talks, convenience and information are rarely a problem.

## The email to HR

> **Subject:** A free lunch talk for your team
>
> Hi Laura — we're a family dental practice five minutes from your office. We run short lunch talks for local teams on everyday topics like sensitivity and whitening, with time for questions. No sales pitch, just practical answers. Would your team be interested in one this quarter?

## Privacy and professionalism

Partnerships must never involve sharing patient information. Don't confirm to an employer which staff became patients; keep any reporting to aggregate, non-identifying numbers if you report anything at all. ## Mistakes to avoid

- **Hard selling at talks.** Teach; the booking follows.
- **Promising coverage.** Explain how to check benefits rather than guaranteeing what insurance pays.
- **Forgetting to follow up.** Offer a second talk in six to twelve months.

## Your first conversation with HR

1. How many employees work on site, and what hours do they keep?
2. Do you run wellness events or lunch-and-learns already?
3. Which dental plans do employees typically have?
4. What's the best way to share information with staff: email, intranet or posters?
5. Are there rules about vendors presenting on site?
6. Would early-morning or lunchtime appointments help your team?

Bring a one-page summary of the talk, your practice hours and location, and a sample handout. Keep everything educational; HR teams are cautious about anything that looks like a sales pitch.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We don't allow vendor presentations." | Company policy. | Offer a printed guide or an email they can forward instead. |
| "Our insurance has a network." | Concern about coverage. | Share which plans you accept and suggest staff check their own benefits. |
| "Staff are too busy." | Time is tight. | Offer a 15-minute talk or a virtual session. |
| "Is there a cost?" | Budget worries. | Confirm talks and handouts are free. |
| "We already have a wellness vendor." | Existing programs. | Offer to contribute a dental session to their existing program. |

## Preparing your practice before outreach

Partners will look you up, and so will their employees. Make sure your Google Business Profile has current hours, photos of the office and team, and replies to reviews. Your website should clearly show insurance accepted, new patient information and online booking or a simple request form. Create a short landing page for partners with a booking link employees can use, so you can see which partnerships drive appointments without collecting any information from the employer. Prepare a talk outline and handouts in advance so you can say yes to a date quickly.

## Partnership formats that work

**Lunch-and-learn talks.** Twenty minutes on a practical topic, followed by questions. Bring handouts and a booking link.

**Priority booking windows.** Reserve early-morning or lunchtime slots for partner employees, so they can visit without taking a half day off.

**Health fair tables.** Many employers and schools run health fairs. Offer oral health information, toothbrushes for kids and a chance to ask questions.

**School visits.** Talks for children on brushing and healthy snacks, with parent handouts that explain when children should first see a dentist.

**Sports mouthguard sessions.** For gyms, martial arts studios and school teams, explain why custom mouthguards matter and how fittings work.

## A worked example

Here's how an employer partnership might develop. The details are illustrative.

You email the HR manager at a software company two blocks from your practice, offering a free lunch talk. She doesn't reply until your second email about popular topics, then books a session on sensitivity and whitening for the following month. Twenty-five employees attend, ask lots of questions about whitening and grinding, and take your handout with a booking link.

Over the next few weeks, several employees book cleanings using the link, and a couple of them book their partners and children too. HR asks you back for a second talk six months later and mentions you to a nearby company in the same building. Because you never shared patient information with HR, and kept everything educational, both companies are comfortable continuing the relationship.

## Mistakes to avoid as partnerships grow

Don't overextend your schedule. If you promise priority booking, make sure those slots exist and are protected. Keep talks educational rather than promotional; the moment it feels like a sales pitch, HR stops inviting you. Track which partnerships bring patients using booking links or "how did you hear about us" questions, never by asking employers for names. And keep any offers within your state board's rules and insurer contracts.

## Making partner patients feel welcome

Patients who come through a partnership often have put off dental care for a while. Make booking easy with a partner booking link, offer convenient times and keep the first visit relaxed. Explain findings and treatment options clearly, with costs and insurance information in writing. Avoid pressure; many of these patients are testing whether they can trust a new dentist.

After the visit, send a friendly follow-up and recall reminders. Ask how they heard about you to track partnerships, but never report individual names back to employers. Patients who have a comfortable first experience become long-term patients and tell colleagues, which strengthens the partnership naturally.

## Designing educational talks people remember

Keep talks practical and short. Start with a common question, such as "why do my teeth feel sensitive?" or "is whitening safe?", explain the answer in plain language and offer simple tips. Use a few visuals, avoid jargon and leave time for questions. Bring handouts with key tips and your booking link. End without a sales pitch; the most effective close is simply "if you have questions later, we're nearby."

Rotate topics each time you return to a partner, so the talks stay fresh: sensitivity, gum health, grinding, children's teeth, dental anxiety and how to read benefits. Over time, employees start to see your practice as a helpful local resource.

## Working within professional rules

Dental marketing is regulated by state boards, and partnerships with employers can raise questions about discounts, inducements and insurance agreements. Keep partnerships educational and convenience-based, check any offers against board rules and insurer contracts, and protect patient privacy at every step. When in doubt, ask your board or professional association. Compliance protects both your practice and the trust partners place in you.

## Building a calendar of partner activities

Plan partnership activities across the year: talks in quieter months for employers, school visits around back-to-school season, sports mouthguard sessions before sports seasons and health fair appearances when partners schedule them. A simple calendar keeps your team prepared and spreads outreach evenly. It also helps you avoid overwhelming the schedule with too many events at once, which protects the patient experience in the office.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can a dental practice partner with local businesses?

Offer free educational lunch talks, priority scheduling for staff and simple information sheets about preventive care. Start with employers, schools and gyms close to your practice.

### Can dentists offer discounts to employer groups?

Sometimes, but rules vary by state and by insurer contract. Check your dental board's advertising rules and your insurance agreements before offering discounts.

### Who should a dentist contact at a company?

Usually the HR or office manager. In smaller businesses, the owner.

### Do partnerships raise privacy concerns?

They can if patient information is shared. Never tell a partner which of their people became patients, and keep any reporting anonymous.

### What topics work best for dental lunch-and-learns?

Practical topics people ask about: sensitivity, whitening options, grinding, gum health, x-rays and how to read dental benefits.

### How can a dental practice track partnership results?

Use partner-specific booking links or ask new patients how they heard about you. Never ask employers which staff became patients.

### Can dental practices exhibit at employer health fairs?

Yes, many do. Bring educational materials, answer questions and keep it helpful rather than promotional.

### How often should a dental practice run partner events?

Once or twice a year per partner is common. Keep a calendar so each partner hears from you regularly without feeling overwhelmed.

### How can dental practices make first visits less stressful?

Offer convenient times, explain everything clearly, provide costs in writing and avoid pressure.

### What topics are good for school dental talks?

Brushing techniques, healthy snacks, when children should first see a dentist and protecting teeth during sports.

### Should dental practices offer virtual talks?

Yes, virtual talks work well for remote and hybrid teams.

### How do I track which partnership brought a patient?

Use partner booking links or ask how they heard about you, without reporting individual details to partners.

### Can dental practices partner with gyms for mouthguards?

Yes. Mouthguard information sessions and fittings are a natural fit for gyms and sports teams.

### How often should dental practices return to the same partner?

Once or twice a year is common, with a new topic each time.

### When are schools most receptive to dental talks?

Often around the start of the school year or during health-focused events.

### How many partner events can a practice handle?

Start with one or two a month and adjust based on staff capacity and results.

### Can dental practices partner with coworking spaces?

Yes. Member talks, convenient booking and simple information sheets work well for freelancers and small teams.

### How do dental practices measure partnership success?

By tracking bookings through partner links or new patient questions, without collecting information from partners.

### How do dental practices follow up after a lunch talk?

Send HR a thank-you note, share the handout digitally for staff who missed it and suggest a date for the next session.
