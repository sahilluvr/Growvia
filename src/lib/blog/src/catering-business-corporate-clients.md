Weddings and parties are memorable, but corporate catering is what keeps a catering business steady. Offices order lunch for meetings, onboarding days and team celebrations. Event planners need caterers for conferences. Coworking spaces host member events every month. One office manager who loves your food can order dozens of times a year.

This guide shows how to find those buyers, make ordering easy and turn one-time orders into standing accounts. See also our [restaurant marketing guide](/blog/ai-marketing-for-restaurants).


## Why corporate catering keeps a catering business steady

Event catering is exciting but unpredictable. Weddings cluster in certain months, parties cancel or shrink, and each event requires a new sale. Corporate catering offers the opposite: regular orders from offices that hold meetings, onboard staff, host clients and celebrate milestones all year round.

An office manager who trusts you may order breakfast for a weekly team meeting, lunch for monthly training sessions and a larger spread for quarterly reviews. Multiply that by a handful of offices and you have a predictable base of orders that keeps your kitchen busy on weekdays, when event work is quiet.

Corporate clients are also loyal when service is reliable. Office managers have enough to worry about; once they find a caterer who delivers on time with clear labels and easy invoices, they rarely look elsewhere. They also talk to other office managers in their building or industry, which makes referrals common.

Event planners and venues add a second stream. Planners need caterers they can trust for conferences and corporate events, and venues without kitchens need preferred caterers to recommend. Being on those lists brings larger, higher-value events.

## Your first conversation with an office manager

1. How often does your team order catering, and for what occasions?
2. How many people do you usually feed?
3. What dietary needs come up most often?
4. What's gone wrong with caterers before?
5. How do you prefer to order and pay?
6. Do you need setup, staff or cleanup?

Listen for pain points like late deliveries, missing labels or complicated invoices. Those become your promises.

## Preparing your business

Office managers will check you out. Make sure your website has a clear corporate catering page with menus, per-person prices, ordering steps, delivery area and lead times. Keep your licenses, food safety certifications and insurance current. Prepare a corporate menu PDF, a planning sheet and an invoice sample showing PO numbers. Make sure orders can be placed easily by email, phone or an online form.

## Corporate menu formats that work

**Breakfast packages.** Pastries, fruit, yogurt and coffee for morning meetings.

**Boxed lunches.** Individually packed meals with labels, ideal for training sessions and meetings.

**Buffet lunches.** Shared trays for team celebrations and larger groups.

**Client meeting platters.** Premium options for hosting visitors.

**Afternoon treats.** Snacks and desserts for milestones and celebrations.

Price each per person, list dietary options clearly and state the minimum headcount and lead time.

## A worked example

Here's how an office account might grow. The details are illustrative.

You email the office manager at a law firm downtown, offering a tasting box. She replies to your follow-up about dietary labels, since a recent lunch from another caterer caused confusion for staff with allergies. You deliver a tasting box, which the team enjoys.

She orders lunch for a training session the following week. You arrive in the agreed window, every dish is labeled and the invoice includes their PO number. Over the next month, she orders twice more and sets up a standing breakfast order for weekly partner meetings.

At a building management meeting, she mentions your service to office managers from two other firms in the building. Both order within a few weeks, and because they're in the same building, deliveries are efficient and profitable.

## Keeping corporate clients loyal

Remember preferences for each office: headcount, dietary needs and favorites. Send a short monthly menu update and seasonal specials. Offer standing orders for recurring meetings. Ask for feedback after larger orders and act on it. A simple loyalty perk, like a free dessert tray after a set number of orders, gives office managers something to share with their teams.

## Delivery logistics that protect your reputation

Corporate catering is judged in the first five minutes: did it arrive on time, was it set up neatly and was everything labeled? Plan deliveries backwards from the meal time, allowing for traffic, parking and building security. Many office buildings require deliveries through specific entrances or loading docks, and some need advance registration for drivers. Ask about this when you take the first order and save it in the client's notes.

Use insulated carriers to keep food at safe temperatures, and bring serving utensils, napkins, plates and labels. Set up quickly and quietly if meetings are in progress. Leave a card with your contact details and a simple feedback link. If a delivery is going to be late, call the office manager before the scheduled time. Clients forgive occasional delays when they're warned; they rarely forgive silence.

## Building a corporate menu that sells itself

Office managers want choices, but not too many. Offer a short menu of breakfast, boxed lunch and buffet options, each with clear per-person pricing and dietary symbols. Include at least one vegetarian, one vegan and one gluten-free option in every package. Rotate a seasonal special to keep regular clients interested. Keep minimum orders and lead times visible on the menu. Provide a simple order form or email template so office managers can order in two minutes.

Consider packages for common occasions: new hire welcomes, monthly all-hands meetings, client presentations and holiday parties. When office managers see the occasion listed, it's easier for them to say "let's use the caterer we always use".

## Working with event planners and venues

Planners and venues need caterers who can handle larger events, follow their timelines and work smoothly with other vendors. Prepare an event menu with tiered pricing, staffing options and equipment details. Share your insurance, food safety certifications and examples of past events. Attend venue walkthroughs when invited, and coordinate setup and breakdown times with the venue. Once you've delivered a few events smoothly, planners add you to their preferred vendor lists, which brings larger and more profitable work.

## Food safety and presentation

Corporate clients expect professional food safety. Keep hot food hot and cold food cold during transport, label items clearly and include serving times where appropriate. Train delivery staff on safe handling and setup. Make sure your licenses and food safety certifications are current and available on request. Presentation matters too: clean trays, neat labels and tidy setup make office managers look good in front of their teams and clients.

## Growing within a building or business park

Once you serve one office in a building, nearby offices become your easiest prospects. Ask your office manager contact whether they'd introduce you to neighbors, and leave menus at reception where allowed. Offer a building-wide special or a lunch-and-learn sponsorship for a building event. Deliveries to several offices in the same building are efficient and profitable, so focusing on clusters can grow revenue quickly.

## Handling last-minute orders and changes

Offices often change headcounts or meeting times at short notice. Decide in advance what you can accommodate: how late headcount changes are accepted, how much extra you can add and what happens with cancellations. Put these terms on your menu and confirm them on each order. When an office manager calls with an urgent request, a quick, clear answer, even if it's "we can do boxed sandwiches but not the hot buffet", builds trust. Being reliably flexible within clear limits is often what wins the account over larger caterers.

## Collecting and acting on feedback

After larger orders, send a two-question feedback request: what went well and what could be better. Act on patterns, such as portion sizes or variety, and tell clients what you changed. Office managers notice when feedback leads to improvements.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do caterers get corporate clients?

Target office managers, event planners, venues and coworking spaces. Offer a tasting, deliver perfectly on the first order and propose standing orders.

### What should a corporate catering menu include?

Per-person packages, clear dietary labels, delivery windows, set-up options and invoicing details.

### Do tasting boxes work for catering sales?

Yes. Letting decision-makers taste the food is one of the most effective ways to win an office account.

### How do I keep corporate catering clients?

Be on time, remember preferences, send monthly menu updates and offer standing orders.

### What lead time do caterers need for corporate orders?

It depends on your kitchen and order size. Many caterers ask for 24 to 48 hours for standard orders and more for large events. State it clearly.

### Should caterers offer standing orders?

Yes. Standing orders for recurring meetings give offices convenience and give you predictable revenue.

### How can caterers get on venue preferred lists?

Contact venues without kitchens, offer a tasting and show your insurance, licenses and event experience.

### What makes office managers switch caterers?

Late deliveries, missing dietary labels, mistakes in orders and difficult invoicing.

### How do caterers handle dietary restrictions for offices?

Label every dish clearly, offer options for common diets in every package and ask about specific allergies when taking the order.

### Do corporate clients expect staff at events?

For drop-off lunches, usually not. For larger events or client meetings, offer optional staff for setup, service and cleanup.

### How should caterers invoice corporate clients?

Clearly, with dates, headcount, items, PO numbers if required and payment terms. Many offices pay monthly.

### Can small caterers serve large corporate events?

Yes, with planning. Know your capacity, partner with other vendors if needed and be honest about what you can deliver.

### How do I get repeat orders from offices?

Deliver on time, remember preferences, follow up after each order and offer standing orders for recurring meetings.

### What should be in a catering tasting box?

A selection of your most popular items with labels and a menu, so decision-makers can taste quality and see your presentation.

### What food safety certifications do caterers need?

Requirements vary by location. Check local health department rules for food handling, licensing and certifications.

### How do caterers keep food at safe temperatures during delivery?

With insulated carriers, appropriate packaging and planned delivery times that minimize holding.

### Can caterers market to all offices in a building?

Yes, with permission. Leaving menus at reception or asking a client for introductions works well.

### What's the best day to approach office managers?

Mid-week is often quieter for office managers than Mondays or Fridays.

### How do caterers handle cancellations?

With a clear policy stated on the menu and order confirmation, such as notice periods and any charges.

### Can caterers accept same-day orders?

Some can for simpler items. State what's possible and the latest time to order.

### How do caterers price corporate packages?

Per person, with clear minimum headcounts and options listed separately for staff, setup and rentals.

### How do caterers find event planners to partner with?

Search Google Maps for event planners and venues in your area, review their past events and offer a tasting with an event menu that fits their style and budget.
