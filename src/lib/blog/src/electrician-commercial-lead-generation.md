Residential service calls keep an electrical business alive. Commercial and contractor work is what lets it grow: bigger tickets, planned schedules and customers who come back for every project. The hard part is getting in the door, because general contractors and facility managers already have someone they call.

The good news: "already have someone" rarely means "happy with them". Electricians who show up when they say, pass inspection first time and communicate clearly get added to bid lists quickly. This guide shows how to find those buyers and earn a first job. Our [electrician marketing guide](/blog/electrician-marketing-guide) covers residential search, reviews and service pages.


## Why contractor and commercial work changes an electrical business

Residential service calls are quick wins but they rarely build momentum on their own. Each one needs a new search, a new booking and a new customer. Contractor and commercial relationships behave differently: a remodeler who trusts you sends every kitchen and basement, a property manager sends every tenant fit-out, a coworking space calls every time it reconfigures a floor. Once you're on the list, work arrives without marketing spend.

Commercial and contractor work also tends to be scheduled in advance, which makes it easier to plan crews and order materials. Larger projects bring bigger tickets, and recurring relationships make your revenue more predictable, which matters when you're deciding whether to hire another electrician or buy another van.

There's a skill-building benefit too. Working with contractors exposes your team to more varied projects, more inspections and more coordination, which raises the quality of everything you do, including residential service.

## The first email

Contractors don't want a brochure. They want to know you'll protect their schedule.

> **Subject:** Electrician for your kitchen and basement jobs
>
> Hi Chris — I'm a licensed electrician based in Lakewood. Contractors use us for rough-in and finish work because we book inspections ourselves and haven't missed a scheduled day this year. If you ever need a second electrician for an overflow job, I'd be glad to bid on the next one.

"A second electrician for overflow" is a smart ask. You're not asking them to fire anyone, just to keep you on the list. Overflow jobs turn into first-choice jobs when you outperform the incumbent.

## Services that open commercial doors

Some services are easy entry points because businesses are already looking for them:

- **EV charger installation** for offices, apartment buildings and retailers.
- **LED lighting retrofits** that lower energy bills.

Mention one of these when it fits the business you're writing to. A coworking space hears "EV chargers for members"; a restaurant hears "a dedicated circuit for the new fryer".

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Underbidding to get in.** You'll be stuck at that price. Win on reliability instead.
- **Going quiet between projects.** A quick note when you have open capacity often lands an overflow job.
- **Not documenting.** Photos of rough-in before drywall protect you and impress the contractor.

## Your first conversation with a contractor

1. What types of projects are you running most right now?
2. How far ahead do you usually schedule electrical rough-in?
3. What causes delays with trades on your jobs?
4. Who handles permits and inspections today?
5. How do you prefer to get bids and updates: email, text or calls?

Bring your license, insurance certificate, a sample bid and photos of rough-in from past jobs. Contractors want to see clean, organized work behind the drywall because it predicts how you'll handle their projects.

## Preparing your company for commercial buyers

- Keep your license and insurance certificates current and ready to email.
- Build a simple capabilities sheet: services, service area, crew size, typical lead times and specialties.
- Add a commercial or contractor page to your website with photos of finished projects.
- Make sure your Google Business Profile lists commercial services and EV charger installation if you offer it.

Contractors and property managers often check these before replying. A clear, professional presence makes your outreach much more effective.

## Specialties that open commercial doors

General electrical work is competitive. A clear specialty gives buyers a reason to choose you, and gives your outreach a sharper hook.

**EV charging.** Offices, apartment buildings, dealerships and retailers are adding chargers. Buyers want help with site assessment, panel capacity, permits and utility coordination. A short guide to "what to check before installing chargers" makes a strong value-add email.

**Lighting retrofits.** Older fluorescent fixtures in offices, warehouses and retail stores are expensive to run and maintain. LED retrofits lower energy use and maintenance, and they're easy for owners to understand. Offer a free walkthrough with a fixture count and an estimate of what changes.

**Restaurant equipment circuits.** Restaurants add fryers, ovens and refrigeration over time, often beyond what the original panel was designed for. Panel upgrades and dedicated circuits scheduled outside service hours are valuable to owners who can't afford downtime.

**Tenant fit-outs.** Property managers and coworking operators reconfigure spaces between tenants. Electricians who turn these around quickly, coordinate with other trades and leave clean documentation become first call.

Pick one or two specialties that match your experience and mention them in outreach to the businesses most likely to need them.

## A worked example

Consider how a remodeler relationship might grow. The details are illustrative.

You email a kitchen and bath remodeler offering overflow help. A week later, their usual electrician is booked solid and a kitchen job is stalling. You bid, win and complete the rough-in on the agreed day. You book the inspection yourself, it passes, and you send the project manager photos of the work before drywall and a clear invoice that afternoon.

On the next project, the remodeler calls you first to "see if you're free". Within a few months, you're handling most of their kitchens, and they introduce you to a plumber and an HVAC company they work with. Each of those trades runs into electrical needs, so your network grows from one relationship into several.

## Pricing and bidding for contractor work

Contractors compare bids line by line, but they remember who caused delays. Your bid should make it easy to see what's included and what isn't. List the scope room by room or by system, state which fixtures and devices are supplied by you and which by the client, include permit and inspection costs, and note assumptions such as panel capacity or wall access. Give a clear timeline for rough-in and finish, and say how change orders are priced.

Avoid winning on price alone. A bid that's slightly higher but clearly scoped, delivered on time and backed by inspections booked for the contractor is often more attractive than a cheap bid with vague terms. Once you've done a few projects with a contractor, consider offering standard pricing for common scopes, such as a typical kitchen or basement, so they can quote homeowners faster. That convenience alone can make you their default electrician.

For property managers and commercial clients, offer a simple rate sheet for common service calls and a separate process for larger projects. Clear pricing reduces back-and-forth and builds the trust that leads to repeat work.

## Working smoothly with other trades

Electrical work sits in the middle of most projects, so how you coordinate matters as much as your wiring. Confirm schedules a few days ahead, communicate with the plumber and HVAC crew about shared spaces, protect finished surfaces and leave the site clean. Contractors notice when one trade makes everyone else's day easier, and they talk about it with other contractors.

## Safety and documentation on commercial sites

Commercial clients and contractors care about safety records. Make sure your team follows safety procedures, wears appropriate protective equipment and keeps sites tidy. Document work with photos, labeled panels and clear notes. Provide as-built information or panel schedules when you finish. Clients notice professionalism, and good documentation reduces disputes and callbacks.

## Responding to service calls for commercial accounts

Commercial service calls often come with urgency: a restaurant without power to a cooler, an office with no lighting in a conference room, a retail store with a tripped circuit. Offer commercial accounts a clear way to request service, a response time commitment and after-hours options if you can. Fast, reliable service on small calls builds trust that leads to larger projects.

## Building relationships with property managers

Property managers need electricians for repairs, tenant improvements and compliance work. Introduce yourself with a capabilities sheet, offer a small first job and provide clear, fast invoices. Managers who find a reliable electrician use them across many properties.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do electricians get commercial work?

Build relationships with general contractors, remodelers and property managers. Offer to take overflow jobs, deliver one project flawlessly and ask to be added to their regular list.

### How do I become a subcontractor for general contractors?

Email or call local contractors with a short introduction, your license details and a request to bid on the next overflow job. Then protect their schedule and inspections on that first job.

### Are EV chargers a good way to win commercial clients?

They can be. Offices, apartment buildings and retailers often want chargers, and a well-done installation leads to other electrical work at the same site.

### How many contractors should I contact?

Start with 30 to 50 nearby. A few strong relationships matter more than a long list.

### What should an electrician's capabilities sheet include?

Services, service area, license and insurance details, crew size, typical lead times, specialties such as EV chargers and a contact person.

### How do electricians win property management work?

Offer fast response, clear invoices and help with tenant changes. Start with a backup role and earn your way to first call.

### Is EV charger installation a good niche for electricians?

It can be, as offices, apartment buildings and retailers add chargers. A well-done installation often leads to other work at the same site.

### What documentation should electricians provide after commercial work?

Photos, updated panel schedules, notes on circuits and any as-built information.

### Do commercial clients expect after-hours service?

Many do, especially restaurants and retailers. Be clear about availability and pricing.

### How do electricians win property management work?

With a capabilities sheet, a small first job done well and fast, clear invoices.

### What safety records do contractors ask for?

Some ask about safety programs, training and insurance. Have the information ready.

### How do electricians price overflow work for contractors?

Using clear, scoped bids with the same terms as their regular work, so contractors can compare easily.

### What should an electrician's commercial rate sheet include?

Rates for common service calls, after-hours charges, trip fees and how larger projects are quoted.

### How do electricians get on a contractor's bid list?

Introduce yourself with a capabilities sheet, ask to bid on overflow work and deliver the first job flawlessly. Many contractors add trades to their regular list only after seeing how they handle a real project and inspection.
