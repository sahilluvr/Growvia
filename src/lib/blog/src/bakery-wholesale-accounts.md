A bakery's retail counter depends on foot traffic. Wholesale accounts don't. A café that takes two dozen croissants every morning, a restaurant that serves your bread with dinner, a grocer with your loaves on the shelf: each is a standing order that keeps ovens busy before the shop opens and smooths out slow retail days.

This guide covers who buys wholesale, how to price it and how to pitch so that the first sample becomes a daily order.


## Why wholesale accounts make a bakery more resilient

A bakery's retail sales rise and fall with weather, holidays, foot traffic and local events. A rainy week can leave unsold bread; a quiet season can leave ovens underused. Wholesale accounts smooth that out. A café that takes the same order every morning, a restaurant that serves your bread every evening and a grocer that restocks your loaves twice a week provide steady volume regardless of who walks past your shop.

Wholesale also improves production efficiency. Larger, predictable batches use labor and oven time better than many small runs. Ingredients can be ordered in bigger quantities, often at better prices. Staff can plan their mornings around confirmed orders rather than guessing demand.

Wholesale builds your brand too. When customers see your croissants in a popular café or your sourdough in a local grocer, they learn your name. Many will visit your shop or ask for your products elsewhere, creating a loop between wholesale and retail.

The challenge is margin and reliability. Wholesale prices are lower than retail, and buyers expect consistent quality and early delivery every day. Bakeries that price carefully and deliver reliably can make wholesale a profitable foundation.

## Keep accounts happy

Wholesale accounts leave for three reasons: late delivery, inconsistent quality and slow responses to changes. Keep a delivery log, check product consistency every batch and confirm order changes the same day. Ask happy accounts if they'd mention you to other café owners.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Pricing wholesale like retail.** Buyers need margin to resell.
- **Too many accounts too fast.** Quality and delivery suffer.
- **Missing allergen information.** Required by many buyers.

## Your first conversation with a café owner

1. What baked goods sell best for you, and at what times?
2. Where do you buy pastries and bread now?
3. What frustrates you about your current supplier?
4. How many items do you sell on a typical day, and on weekends?
5. When do you need deliveries, and who receives them?
6. Do you need allergen or ingredient information for customers?

Use the answers to suggest a starter order sized to their actual sales, so they don't waste product during the trial.

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |

| "Minimum order is too big." | Small business. | Offer a smaller starter order during the trial. |
| "What if it doesn't sell?" | Waste risk. | Suggest a small trial and adjust quantities weekly. |

## Preparing your bakery

Buyers will look at your products and reputation. Make sure your website or social profiles show your wholesale range with photos. Keep food safety licenses and insurance current, and prepare allergen and ingredient information for every product. Create a wholesale price sheet with minimums, order cut-off times and delivery days. Plan your delivery route so new accounts fit efficiently.

## Wholesale programs that work

**Café pastry program.** A rotating selection of pastries delivered daily, with a weekly order review.

**Restaurant bread service.** Daily bread and rolls matched to the restaurant's menu style.

**Grocery shelf program.** Packaged loaves and treats with clear labels and shelf-life guidance.

**Office breakfast boxes.** Weekly orders for meetings and team breakfasts.

**Seasonal specials.** Holiday breads and seasonal pastries offered to all accounts, adding variety and sales.

## A worked example

Here's how a wholesale account might grow. The details are illustrative.

You email an independent café near your bakery, offering a sample box. The owner replies to your follow-up about early delivery, because her current supplier often arrives after opening. You drop off croissants and morning buns, and her staff love them.

She starts a two-week trial with a small daily order. Deliveries arrive by 6:30am with a text confirmation. After the first week, you adjust quantities based on what sold out. She signs on as a regular account and adds your sourdough for her sandwiches.

Her customers start asking where the pastries come from, and she points them to your shop. Two other café owners she knows contact you, and because they're along the same route, adding them is efficient.

## Keeping wholesale accounts happy

Review orders with each account weekly at first, then monthly. Keep quality consistent from batch to batch. Confirm order changes the same day. Introduce seasonal specials to keep menus fresh. Ask for feedback and act on it. Accounts that feel looked after rarely switch suppliers.

## Costing wholesale products properly

Wholesale only works if every product earns a margin at the price you charge. For each item, add up ingredient cost per unit (including waste), packaging, the labor time to mix, shape, bake and pack, and a share of overheads such as energy, rent and delivery. That's your true cost. Wholesale prices must cover it with a margin you're comfortable with at volume, while leaving the buyer enough room to resell at a sensible retail price.

Some products will come out well; others won't. That's useful information. You may decide to offer only the products with healthy margins, adjust recipes or batch sizes, or set higher minimums on labor-intensive items. Review costs every few months, because ingredient prices change, and give accounts notice before any price change. Buyers accept increases far more readily when you explain them and give them time to adjust their menus.

## Planning production and delivery

Wholesale orders need to be in before your production plan for the next morning. Set a clear daily cut-off time and stick to it. Use a simple order sheet or form so changes are written down, not remembered. Plan a delivery route that starts with the earliest openers and groups nearby accounts. Keep a delivery log with times and any issues. If a delivery will be late, tell the account before they open, not after. These small systems prevent most of the problems that make wholesale buyers switch suppliers.

## Growing wholesale without hurting retail

Wholesale can strengthen your shop if you manage it carefully. Make sure production capacity covers both wholesale orders and your retail counter, especially on weekends. Keep a few signature items exclusive to your shop so customers still have a reason to visit. Ask cafés to mention your bakery on their menu or display, which turns their customers into your customers. Track how wholesale affects staffing and oven time, and pause new accounts if quality starts to slip.

## Packaging and labeling for wholesale

Wholesale buyers often need products ready to display or sell. Use packaging that protects products during delivery and fits the buyer's display, such as bakery trays for cafés or bags with labels for grocers. Labels should include product name, ingredients, allergens and any required dates. Ask each buyer about their requirements during onboarding and keep a note for each account. Consistent, professional packaging makes your products look premium and reduces complaints.

## Building your bakery's story

Wholesale buyers, especially independent cafés and grocers, love local stories. Share how long you've been baking, what makes your products special and where you source key ingredients. Provide a short description and photo they can display or put on their menu, such as "Croissants from Riverside Bakery, baked fresh this morning." This helps buyers sell more and turns their customers into your customers.

## When to say no to an account

Not every account is worth taking. Accounts outside your delivery route, buyers who want prices below your cost or businesses whose style doesn't fit your products can stretch your team and hurt your margins. It's fine to decline politely or suggest a pickup option. Saying no to the wrong accounts protects quality for the right ones.

## Communicating with accounts every week

A short weekly message keeps wholesale accounts running smoothly. Confirm next week's order, mention any specials or seasonal items and ask whether quantities need adjusting. Keep it to a few lines. Regular communication catches problems early, such as products selling out too fast or not fast enough, and gives buyers an easy way to grow their order. Accounts that hear from you regularly feel looked after and are much less likely to try another supplier.

## Handling quality issues quickly

If an account reports a problem, respond the same day, replace the product if appropriate and explain what you'll change. Buyers forgive occasional issues when they're handled well.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do bakeries get wholesale accounts?

Build a wholesale price sheet, target cafés and restaurants nearby, deliver sample boxes and offer a short trial with early delivery.

### How should a bakery price wholesale?

Calculate true cost per item, then set prices that leave the buyer room to resell while keeping your margin at volume. Use minimum orders and clear delivery terms.

### Which businesses buy wholesale baked goods?

Cafés, restaurants, delis, grocers, hotels and offices.

### What do wholesale buyers care about most?

On-time delivery, consistent quality and clear allergen information.

### How much should wholesale pastries cost?

Base prices on your true cost per item, leaving room for the buyer to resell and for your margin at volume. Many bakeries set minimum orders.

### Do bakeries need special licenses for wholesale?

Requirements vary by location. Check local food safety and licensing rules for selling to other businesses.

### How do I handle unsold products during a trial?

Start with small orders and adjust weekly based on sales, so the buyer isn't stuck with waste.

### How many wholesale accounts can a small bakery handle?

As many as your production and delivery can serve reliably. Grow along an efficient route.

### Should bakeries require contracts for wholesale accounts?

Many start with a simple written agreement covering prices, minimums, cut-off times, delivery and payment terms. It prevents misunderstandings later.

### How should wholesale accounts pay?

Common options are weekly or monthly invoices with agreed payment terms. Be clear from the start and follow up promptly on late payments.

### Can bakeries sell wholesale to grocery stores?

Yes, but grocers often need labels with ingredients, allergens and best-before dates, and may have specific packaging requirements.

### How do I handle seasonal demand changes?

Review orders with accounts before holidays and seasonal shifts, and offer seasonal products to keep sales steady.

### What's a good first product for wholesale?

Items that are popular, consistent and efficient to produce at scale, such as a signature pastry or a reliable loaf.

### How do I tell an account I can't take more orders?

Be honest and early. Offer a waiting list or a reduced selection rather than risking quality for everyone.

### What should be on a wholesale bakery label?

Typically product name, ingredients, allergens and any dates required by local rules or the buyer.

### Should bakeries offer free samples to wholesale prospects?

Yes, sample boxes are one of the most effective ways to win accounts.

### How do bakeries handle returns from wholesale accounts?

Agree on a policy upfront. Many bakeries adjust quantities rather than accepting returns of unsold products.

### Can bakeries deliver wholesale orders with their own staff?

Many do, especially for local routes. Plan routes efficiently and consider delivery costs in pricing.

### How often should bakeries communicate with wholesale accounts?

A short weekly check-in works well, with more contact during holidays and seasonal changes.

### What should bakeries do if an account reports a quality problem?

Respond the same day, replace products if appropriate and explain how you'll prevent it.

### How do bakeries set delivery minimums?

By calculating the delivery cost per stop and setting a minimum order that keeps each stop profitable.

### Can bakeries offer exclusive products to one account?

Some do for key accounts, such as a signature pastry made only for one café. Price exclusives carefully, because they take extra production time and planning.
