Your restaurant Google Maps ranking decides whether a hungry person nearby ever hears your name. When someone types "pizza near me" or "brunch downtown," Google shows a map with three places, and most people pick from those three. If you're fourth, you're invisible to a lot of them, no matter how good your food is.

Maps ranking isn't a mystery, and it isn't something you can buy. Google tells you roughly how it works. Most restaurants just leave half their profile empty, keep last year's hours and ignore reviews for months. Fixing that puts you ahead of a surprising number of competitors.

This guide walks through every part of your Google Business Profile that affects ranking and choice, in order of impact: categories, menu, photos, hours, attributes, reviews, posts, reservations and ordering links, and a weekly routine to keep it all fresh. ## How Google decides which restaurants to show

Google's own [guide to improving your local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) names three main factors.

- **Relevance:** how well your profile matches what someone searched. "Taco restaurant" matches "tacos near me" better than plain "Restaurant."
- **Distance:** how far you are from the searcher, or from the area they named.
- **Prominence:** how well known you are. Google says this draws on information from across the web, such as links, articles and directories, and that review count and positive ratings factor in.

::flow How a restaurant reaches the map pack | Relevance from categories, menu and reviews | Distance from the searcher | Prominence from reviews, links and press | A spot in the top three

You can't control distance. You can control almost everything about relevance and a lot about prominence. The rest of this guide is about those two.

## Choose categories that match how people search

Your primary category is one of the strongest relevance signals you have. Pick the most specific one that truly describes you.

| If you are… | Weak primary category | Stronger primary category |
|---|---|---|
| A taqueria | Restaurant | Taco restaurant or Mexican restaurant |
| A neighborhood pizza shop | Restaurant | Pizza restaurant |
| A ramen bar | Japanese restaurant | Ramen restaurant |
| A breakfast and lunch spot | Restaurant | Breakfast restaurant or Brunch restaurant |
| A cocktail bar with a full menu | Restaurant | Cocktail bar, with Restaurant as secondary |
| A coffee shop with pastries | Bakery | Coffee shop, with Bakery as secondary |

Then add secondary categories for other things you genuinely are: Caterer if you cater, Bar if you have a real bar program, Vegan restaurant only if your whole menu is vegan. Don't add categories for dishes you happen to serve. A pizza place with one pasta dish isn't an "Italian restaurant" in Google's eyes or a diner's.

A practical way to choose: search your main dish in your area, open the top three places and look at the category shown under each name. That tells you what Google associates with the search.

## Get your menu onto Google properly

Google can show menu items on your profile and uses menu text to understand what you serve. A searcher looking for "birria" or "gluten-free pizza" is more likely to see a place whose menu names those dishes.

- **Add your menu in your Business Profile.** Where the menu editor is available, enter sections, items, descriptions and prices. Otherwise add a link to your menu page.
- **Publish a text menu on your website.** A PDF or photo is hard for Google, AI assistants and guests on phones. Text is easy for all three.
- **Use the names guests use.** If everyone calls it "the spicy chicken sandwich," don't only list it as "Nashville-style hot poultry."
- **Update prices everywhere at once:** profile, website and delivery apps.

Google may also show "popular dishes" drawn from guest reviews and photos. You can't edit those directly, but you can influence them by featuring your best dishes in your own photos and by having guests who love a dish mention it in reviews.

## Photos: the first thing guests look at

On a phone, a guest opens your profile and scrolls photos before reading anything. Strong photos win the click even when you're second or third in the pack.

- **Your top ten dishes,** shot in natural light, close up, the way they're served.
- **The dining room** at a busy, inviting moment.
- **The exterior and entrance,** so people recognize you from the street and know where to park.
- **Your team,** the kitchen, the bar.
- **Seasonal items and specials** as they come and go.

Add a few new photos every month. Customers will add their own, including some unflattering ones. Your fresh, good photos keep the gallery balanced. Don't use stock photos or images of dishes you don't serve; that frustrates guests and can earn a pointed review.

## Hours: the fastest way to lose a guest

Wrong hours cost you twice. The guest who drives over to find you closed won't come back, and they may leave a one-star review that says so.

- **Regular hours** must match reality, including a kitchen that closes earlier than the bar.
- **Special hours** for every holiday, private event closure or weather closure. Set them as far ahead as you can.
- **More hours** let you show separate times for things like breakfast, happy hour, takeout, delivery or kitchen hours. Use them if your service times differ.

Google sometimes asks users to confirm or suggest hours. If your hours change, update them yourself first, so Google doesn't have to guess.

## Attributes: small details that match real searches

Attributes are the yes/no details on your profile: dine-in, takeout, delivery, outdoor seating, wheelchair-accessible entrance, vegetarian options, good for kids, live music, accepts reservations and many more. Some you set yourself. Others come from customer feedback.

They matter because people search with them. "Restaurants with outdoor seating near me," "dog-friendly patio," "kid-friendly brunch." Fill in every attribute that's true, and check customer-suggested ones for accuracy.

::compare Example: a half-finished profile vs a complete one (illustrative) | Before: primary category "Restaurant"; no menu; 12 old photos; last year's holiday hours; no attributes; no posts since spring | After: specific category; full text menu; fresh dish and room photos; holiday hours set; dine-in, patio and vegetarian options marked; weekly posts about specials

## Reviews: the biggest lever you control

Reviews feed prominence directly. Google says review count and positive ratings factor into ranking. They also drive choice: guests compare ratings and read recent reviews before picking between the three places in the pack.

### Getting more reviews

Ask every guest, every day. Small, consistent asks beat a big push once a year.

- A small card with a QR code that opens your Google review form, placed in the check presenter.
- A line on receipts.
- A follow-up email to guests who booked online or ordered direct, if they've opted in.
- Servers who mention it naturally at the end of a good meal: "If you enjoyed tonight, a Google review really helps us."

Stay inside the rules. Google's [policies on prohibited content](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) ban incentivized and fake reviews, and Google bans "review gating," which means only asking happy guests. The FTC's [Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, buying reviews, undisclosed reviews by staff or family, and suppressing negative reviews through threats, with civil penalties per violation. So no free dessert for a five-star review, and no reviews from your cousin. Our guide on [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews) has more ways to ask.

> Steady beats sudden. A handful of new reviews every week looks natural and keeps your recent reviews fresh. A burst of dozens in one weekend followed by silence can look suspicious to Google and to guests.

### Replying to reviews

Reply to as many reviews as you can, especially negative ones. Replies show guests that someone cares, and they let you add context.

- **Positive:** thank them and mention what they enjoyed. "So glad you loved the carnitas, Jess. See you next taco Tuesday."
- **Negative:** apologize for the experience, skip the excuses, and invite them to contact you directly. Never argue in public.
- **Possibly fake:** reply briefly and calmly ("We can't find a record of this visit. Please contact us at…") and flag it to Google rather than accusing anyone.

::scale Your review profile at a glance | Good: new reviews every week, most replied to within a few days, rating holding steady | Needs work: a few reviews a month, replies only to complaints | Poor: no new reviews for months, unanswered complaints at the top

## Posts: show Google you're active

Business Profile posts let you share updates, offers and events right on your listing. Posts aren't a major ranking factor on their own, but they keep your profile fresh, give guests a reason to choose you today, and show you're open and active.

Good post ideas for restaurants:

- This week's special or a new menu item, with a photo.
- An event: live music, trivia, a tasting dinner, a game-day menu.
- Holiday hours and booking reminders before busy dates.
- A seasonal change: patio open, soup season, summer menu.

## Reservations and ordering links

The buttons on your profile shape where your guests go next.

**Reserve with Google** lets guests book a table directly from your profile through supported reservation partners, such as OpenTable and others. If you already use a partner platform, check that the integration is turned on. A "Reserve a table" button removes a step for guests who've already decided.

**Order buttons** may show delivery apps by default. Where Google lets you, set your preferred ordering option and add a link to your own direct ordering page. Every regular who orders through an app instead of directly costs you a commission. Our [restaurant marketing guide](/blog/ai-marketing-for-restaurants) explains the delivery app vs direct ordering trade-off in more detail.

**Website link:** point it at your homepage or a page that clearly shows the menu, hours and ordering, not a page buried three clicks deep.

## Your website and listings support your Maps ranking

Google uses information from across the web to judge prominence. Three things help most for restaurants.

- **Consistent details everywhere.** Your name, address, phone and hours should match on Google, Yelp, Apple Maps, TripAdvisor, your reservation platform and delivery apps. Our [local SEO checklist](/blog/local-seo-checklist-small-business) covers the main listings.
- **Local press and mentions.** A write-up in a local food blog, a neighborhood best-of list or a chamber of commerce feature all add prominence. Invite local writers for a tasting when you launch something new.
- **Schema markup on your website.** Schema.org's [Restaurant type](https://schema.org/Restaurant) lets you mark up cuisine, menu, price range and reservations. Google's [local business structured data guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) shows the format, and our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-for-local-business) walks through setup.

## Mistakes that hurt restaurant rankings

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Keywords in the business name ("Joe's Pizza Best Pizza Chicago") | Breaks Google's guidelines and risks suspension | Use your real name only |
| Generic "Restaurant" category | Weak relevance for specific searches | Choose the most specific true category |
| PDF-only menu | Hard for Google and phones to read | Text menu on profile and website |
| Old holiday hours | Angry guests, bad reviews | Set special hours weeks ahead |
| Offering perks for reviews | Breaks Google policy and the FTC rule | Ask everyone, offer nothing |
| Duplicate listings after a move or rebrand | Splits reviews and confuses Google | Request removal or merging of duplicates |
| No replies to negative reviews | Guests assume nobody cares | Reply calmly within a few days |

Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) cover names, addresses and duplicates in detail. They're worth a 10-minute read before you change anything major.

Ranking well isn't a one-time setup. It's a short habit. Here's a routine one manager can run every Monday.

::steps The Monday Maps routine | Check hours — confirm the week ahead, add any special hours | Reply to reviews — every new review from the past week | Add photos — two or three new dish or room shots | Post an update — this week's special or event | Check the competition — where you rank for your top two searches

::check Monthly profile review | Categories still the best fit | Menu and prices match the restaurant | Attributes accurate, including customer-suggested ones | Reservation and order links work | No duplicate listings | Review count and rating compared with three rivals

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I get my restaurant to the top of Google Maps?

Choose the most specific primary category, complete every profile field, add a text menu and fresh photos, keep hours accurate and earn steady reviews within Google's rules. Distance from the searcher also matters, and you can't change that.

### Does replying to reviews help my restaurant rank higher?

Replies mainly help guests choose you and show the profile is active. Google encourages responding to reviews, and a profile with thoughtful replies tends to convert more of the people who see it.

### Can I offer a free appetizer for a Google review?

No. Google's policies ban incentivized reviews, and the FTC's review rule bans buying reviews. Ask every guest for an honest review and offer nothing in return.

### Should I use the "Restaurant" category or something more specific?

Use the most specific category that truly describes your main offering, such as "Pizza restaurant" or "Thai restaurant." You can add "Restaurant" or other true categories as secondary ones.

### How often should a restaurant update its Google Business Profile?

Check it weekly. Reply to reviews, add a few photos, post a special or event and confirm hours for the week ahead, especially around holidays.
