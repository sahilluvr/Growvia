You search for your own service, and there it is again. The salon two streets away sits in the top three on Google Maps. You're stuck at number seven, or missing altogether. Your work is just as good, maybe better. So what do they have that you don't?

The answer is almost never luck or a secret trick. It's usually a handful of visible things: better categories, more recent reviews, a page for every service, a few local websites linking to them. The good news is that nearly all of this is public. You can see it with a browser, a notebook and an hour of focused time.

This guide walks you through a full local competitor analysis, step by step. You'll learn who your real competitors are, how Google decides local rankings, what to compare, and how to turn what you find into a short, practical action plan. There's a worked example, a scorecard you can copy, and a simple monthly routine to keep you ahead.

## Who your real competitors are

Most owners can name their competitors from memory. That list is only half the picture. There are two kinds of competitor.

- **Business competitors** are the ones customers compare you with directly. Same service, similar price, similar area.
- **Search competitors** are the ones who appear when people search for what you sell. They might be a chain, a directory, a marketplace, or a business across town you've never heard of.

For ranking purposes, search competitors matter most. They're the ones taking the clicks and calls you want. A bakery might think of the café next door as its rival, while Google is actually showing a cloud kitchen and a delivery app for "birthday cakes near me".

### Search like a customer would

To find your search competitors, act like a customer, not an owner.

1. Open a private or incognito browser window so your own history doesn't skew results.
2. Write down five to ten searches a real customer would type. Mix service terms ("hair colour"), problem terms ("frizzy hair treatment") and "near me" terms.
3. Run each search on Google and on Google Maps. Note the top three in the map pack and the top five normal results.
4. Repeat a few searches on your phone, with location turned on.

Incognito removes your personal history, but it doesn't remove location. **Local results change depending on where the searcher is standing.** Someone searching from your street sees a different map pack than someone two kilometres away. So, if you can, run the same searches from a few spots in your service area. Ask a friend in another neighbourhood to send you screenshots, or search while you're out on errands.

> Tip: Pick the three to five names that appear most often across all your searches. Those are your real competitors for this analysis.

## How Google decides who ranks in local results

Before comparing anything, it helps to know what Google says it looks at. Google's own help page on [how local results are ranked](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) names three main factors.

| Factor | What Google means | What you can influence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | How well a Business Profile matches what someone searched for | Categories, services, description, website content |
| Distance | How far the business is from the searcher, or the location in the search | Very little, apart from accurate address and service areas |
| Prominence | How well known the business is, online and offline | Reviews, ratings, links, mentions, articles, overall reputation |

Google also says plainly that there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. That's worth remembering. Anyone promising a guaranteed top spot for a fee is selling something Google says doesn't exist.

Distance is the factor people forget. A competitor might outrank you mainly because they're closer to where most searches happen. You can't move your shop, but you can win on relevance and prominence. A strong profile often beats a closer, weaker one, especially just outside the competitor's immediate neighbourhood.

## Compare Google Business Profiles first

The Business Profile is where most local battles are won or lost. Open each competitor's profile on Google Maps and look at the following, side by side with your own.

### Categories

Your primary category is one of the strongest relevance signals you control. Check what primary category each competitor uses. You can't always see secondary categories directly, but the services and attributes listed often hint at them. If the top three salons all use "Hair salon" and you use "Beauty salon", that alone could explain a gap for hair searches.

### Reviews: count, speed and rating

Look at three things, not just the star rating.

- **Total review count.** How many reviews each business has overall.
- **Review velocity.** How many new reviews they've received recently. Sort reviews by "Newest" and count the last 30 to 90 days.
- **Average rating and replies.** Their score, and whether the owner replies to reviews, good and bad.

Velocity is often the hidden reason. A business with 150 reviews, ten of them this month, can look more active than one with 300 reviews and nothing new since spring. Customers notice recent reviews too.

### Photos, posts and services

- **Photos:** How many, how recent, and whether they show the real place, team and work.
- **Posts:** Whether they publish updates, offers or events, and how often.
- **Services and products:** Whether each service is listed, ideally with a short description.
- **Q&A and attributes:** Details like parking, accessibility, payment options, or "women-owned".
- **Hours and accuracy:** Holiday hours, correct phone, matching website link.

> Tip: Take screenshots of each profile on the day you check. Profiles change, and you'll want a "before" record to measure against.

## Compare websites

A Business Profile links to a website, and Google uses that site to understand what you offer. Visit each competitor's site and look for these things.

- **A page per service.** Does the competitor have separate pages for "keratin treatment", "bridal makeup" and "hair colour", while you have one "Services" page with a list? Dedicated pages usually match searches far better.
- **Location pages.** If they serve several areas, do they have a useful page for each, with real local detail? Thin pages that only swap the town name don't count.
- **Content depth.** Do their pages answer real questions, such as prices, timings, what to expect, and aftercare? Or are they a few lines long?
- **Speed and mobile use.** Run both sites through Google's free [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/). Also simply open them on your phone. Is booking or calling easy?
- **Trust signals.** Real photos, team names, reviews, clear contact details and an address that matches the Business Profile.
- **Structured data.** Some sites add [LocalBusiness structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business), which helps Google read details like hours and address. You can check any page with Google's Rich Results Test.

You're not looking to copy their pages. You're looking for gaps. If three competitors each have a page for a service you offer and you don't, that's a clear, fixable gap.

## Backlinks and local citations, the basics

Links and mentions from other websites are part of prominence. You don't need to become an SEO expert to compare them at a basic level.

### Local citations

A citation is any listing of your business name, address and phone number. Think of local directories, industry listings, maps apps and chamber of commerce pages. Search each competitor's exact business name in quotes. Note where they're listed and whether their details match everywhere.

If competitors appear on the main local directories and you don't, add yourself. Then make sure your name, address and phone are identical everywhere.

### Backlinks

Backlinks are links from other sites to yours. For local businesses, the most useful ones are often local and real: a newspaper feature, a sponsor page for a school event, a supplier's "where to buy" page, a local blogger's review.

Free SEO tools offer a limited look at any site's links. Even a rough list tells you a lot. If a competitor has links from the local news site and two community groups, you know where to start building relationships.

> Tip: Look for the "why" behind each link. A sponsorship, an event or a partnership is something you can do too, in your own way.

## Compare ads and social presence

Sometimes a competitor seems to be everywhere simply because they're paying for it. Two free official tools let you see this.

- **[Google Ads Transparency Center](https://adstransparency.google.com/):** Search an advertiser or website name and filter by date and location. Google says it covers ads shown on Search, Display, Gmail and YouTube. You'll see which ads they run and roughly what they promote.
- **[Meta Ad Library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library/):** Search a business's Page name to see the ads it's currently running on Facebook and Instagram. Look at the offers, images and how many different versions they test.

Ads don't change organic or map rankings. But they show you which services a competitor thinks are worth paying to promote. That's useful market research.

For social media, check each competitor's main accounts. Note how often they post, what gets real comments, and whether they share customer work. You're looking for patterns, not follower counts.

## Check who AI assistants recommend

More customers now ask ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or Google's AI Overviews for recommendations. These answers usually name only a few businesses, so being left out matters.

Try the same questions a customer would ask, for example: *"Best salon for balayage in Sector 70 Mohali?"* or *"Which family restaurant near Phase 7 has outdoor seating?"* Run each question in a few assistants. Note:

- Which businesses are named, and in what order.
- What reasons the assistant gives ("known for", "highly rated for").
- Which sources it cites, if any.

The reasons are the useful part. If an assistant says a competitor is "known for organic products", that phrase probably appears in their reviews, website and listings. Answers also vary between runs and between tools, so check a few times before drawing conclusions. ## A worked example: two salons in one city

Here's a made-up example to show how this works in practice. The businesses and numbers are fictional.

**Glow Studio** and **Mirror Mirror Salon** are both in the same part of Mohali. Mirror Mirror shows in the Maps top three for "hair salon near me" and "keratin treatment Mohali". Glow Studio rarely appears. Glow's owner, Neha, runs through the steps above.

Here's what she finds:

- Mirror Mirror's primary category is "Hair salon". Glow uses "Beauty salon".
- Mirror Mirror has fewer total reviews than Glow, but has received around a dozen in the last month. Glow has had two.
- Mirror Mirror replies to every review. Glow replies to a few.
- Mirror Mirror's website has separate pages for keratin, hair colour, bridal and hair spa. Glow has one page listing everything.
- Mirror Mirror posts weekly offers on its Business Profile. Glow last posted months ago.
- Mirror Mirror is listed in two local wedding directories and was featured on a city lifestyle blog.
- When Neha asks an AI assistant for keratin treatment recommendations nearby, it names Mirror Mirror and mentions "experienced with keratin".

None of this is mysterious. Mirror Mirror isn't winning on quality. It's winning on relevance (category, service pages) and fresh prominence (recent reviews, local mentions). Each of those is something Glow can work on.

## Turn findings into an action plan

A long list of observations won't move your rankings. A short, prioritised plan will. Use a gap table with five columns: the area, what they have, what you have, the action, and the effort.

::steps From findings to a plan | List every gap — categories, reviews, pages, links, photos | Rank each gap by impact and effort | Pick three fixes for this month — and finish them | Re-check — search again next month and compare

Here's Neha's gap table for Glow Studio:

| Area | Them (Mirror Mirror) | You (Glow Studio) | Action | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary category | Hair salon | Beauty salon | Switch primary to Hair salon if hair is the main business | Low |
| New reviews | About 12 last month | 2 last month | Ask every happy client in person, with a QR card at the counter | Low, ongoing |
| Review replies | Replies to all | Replies to some | Reply to every review within a few days | Low |
| Service pages | 4 dedicated pages | 1 list page | Write a proper page for each main service | Medium |
| Profile posts | Weekly | None recently | Post one update or offer each week | Low |
| Local mentions | 2 directories, 1 blog | None found | List in local directories, pitch a local blog | Medium |
| AI mentions | Named for keratin | Not named | Add clear keratin details to site, profile and services | Medium |

### How to prioritise

1. Start with **low effort, high impact** items, such as category fixes, review replies and profile details.
2. Next, set up **habits**, such as asking for reviews and posting weekly. These compound over time.
3. Then tackle **medium effort** work, such as service pages and local outreach.
4. Leave **big projects**, like a full website rebuild, until the basics are done.

Reviews are usually the slowest gap to close, so start early. Our guide on [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews) has scripts and timing ideas that work for busy owners.

> Tip: Don't try to fix everything at once. Three actions done properly this month beat ten half-started.

## A simple monthly tracking routine

Competitor analysis isn't a one-time job. Profiles change, new businesses open, and Google's results move. A short monthly check keeps you informed without eating your week.

Set aside about an hour on the same day each month:

1. Run your five to ten key searches in incognito, ideally from two locations. Note Maps positions.
2. Update review counts, new reviews and ratings in your scorecard.
3. Glance at each competitor's recent Business Profile posts and photos.
4. Check the Google Ads Transparency Center and Meta Ad Library for new campaigns.
5. Ask two or three AI assistants your main customer questions and note who's named.
6. Review your gap table. Tick off what's done and pick the next three actions.

Every quarter, do a deeper review. Look at competitors' websites again for new pages, and recheck which businesses are really appearing in your searches. A new rival may have arrived. ## The ethical lines you shouldn't cross

When you see a competitor winning, it's tempting to look for shortcuts. Some shortcuts break platform rules and can get your profile suspended. Others are simply unfair. Stay well clear of these.

- **Don't fake or buy reviews.** Google's [Maps content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) bans paid reviews, reviews posted from multiple accounts, and offering discounts or freebies in exchange for reviews. It also bans asking only happy customers while discouraging others.
- **Don't attack competitors' profiles.** The same policy prohibits posting content on a competitor's listing to damage their reputation. That includes fake negative reviews and false edits to their details.
- **Don't copy their content.** Learning from a competitor's page structure is fine. Copying their text, photos or offers word for word is not. It can create legal trouble and duplicate content rarely helps you rank.
- **Don't click their ads.** Google's [definition of invalid clicks](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/42995) includes manual clicks that increase an advertiser's costs. It's against the rules, and Google filters much of it out anyway.
- **Don't stuff your business name.** Adding keywords like "Best Salon Mohali" to your profile name breaks Google's guidelines and can get your listing suspended.

Fair play is also more effective. Real reviews, useful pages and local relationships can't be taken away from you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do competitors rank higher on Google Maps than me?

Usually it's a mix of relevance, distance and prominence, the three factors Google names. They may use a better-matching category, have more recent reviews, or have stronger local mentions. They may also simply be closer to where the searcher is. A side-by-side comparison of profiles and websites usually reveals the main gap within an hour.

### How often should I do a local competitor analysis?

Do one thorough analysis to start, then a quick check each month. The monthly check covers rankings, reviews and new activity. Every three months, take a deeper look at websites and whether new competitors have appeared.

### Is it legal to spy on competitors?

Looking at public information is normal and legal. Business Profiles, websites, public reviews and ad libraries are all designed to be seen. What crosses the line is deception, such as fake reviews, fake accounts, copying content or clicking their ads to waste their budget.

### Do I need paid SEO tools for competitor analysis?

No, not to start. Google Maps, an incognito browser, PageSpeed Insights, the Google Ads Transparency Center and the Meta Ad Library are all free. Paid tools save time and show more backlink data, which helps once you're tracking several competitors regularly.

### Can I outrank a competitor who is closer to the customer?

Often, yes, especially for searchers a little further from them. Distance matters, but a profile with stronger relevance and prominence can beat a closer, weaker one. Focus on categories, service pages, steady reviews and local mentions.

### Should I copy what my top competitor does?

Learn from them, but don't copy them. Note the gaps, such as missing service pages or slow review growth, and fill them in your own words and style. Your goal is to be the clearer, more trusted choice, not a second version of them.
