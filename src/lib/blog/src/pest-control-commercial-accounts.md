Pest control has a natural advantage over most trades: the best customers need you on a schedule. A restaurant can't wait for a problem to appear; an inspector might find it first. A warehouse storing food or packaging needs documented monitoring. A property manager with hundreds of units would rather prevent infestations than fight them building by building.

That makes commercial accounts the fastest route to predictable revenue for a pest control business. This guide covers who to target, how to reach them and what a winning service plan looks like.


## Why commercial accounts make a pest control company stronger

Residential pest control is seasonal and reactive. Ants in spring, wasps in summer, rodents when the weather turns cold. Homeowners call when they see a problem, pay for a treatment and often don't call again until the next one. Even residential service plans see cancellations whenever budgets tighten.

Commercial accounts work on prevention, and prevention runs on a schedule. A restaurant needs regular visits whether or not anyone has seen a pest, because an inspector or a customer might see one first. A food warehouse needs monitoring devices checked and logged because auditors and customers expect evidence. A hotel can't risk a bed bug review. A property manager wants building-wide programs because treating one unit at a time never solves the problem.

That makes commercial revenue predictable. Visits can be routed efficiently, technicians build familiarity with each site and the business relies on you for documentation as much as treatment. Accounts that are serviced well tend to stay, because switching providers means re-establishing monitoring, logs and trust, and few managers want that hassle.

Commercial work also raises your company's professional standing. Technicians who learn to work in food environments, document carefully and communicate with managers become more skilled overall, which improves your residential service too.

## The first email

> **Subject:** Keeping your kitchen inspection-ready
>
> Hi Marco — we service several restaurants on Westheimer with monthly visits before opening and a service log after every visit, so your paperwork is ready whenever an inspector walks in. Would a free inspection next week be helpful? It takes about 30 minutes and you'll get a written report either way.

The service log is the hook. Restaurant owners worry about inspections; a vendor who reduces that worry gets attention.

## Keeping accounts

Commercial pest accounts rarely leave because of price. They leave because of missed visits, unclear reports or a technician who doesn't explain what they did. Keep the same technician on the account where you can, leave a short note after every visit and review the account with the owner every few months.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Referencing someone's bad reviews.** It feels like a threat. Lead with prevention.
- **Selling one-off treatments to commercial clients.** Plans create reliability for both of you.
- **Skipping documentation.** For food businesses, the log is part of what they're paying for.

## Your first inspection visit

1. Ask about any recent sightings, complaints or inspection notes.
2. Walk the kitchen, storage, receiving area, dumpsters and exterior.
3. Note entry points such as gaps under doors, around pipes and in walls.
4. Look for conditions that attract pests: standing water, food debris, cluttered storage.
5. Explain findings to the owner or manager before you leave.
6. Send a written report with photos and a recommended plan within a day.

Be careful with tone. Owners can feel judged during inspections. Present findings as practical steps rather than criticism, and acknowledge what they're doing well.

## Getting your company ready for commercial buyers

Commercial clients check licenses and insurance first, so have current certificates and applicator licenses ready to send. Build a commercial page on your website explaining your service plans, how you document visits and the industries you serve. Prepare a sample service log and a sample inspection report with the business details removed. Make sure technicians on commercial routes wear uniforms, drive marked vehicles where appropriate and know how to be discreet in customer-facing spaces. In restaurants and hotels, discretion is part of the service.

## Building plans by industry

**Restaurants and cafés.** Monthly visits (or more often for busy kitchens), interior and exterior monitoring, a service log after each visit and fast call-backs. Schedule visits before opening or after closing.

**Food warehouses.** Device maps, regular monitoring, documented trend reports and corrective action records. These clients value consistency and paperwork above everything.

**Hotels.** Preventive inspections, staff training on early signs of bed bugs and a rapid-response protocol for guest complaints. Discretion is essential.

**Property managers.** Common-area programs plus on-demand unit treatments, with clear pricing and reporting by building.

**Schools and daycares.** Treatments scheduled when children are absent, clear communication with administrators and methods appropriate for sensitive environments.

## A worked example

Here's how a restaurant group account might develop. The details are illustrative.

You email the owner of a small group with three restaurants near your routes. Your follow-up about service logs arrives a few weeks before a scheduled health inspection, and the owner books a free inspection at the busiest location. You find gaps under the back door, dry goods stored on the floor and a grease trap overdue for service, and you note that their current provider's logs are thin.

Your report that afternoon lists the issues with photos, practical fixes for the kitchen team and a monthly plan with service logs. The owner signs for that location. After the inspection goes smoothly, she adds the other two locations and asks you to brief her managers on the kitchen habits checklist. A year later, when she opens a fourth location, you're part of the opening plan.

## Mistakes to avoid as you grow

Growing commercial accounts too quickly can stretch technicians thin, and missed visits are the fastest way to lose them. Add accounts at a pace your team can serve. Keep logs consistent across technicians with a standard format. Review each account's trend data every few months, and share it with the client so they see the value of prevention. Finally, don't let price cuts become your main tool; commercial clients care far more about reliability and documentation.

## Training technicians for commercial accounts

Commercial accounts need technicians who are thorough, discreet and good communicators. Train them on how to work in food environments without disrupting service, how to document visits consistently and how to explain findings to managers in plain language. Role-play common situations, such as a manager who's defensive about sanitation issues or a hotel guest complaint. Technicians who handle these conversations well build trust, and trust keeps accounts.

Pair new technicians with experienced ones on commercial routes before they work alone. Review their logs regularly for completeness. Recognize technicians whose accounts renew and grow; they're your most valuable sales team, even if they never think of themselves that way.

## Preparing clients for audits and inspections

Food businesses and warehouses face inspections and audits that look at pest control records. Help clients prepare: keep logs organized and accessible, maintain device maps, document corrective actions and note any recommendations the client has acted on. Offer a pre-audit review for warehouse clients to check documentation is complete. Clients who pass inspections with your help see clear value in your service and are far less likely to switch providers on price.

## Building a referral loop

Restaurant owners, hotel managers and warehouse operators talk to each other. After a few months of reliable service, ask happy clients if they know other businesses that might benefit. Offer a simple thank-you, within any rules that apply, and follow up promptly on introductions. Industry associations and local business groups are also good places to meet prospects in a relaxed setting.

## Communicating findings without blame

Managers can feel criticized when pest issues are found. Frame findings around solutions: what you found, why it matters and what both sides will do. Acknowledge what's working well. A respectful tone keeps managers engaged and makes them more likely to follow recommendations.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do pest control companies get commercial clients?

Target businesses that need prevention on a schedule, such as restaurants, food warehouses and property managers. Offer a free inspection, send written findings and propose a monthly service plan with logs.

### What should a commercial pest control agreement include?

Visit frequency, monitoring devices, the areas covered, service logs, response time for call-backs and a fixed monthly price.

### Why do restaurants need service logs?

Logs show inspectors and auditors that pest prevention is ongoing. They're also a record of what was found and treated.

### How many prospects should I contact each week?

Fifty to seventy-five well-chosen local businesses a week is manageable for most owners, with follow-ups. Focus on your existing routes.

### How often should restaurants get pest control service?

Many restaurants have monthly service, and busy kitchens or those with past issues may need more frequent visits. Base the frequency on the inspection and local requirements.

### What should a pest control service log include?

The date, areas inspected, devices checked, any activity found, actions taken and recommendations for the client.

### Do pest control companies need special licenses for commercial work?

Most states require applicator licenses and may have specific categories for commercial or structural work. Check your state's rules.

### How do I keep commercial pest control clients long term?

Never miss visits, keep logs clear and consistent, share trends with the client and respond quickly to call-backs.

### Can I offer free inspections to commercial prospects?

Yes. A short free inspection with a written report is a common and effective way to start a commercial relationship.

### What is a device map in pest control?

A diagram showing where monitoring devices and stations are placed, used to document coverage and track activity.

### How do pest control companies help with audits?

By maintaining organized logs, device maps and corrective action records, and reviewing documentation before audits.

### What should technicians tell restaurant managers after a visit?

What they inspected, any activity found, actions taken and practical recommendations for staff.

### How can pest control companies reduce call-backs?

Thorough inspections, addressing entry points, clear recommendations to clients and consistent follow-up.

### Do commercial clients need emergency service?

Many value it, especially restaurants and hotels. Be clear about response times and any extra charges.

### How should technicians discuss sanitation issues with clients?

Respectfully, focusing on practical solutions and acknowledging what's working well.

### How do pest control companies price commercial plans?

Usually by site size, risk level and visit frequency, with a fixed monthly price and clear call-back terms.

### What makes a pest control company audit-ready?

Organized logs, device maps, corrective action records and consistent documentation across visits.

### How do I find out who handles pest control at a restaurant group?

Start with the owner or general manager listed on the website. Larger groups may have an operations or facilities manager. Ask politely during your first contact who makes vendor decisions for each location.
