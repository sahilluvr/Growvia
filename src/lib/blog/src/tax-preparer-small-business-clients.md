Individual tax returns are seasonal and price-sensitive. Small business clients are different: their returns are more involved, they need help during the year and they rarely switch preparers once they trust one. A practice built on small business clients has steadier income and less of a tax-season crush.

The catch is timing. Business owners choose a preparer before deadlines approach, and many have stayed with someone they're unhappy with because switching felt like work. This guide shows how to reach them early with a reason to switch. Also see our [CPA tax season marketing calendar](/blog/cpa-tax-season-marketing-calendar).


## Why small business clients are the better book of business

Individual returns pay the bills in spring and then go quiet. Fees are under pressure from software and chain preparers, and many individual clients shop around every year. Small business clients are different. Their returns involve more forms, more judgment and more planning, and the relationship extends throughout the year: quarterly estimates, payroll questions, entity changes, equipment purchases and year-end decisions.

Business clients also stay longer. Once a preparer understands an owner's business, switching is risky and time-consuming for the owner, so good relationships last for years. Each business client often brings related work too, such as the owner's personal return, a spouse's business or a second entity.

A practice built on business clients has a smoother calendar. Year-end planning in the autumn, quarterly reviews and mid-year check-ins spread work across the year instead of concentrating everything in a few frantic months. That makes staffing easier and improves the quality of work.

The catch is that business owners need a reason to switch. Most stay with a preparer they're mildly unhappy with because switching feels like effort. The strongest reason you can offer is proactive planning: help that changes the outcome before the year ends, not just a return filed afterwards.

::steps When to reach business owners | Late summer: offer a mid-year review | October–November: offer year-end planning before the year closes | January: confirm documents and deadlines | After filing: plan next year's estimates

Year-end planning is the strongest hook. It's the last chance to act on the current year, and owners know it.

## Your first review meeting

1. How is the business structured, and has that changed recently?
2. How did last year's return go, and were there any surprises?
3. Are your estimated payments current?
4. What big purchases or changes are planned for next year?
5. Who keeps your books, and how up to date are they?
6. What would make tax season less stressful for you?

Use the answers to identify one or two concrete planning opportunities. Owners switch when they see a specific benefit, not general promises.

## Preparing your practice

Business owners will research you. Make sure your website explains business tax services, planning and how engagements work. List credentials clearly, such as CPA or enrolled agent status. Prepare a document checklist, a sample planning summary and a clear engagement letter. Have a secure way for clients to send documents. Respond to inquiries quickly in autumn, when owners are thinking about year end.

## Year-round services that keep clients

**Year-end planning.** A review in October or November covering purchases, retirement contributions and estimates.

**Quarterly estimate reviews.** Short check-ins to adjust estimated payments based on actual results.

**Entity reviews.** Periodic reviews of whether the business structure still fits.

**Coordination with bookkeepers.** Agreed timelines for clean books before filing.

**Mid-year check-ins.** A quick review after the first half to catch changes early.

Package these into an annual fee so owners know what to expect and feel the value throughout the year.

## A worked example

Here's how a business client might come on board. The details are illustrative.

In October, you email a roofing contractor offering a year-end review. He replies to your follow-up about equipment purchases because he's been thinking about a new truck and isn't sure about timing. In a short review, you look at his year-to-date numbers, discuss the purchase and point out that his estimated payments are behind.

He decides to buy the truck before year end and catches up on estimates, avoiding a surprise in April. Impressed, he moves his business and personal returns to you and signs up for quarterly reviews. In spring, he mentions you to his brother, who runs a landscaping company, and to his bookkeeper, who starts referring clients.

## Building referrals with bookkeepers and bankers

Bookkeepers need a reliable preparer for clients with clean books, and they appreciate preparers who communicate deadlines clearly. Bankers meet owners who need financial statements and tax returns for loans. Introduce yourself with a one-page summary, explain how you coordinate and keep partners informed with client consent. Referrals from these advisors arrive with trust already built.

## Onboarding new business clients smoothly

The first few weeks with a new business client set the tone for the relationship. Send an engagement letter that explains scope, fees and responsibilities. Follow with a document checklist and a secure way to upload files. Request prior returns, financial statements, payroll reports and any notices from tax authorities. Schedule a short kickoff call to confirm deadlines, estimated payments and who keeps the books.

During onboarding, look for quick wins: missed deductions, estimated payments that are off or registrations that need updating. Fixing something early shows value immediately. Set calendar reminders for each client's key dates, such as quarterly estimates, payroll filings and year-end planning, so you contact them before deadlines rather than after.

## Communicating during tax season

Business owners worry most when they don't know what's happening. Send short status updates: documents received, return in progress, questions for the client, return ready for review, filed. Answer questions within a business day, even if the answer is "we're looking into it". Explain any balance due well before the deadline, with options for payment. Clear communication reduces stress for clients and fewer frantic calls for you.

After filing, send a brief summary of the year: what changed, what to watch next year and the next planning date. This turns a once-a-year transaction into an ongoing relationship.

## Building a niche reputation

Preparers who focus on one or two industries learn the common issues, deductions and pitfalls quickly. Share that knowledge through short articles, workshops with bookkeepers or industry associations, and examples (without client details) in your outreach. A contractor is more likely to choose a preparer who understands equipment, vehicles and subcontractors than a generalist. Over time, niche clients refer peers, and industry associations may invite you to speak.

## Helping clients with notices and letters

Business owners often panic when a letter arrives from a tax authority. Offer to review notices promptly, explain what they mean in plain language and outline the next steps. Many notices are routine and can be resolved with a short response or documentation. Clients who see you handle these calmly gain confidence in your practice, and notice support is a strong reason to stay with you year after year. Include notice review in annual packages or price it clearly as a separate service.

## Explaining entity choices without overwhelming owners

Many owners chose their business structure when they started and haven't revisited it since. As businesses grow, the right structure can change. When reviewing entity choices, explain the main differences in plain language, focus on how each option affects their situation and coordinate with the client's attorney for legal aspects. Avoid presenting entity changes as a magic solution; explain trade-offs, costs and timing. Owners appreciate balanced advice, and these conversations often lead to deeper planning relationships.

## Using reminders to stay top of mind

A simple reminder system keeps you present between tax seasons. Send short notes before estimated payment deadlines, a mid-year check-in invitation and a reminder about year-end planning in the autumn. Keep messages brief and useful. Clients who hear from you at the right moments rarely drift to another preparer, and they're more likely to refer you because you're on their mind.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do tax preparers get small business clients?

Contact businesses before year-end with an offer of planning, not just filing. Build referrals with bookkeepers, bankers and financial advisors.

### When should tax preparers market to businesses?

Late summer through November is ideal, while there's still time to act on the current year.

### What should a tax preparer offer beyond filing?

Year-end planning, quarterly estimate reviews and coordination with the client's bookkeeper and advisors.

### Should tax preparers compete on price?

Not with business clients. Planning, responsiveness and fewer surprises matter more to them.

### What's the best time to win new business tax clients?

Early autumn through November, when owners can still act on the current year.

### Should tax preparers offer annual packages?

Yes. Annual packages with planning, estimates and filing make value visible and revenue predictable.

### Do enrolled agents need to follow advertising rules?

Tax professionals have their own professional standards on advertising and solicitation. Review the rules that apply to your credential.

### How can tax preparers work with bookkeepers?

Agree on timelines for clean books, how files are shared and how you'll communicate about clients, with client consent.

### What should a tax engagement letter include?

The scope of services, fees, responsibilities of both sides, deadlines and how documents will be shared.

### How can business owners avoid surprises at tax time?

By keeping books current, reviewing estimated payments during the year and doing year-end planning before December 31.

### Should tax preparers offer bookkeeping too?

Some do; others partner with bookkeepers. Partnering lets you focus on tax work while ensuring clean books.

### What documents do business clients need to provide?

Typically income records, expense details, asset purchases, payroll reports, prior returns and any tax notices.

### How do I handle a client who's behind on filings?

Start with an assessment of what's missing, then create a plan to catch up, prioritizing the most urgent filings.

### When should business owners make estimated tax payments?

Estimated payments follow set deadlines during the year. A preparer can calculate amounts and remind clients before each due date.

### What should I do if a client receives a tax notice?

Review it promptly, explain what it means and outline next steps, which may include a response or documentation.

### Should business owners review their entity structure?

Periodically, especially as the business grows or changes. Coordinate with the client's attorney for legal aspects.

### How often should tax preparers contact business clients?

Before estimated payment deadlines, mid-year and before year end, plus during tax season.

### Do tax preparers need a secure document portal?

A secure method for sharing documents protects client information and is expected by many clients.

### How do I explain fees to new business clients?

Clearly in the engagement letter, listing what's included and how additional work is priced.

### How do tax preparers attract new businesses mid-year?

With mid-year reviews that check estimated payments and planning opportunities before year end.

### What should a year-end planning review cover?

Year-to-date results, planned purchases, retirement contributions, estimated payments and any changes expected next year.

### Can tax preparers help with payroll questions?

Many coordinate with payroll providers or bookkeepers and can explain how payroll affects the business's taxes.

### How do tax preparers keep business clients year round?

With quarterly check-ins, year-end planning, notice support and quick answers to questions.
