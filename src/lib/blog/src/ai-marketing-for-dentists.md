Most dental practices don't have a marketing problem. They have a leaking-bucket problem. New patients find you on Google Maps, call once, get voicemail, and book with the office down the street. Hygiene patients drift past their recall date and nobody notices for a year. Reviews pile up unanswered, or worse, get answered in a way that breaks HIPAA.

An all-in-one AI marketing tool for dentists can help with a lot of this, but only if it sits inside a clear system. Software doesn't fix a practice that has no process for asking for reviews or following up with overdue patients. It just makes a good process faster.

This guide lays out that system end to end: how patients actually choose a dentist, how to rank in Google Maps, how to collect and reply to reviews safely, how to fill the hygiene schedule, how to choose between ads and Local Services Ads, how to show up in AI assistants, and which tools do which job. It ends with a 90-day plan and the three numbers you should track every month.

## How patients choose a dentist in 2026

Before spending a dollar, picture the path a new patient takes. It is shorter and more impatient than most practice owners assume.

::flow How a new patient finds and books a dentist | Searches "dentist near me" or asks an AI assistant | Compares 3 Maps listings by stars, reviews and photos | Visits the website to check insurance and hours | Calls, books online or fills a form

Every step is a place to lose them. A listing with 40 reviews from 2021 loses to one with 200 recent reviews. A site that hides insurance loses to one that names accepted plans up front. A phone that rings out at lunch loses to one that is answered.

The practical lesson: fix the steps in order. There is no point buying ads to send traffic to a profile with weak reviews, or to a website with no booking path.

### The jobs a dental marketing system has to do

Think of marketing as six jobs.

1. **Get found** in Google Maps, organic search and AI assistants.
2. **Get chosen** through reviews, photos and a clear website.
3. **Get booked** by answering fast and making booking easy.
4. **Keep patients** through recall, reactivation and good service.
5. **Stay compliant** with HIPAA, the FTC review rule, state dental board advertising rules and texting law.
6. **Measure** new patients, cost per new patient and recall rate.

## Job 1: Get found in Google Maps and search

For most practices, the Google Maps "local pack" (the three listings under the map) drives more new-patient calls than anything else. Google says local results are based mainly on **relevance, distance and prominence** in its [official explanation of local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091). You can't move your building, but you can improve relevance and prominence.

### Your Google Business Profile comes first

Your Google Business Profile (GBP) is the listing that appears in Maps. Treat it like a second website.

::check Google Business Profile basics for a dental practice | Primary category set to "Dentist" (or your specialty) | Secondary categories that match real services, such as "Emergency dental service" | Exact name, address and phone that match your website | Hours, holiday hours and "Accepts new patients" kept current | Services list with implants, Invisalign, crowns, cleanings | 20+ real photos of the office, team and equipment | A weekly post or update

Don't stuff keywords into your business name ("Smile Dental Best Dentist Tampa Implants"). It breaks [Google's guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) and can get the profile suspended. Our [Google Business Profile optimization guide](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-guide) covers every field in detail.

### Dental SEO: service pages beat a single "Services" page

Organic search still matters, especially for high-value treatments. Someone searching "dental implants cost Tampa" wants a page about implants in Tampa, not a homepage with a bullet list.

Build one page per major treatment (implants, Invisalign or clear aligners, emergency dentistry, crowns, veneers, pediatric, sedation if you offer it). Each page should explain who it's for, what happens at each visit, a realistic range of time, how insurance and financing work, and what makes your practice different. Add [Dentist schema markup](/blog/schema-markup-for-local-business) so Google understands the page.

You rank against specific offices, not against an abstract algorithm. ## Job 2: Get chosen with reviews (without breaking HIPAA)

Reviews influence both ranking and choice. Google lists review count and score as local ranking factors on the page linked above, and patients compare stars before they ever see your website.

But dentistry has a trap that most businesses don't: **your review replies can violate HIPAA.**

::stats Real HIPAA settlements over dental review replies | $10,000 | Elite Dental Associates, Dallas (2019) | $23,000 | New Vision Dental, California (2022)

Both practices settled with the HHS Office for Civil Rights after replying to Yelp reviews with patient information. You can browse OCR's [resolution agreements](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/index.html) yourself. The lesson is simple. In a public reply, never confirm that someone is a patient, and never mention treatment, appointments, insurance or anything from their chart. Even "We're sorry your crown didn't fit" confirms both.

> The safe default for any review reply: thank them, speak about your practice's general policies, and invite them to call the office. Never confirm the person was ever a patient.

### Getting more reviews the right way

- **Ask everyone.** Google's policies ban "review gating", which means only asking patients you think are happy. The FTC's [Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) (16 CFR Part 465, effective October 21, 2024) also bans buying reviews, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression.
- **Ask at checkout.** The best moment is right after a good visit, at the front desk, with a QR card that opens your review link.
- **Follow up once.** Use your patient communication platform (the one covered by a business associate agreement) to send a single follow-up link.
- **Never offer incentives tied to a review.** No discounts, raffles or free whitening for reviews.

## Job 3: Get booked: website and response speed

A practice can rank first and still lose the patient on its website or phone.

### What a new-patient website needs

The first screen of your homepage should answer four questions in five seconds: what you do, where you are, whether you take the patient's insurance, and how to book. Most dental sites bury two of the four.

Put a click-to-call phone number and a "Book online" button in the header on mobile. Add an insurance section that names the plans you're in-network with. Make the emergency path obvious ("Same-day emergency appointments, call now"). Keep the site fast, since a slow mobile page loses visitors before it loads. Our [Core Web Vitals guide](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained) explains what "fast" means to Google.

Before-and-after photos need written patient consent, and your state dental board may have rules on how results and claims are presented. Check your state's current rules before publishing them.

The full teardown is in our guide to building a [dental website that converts new patients](/blog/dental-website-new-patient-conversion), including an above-the-fold checklist and accessibility basics.

### Forms: collect less, not more

Web forms should collect only what you need to call someone back: name, phone, email, preferred time and whether they're a new patient. Don't ask for symptoms, medical history or insurance member IDs on a general marketing form. That information belongs in your HIPAA-compliant intake or patient portal. Our guide to [website contact form best practices](/blog/website-contact-form-best-practices) covers layout, spam protection and auto-replies.

### Answer the phone, or call back fast

The office that responds first usually books the patient. If calls go to voicemail at lunch or after 5 p.m., you're paying to generate leads for competitors. Options include staggered lunch breaks, a call overflow service, or a phone system that texts back missed callers. Weave and similar dental phone platforms do the text-back part well. ## Job 4: Keep patients with recall and reactivation

New-patient marketing gets the attention, but the hygiene schedule is where many practices leave the most money on the table. A patient who is six months overdue already knows you, trusts you and has a chart on file. Bringing them back costs a few messages.

::funnel Example: reactivating overdue patients (illustrative numbers) | Patients 12+ months overdue: 800 | Reached by message: 600 | Replied or clicked: 150 | Booked a hygiene visit: 60

The numbers above are illustrative, not a benchmark. The point is that even a modest response from a list you already own often beats paid acquisition on cost.

### How to run it

1. Pull a list from your practice management system (PMS) of patients with no future appointment, grouped by last visit: 6–12 months, 12–18 months, 18–36 months.
2. Send recall and reactivation messages from your PMS or a HIPAA-compliant platform such as Weave or NexHealth. Under HIPAA, appointment reminders and recall notices are treatment communications and don't need marketing authorization, but they still involve PHI, so they belong in a system covered by a business associate agreement.
3. Use short, warm wording that doesn't mention specific procedures in subject lines or previews.
4. Measure reactivation rate: patients who booked divided by patients contacted.

## Job 5: Paid ads vs Local Services Ads

Paid search can fill gaps quickly, especially for high-value treatments and emergencies. Dentists have two main Google options.

**Google Local Services Ads (LSA)** appear at the very top of some searches with a "Google Verified" badge. You pay per lead (a call or message), not per click, after passing Google's screening. Dentists are an eligible category in the US. You can dispute leads that clearly aren't valid. See [Google's Local Services help center](https://support.google.com/localservices) for current eligibility and verification steps.

**Google Search ads** charge per click and give you more control over keywords ("emergency dentist open Saturday", "dental implants near me"), ad copy and landing pages. They also need more management and a good landing page to work.

| | Local Services Ads | Google Search ads |
|---|---|---|
| You pay for | Each lead (call or message) | Each click |
| Setup | Verification and screening | Account, keywords, ads, landing pages |
| Control | Low: categories, budget, hours | High: keywords, bids, copy, pages |
| Best for | Calls from people ready to book | Specific treatments and emergencies |
| Biggest risk | Missed calls still cost you trust and rank | Wasted clicks on broad keywords |

Many practices start with LSA for steady calls and add Search ads for implants or Invisalign later. ## Job 6: Show up in AI assistants

A growing number of patients ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity "Who's a good family dentist near me that takes Delta?" before they ever open Maps. These assistants draw on your website, your Google profile, review themes and directory listings.

What helps:

- Consistent name, address, phone and hours across your site, GBP, Healthgrades, Zocdoc, Yelp and insurance directories.
- Service pages that answer real questions in plain language.
- Reviews that mention specific services in patients' own words (you can't script this, but you can ask patients to "mention what you came in for" if they're comfortable).
- An "About" page with each dentist's credentials, training and memberships.

## Social media: useful, but not first

Social media rarely drives as many new patients as Maps and search, but it builds familiarity and supports recruiting. Two or three posts a week is enough.

Good content pillars: meet-the-team posts, office tours, quick hygiene tips, myth-busting, community events, and before-and-after cases with written consent. Never post a patient's face, story or treatment without signed authorization.

## A 90-day dental marketing plan

Here's a realistic plan for a one- to three-dentist practice with a front desk and no marketing staff. Dr. Patel's two-chair practice in Tampa is a useful (hypothetical) picture: decent reputation, 70 Google reviews, page two on Maps for "dentist near me", and a hygiene schedule with gaps.

::steps 90-day dental growth plan | Days 1–14 — Fix GBP, NAP consistency, insurance section and phone coverage | Days 15–30 — Start the checkout review habit and adopt safe reply templates | Days 31–45 — Launch recall and reactivation in your PMS or patient platform | Days 46–60 — Publish implant, emergency and Invisalign service pages | Days 61–75 — Test LSA with a small budget and answer every lead | Days 76–90 — Review KPIs, check AI visibility, set next quarter's targets

### What each phase looks like in practice

- **Foundations (days 1–14):** audit your GBP and website, fix the top five SEO issues by impact, make name, address and phone match everywhere, add an insurance section and mobile click-to-call, and decide who answers calls at lunch and after hours.
- **Reviews (days 15–30):** print QR review cards, train one checkout line ("If you had a good visit today, a Google review really helps other families find us"), and reply safely to every review from the last 90 days.
- **Recall (days 31–45):** pull overdue segments from your PMS, send a three-message sequence through your HIPAA-compliant platform, and block hygiene time so reactivated patients can book within two weeks.
- **Content (days 46–60):** rewrite your top three service pages, add Dentist schema and FAQs, and post two or three times a week.
- **Paid test (days 61–75):** run LSA or Search ads for one treatment with a capped budget, and track every lead to a booked appointment.
- **Measure (days 76–90):** compare the three KPIs below against day one, check competitor review velocity, and run an AI-search check for your top questions.

## The three KPIs that matter

Practices drown in dashboards. Track these three every month and you'll know whether marketing is working.

| KPI | Formula | Where the data comes from |
|---|---|---|
| New patients per month | First-visit patients in the month | Your PMS |
| Cost per new patient | Total marketing spend ÷ new patients | Invoices plus PMS |
| Recall rate | Active patients with a future hygiene appointment ÷ active patients | Your PMS |

Define "active patient" once (for example, seen in the last 18 months) and keep the definition stable so the trend means something. Ask new patients "How did you hear about us?" at intake and record it in the PMS, because Google Analytics can't see phone calls or walk-ins.

## Compliance quick reference

Dental marketing touches several sets of rules. None of this is legal advice, and rules vary by state, so check your state's current rules and talk to a healthcare attorney for anything specific.

- **HIPAA:** using PHI for marketing generally needs written patient authorization. Appointment reminders and recall notices are treatment communications and are allowed. See HHS's [guidance on marketing under HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html). Our [HIPAA compliant marketing guide](/blog/hipaa-compliant-marketing-guide) goes deeper.
- **Tracking pixels:** HHS issued guidance on online tracking technologies in December 2022 and updated it in March 2024. A federal court vacated part of it in June 2024, but tracking on patient portals and booking flows remains high risk. Read our [healthcare tracking pixels guide](/blog/healthcare-tracking-pixels-privacy).
- **State health data laws:** Washington's My Health My Data Act and Nevada's SB 370 cover consumer health data outside HIPAA. If you practice in Washington, see our [Seattle and King County marketing page](/local-marketing/king-county-wa).
- **Reviews:** the FTC review rule and Google's policies ban fake reviews, paid reviews, gating and suppression.
- **Texting:** marketing texts need prior express written consent under the TCPA, and business texting from local numbers needs 10DLC registration. See our [SMS marketing and TCPA guide](/blog/sms-marketing-tcpa-10dlc-guide).
- **Email:** CAN-SPAM requires honest headers, a physical address and a working unsubscribe.
- **Advertising claims:** your state dental board regulates claims like "specialist" and how results are presented.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best all-in-one AI marketing tool for dentists?

There isn't one tool that does everything well. Most practices pair a HIPAA-compliant patient platform such as Weave or NexHealth for reminders and texting with a marketing tool such as Growvia for SEO, rank tracking, AI-search visibility, competitor tracking, social and reporting.

### Can I reply to Google reviews from patients without violating HIPAA?

Yes, if you never confirm the reviewer is a patient and never mention treatment or visit details. Thank them, speak about general policies, and invite them to call the office. HHS has fined dental practices $10,000 and $23,000 for review replies that disclosed patient information.

### Do I need patient authorization to send recall reminders?

Generally no. HHS treats appointment reminders and recall notices as treatment communications, which don't need marketing authorization. Send them through a system covered by a business associate agreement, and get legal advice for anything that looks like promotion of a new service.

### Should my dental practice use Local Services Ads or Google Search ads?

Local Services Ads charge per lead and suit practices that want steady calls with little management. Search ads charge per click and suit specific treatments like implants or emergencies. Many practices start with LSA and add Search ads once their landing pages are strong.

### How do I measure whether dental marketing is working?

Track new patients per month, cost per new patient (marketing spend divided by new patients) and recall rate in your practice management system. Ask every new patient how they found you, because analytics tools can't see phone calls.

### How long does it take to rank higher on Google Maps?

It depends on competition, but foundation fixes like categories, consistent details and photos can help within weeks, while review growth and service pages build over several months. Track your Maps position for your top searches monthly so you can see the trend.
