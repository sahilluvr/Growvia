Nobody wakes up excited to hire a divorce lawyer or write a will. Family law clients are usually scared, angry or grieving. Estate planning clients know they should act, and put it off for years. Both groups take a long time to decide, and both are quietly judging whether you understand what they're going through.

That makes family law and estate planning marketing different from personal injury or criminal defense. Speed still matters when someone finally calls, but most of the work happens earlier: content that answers the questions people are afraid to ask, a tone that feels safe, email that stays in touch for months, and referral partners who send clients who already trust you.

This guide covers each of those, with templates and a simple plan. It sits under our pillar on [AI marketing for lawyers](/blog/ai-marketing-for-lawyers), which covers the whole system, from intake to AI search.

## How these clients decide

Family law and estate planning share a long, emotional decision cycle, but the triggers differ.

| | Family law (divorce, custody, support) | Estate planning (wills, trusts, probate) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical trigger | Separation, being served, a custody dispute, a move | New baby, marriage, diagnosis in the family, death of a parent, retirement, buying a home |
| Emotional state | Fear, anger, urgency, shame | Procrastination, discomfort with mortality, confusion |
| Research period | Weeks to months, often secretly | Months to years |
| Biggest worry | "Will I lose my kids or my house?" | "Is this expensive, and do I really need a trust?" |
| What wins trust | Calm explanations, clear process, privacy | Plain answers, clear pricing, a guide through the steps |
| Best channels | Local search, Maps, referrals, LSA | Seminars, referral partners, content, email |

::steps How a family law prospect usually moves (illustrative) | Months before — searches "how does custody work in my state" privately | Weeks before — reads a few firm websites and reviews | Trigger — separation or being served | Same day — calls two or three firms | Within a week — books a consult with the one that felt calm and clear

The lesson for family law: be findable and trustworthy long before the trigger, then answer fast when it happens. For estate planning, the trigger often never arrives on its own. Your marketing has to create the nudge.

## Tone: write like a calm professional, not an ad

Tone is a competitive advantage in these practice areas, because so much legal marketing sounds aggressive. "Bulldog divorce lawyer," "we fight for YOU," and photos of gavels push away the many clients who want a fair, quiet resolution.

### Tone guidelines that work

- **Lead with the reader's situation, not your accolades.** "If you've just been served with divorce papers, here's what happens in the next 30 days" beats "Award-winning family law firm."
- **Use plain words.** Say "child support," not "maintenance obligations," unless your state uses that term, and then explain it.
- **Name the fear and answer it.** "Many parents worry that moving out means losing custody. In most states, that's not automatic. Here's what courts actually look at."
- **Be careful with promises.** ABA Model Rule 7.1 bars misleading statements, and phrases like "we'll get you custody" or "avoid probate guaranteed" create expectations you can't promise. Your state's rules may also restrict words like "expert" or "specialist" unless you hold a recognized certification.
- **Respect privacy in your design.** Many divorce clients research on shared devices. Avoid aggressive retargeting ads that follow them around the web. A spouse might see them.

::compare Aggressive vs calm messaging for a divorce practice | Before: "Ruthless divorce attorneys"; "Don't let your ex take everything"; gavel stock photos; pop-up chat that won't close | After: "Divorce guidance for Denver families"; "What to expect in your first 30 days"; real photos of your team; a clear, quiet consult form

For the exact rules on claims, testimonials and labels in your state, see our [lawyer advertising rules by state](/blog/lawyer-advertising-rules-by-state) guide. Nothing here is legal advice, so check your state bar's current rules.

## Content that answers the questions people are afraid to ask

The best content for these practice areas answers the specific, sometimes embarrassing questions people type at midnight. It also tends to be what AI assistants quote when someone asks ChatGPT or Gemini for help.

### Family law question bank

- How is custody decided in [state]?
- Can I move out of state with my kids during a divorce?
- How long does a divorce take in [county]?
- What happens to the house in a divorce?
- Do I have to go to court for an uncontested divorce?
- How is child support calculated in [state]?
- Can I get divorced if my spouse won't sign?
- What should I not do before filing for divorce?

### Estate planning question bank

- Do I need a will or a trust?
- What happens if I die without a will in [state]?
- How much does an estate plan cost? (Answer honestly, with ranges or flat fees if you offer them.)
- Who should I name as guardian for my kids?
- What is probate and how long does it take in [state]?
- What is a power of attorney, and when do I need one?
- How often should I update my estate plan?
- What should I do in the first month after a parent dies?

### How to write each piece

1. **Answer in the first two sentences.** People want the short answer before the nuance.
2. **Name your state and cite the statute** or court resource where possible. State courts often publish self-help pages you can link.
3. **Explain the process step by step**, with realistic timelines and what the reader can do today.
4. **Say when they need a lawyer**, and when they might not (an honest "you may be able to do this yourself" builds trust).
5. **Add a soft next step**: a free guide, a seminar invite or a consult form, not a hard sell.
6. **Add an attorney byline and review date.** It shows experience and keeps content accountable.

Our [law firm SEO guide](/blog/law-firm-seo-guide) covers page structure, attorney bios and directories, and our [keyword research guide](/blog/keyword-research-for-small-business) shows how to find the questions people actually search.

> Key takeaway: in family law and estate planning, the firm that explains things most clearly before the client is ready usually gets the call when they are. Write for the 2 a.m. reader, not for other lawyers.

## Seminars and webinars

Seminars are one of the oldest estate planning marketing tools because they work: people who attend have chosen to learn, and they meet you in a low-pressure setting. They also tend to fit within bar rules, because attendees come to you. You still need to follow your state's advertising rules for the invitations and any materials, and avoid pressuring attendees one-on-one at the event.

### Formats that work

- **"Estate planning for new parents"** at a library or community center, or online at lunchtime.
- **"What to do when a parent dies"** for adult children, co-hosted with a local CPA.
- **"Planning for blended families"**, which overlaps family law and estate planning.
- **"Divorce and your finances"**, co-hosted with a financial planner (be careful to keep it educational).
- **Retirement community talks** on wills, powers of attorney and healthcare directives.

### A simple webinar funnel

::funnel Example: one estate planning webinar (illustrative) | Invites sent to list and partners: 800 | Registered: 90 | Attended: 45 | Booked a consult: 9 | Signed an engagement: 5

To run one:

1. Pick a narrow topic and a date about 3 to 4 weeks out.
2. Ask one or two referral partners to co-host and invite their clients.
3. Promote by email, Google Business Profile posts, social posts and your website.
4. Send reminders a week before, a day before and an hour before.
5. Teach for 30 to 40 minutes. No hard pitch. Offer a consult at the end.
6. Send the recording and a short follow-up sequence to everyone who registered, including no-shows.

## Email nurture for long decision cycles

Most estate planning prospects don't book on the first visit, and many family law consults don't retain right away. Email keeps you present without being pushy, as long as you follow [CAN-SPAM](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) (honest sender and subject lines, a physical postal address, and a working unsubscribe honored within 10 business days). Our [email marketing laws guide](/blog/email-marketing-laws-by-country) has the details.

### An estate planning nurture sequence

| Send | Subject idea | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Your estate planning checklist | The guide they requested, plus one sentence about who you are |
| Day 3 | Will or trust? A plain-English answer | A short explainer and when each makes sense |
| Day 10 | The 3 documents every parent should have | Will, guardianship, powers of attorney |
| Day 20 | What a planning meeting with us looks like | Process, timeline, flat-fee options |
| Day 35 | A question we hear every week | One FAQ answered |
| Monthly after | Short, useful update | Law changes (checked), stories without client details, seminar invites |

### A family law follow-up after a consult

Keep it short, warm and discreet. Confirm the email address belongs to the client alone before you send anything. Many family law clients share accounts with a spouse, so ask which address and phone number are safe to use, and note it in your intake records.

## Referral partners

For estate planning in particular, referral partners are often the biggest source of good clients. For family law, therapists, mediators and financial professionals see people at the start of a separation.

### Who to build relationships with

- **CPAs and tax preparers.** They see clients' finances every year and know who needs a trust.
- **Financial planners and wealth advisors.** Their clients need beneficiary planning and trusts.
- **Therapists and family counselors.** They work with couples and parents in transition. Respect their confidentiality obligations; they can't share client details with you.
- **Mediators** (for family law firms that litigate, and vice versa).
- **Realtors and mortgage brokers.** Buying a home and divorcing both raise property questions.
- **Funeral homes and senior living communities**, for probate and elder-law-adjacent work.
- **Other lawyers** who don't handle your practice area.

### Stay within the rules

ABA Model Rule 7.2(b) generally bars giving anything of value to someone for recommending your services. Rule 7.2(b)(4) allows reciprocal referral agreements with other lawyers or non-lawyer professionals, but only if the agreement isn't exclusive and the client is informed. Fee-sharing with non-lawyers is barred by Rule 5.4. Your state's versions may differ, so check them, and read the [ABA Model Rules](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/) alongside your state bar's rules.

What you can do freely: be useful. Send partners a short quarterly update on law changes that affect their clients, co-host educational events, and refer clients to them when it genuinely helps.

::check Referral partner habits that work | Keep a list of 15 to 25 partners with notes | Meet one partner for coffee every week | Send a useful quarterly email for professionals | Co-host one educational event per quarter | Thank partners for referrals (without paying for them) | Refer clients back when it genuinely fits

## Reviews in sensitive practice areas

Reviews matter for Maps ranking and trust, but family law and estate planning clients are often reluctant to post publicly. A divorce client may not want their name next to "my divorce lawyer." That's normal. Ask anyway, gently, and make it easy.

- **Timing.** Estate planning: right after the signing meeting, when they feel relief. Family law: after the matter resolves, not during a hard stretch.
- **Wording.** "If you felt we helped, a short Google review helps other families find us. You can use just your first name or initials."
- **No incentives, no gating.** The FTC's Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, effective October 21, 2024) bans buying reviews and suppressing negative ones, and Google bans asking only happy clients. See the [FTC's guidance on the rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers).
- **Replies protect confidentiality.** Never confirm details of a matter in a reply. ABA Formal Opinion 496 addresses how lawyers may respond to online criticism without revealing client information.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best marketing channel for estate planning attorneys?

For many firms, referral partners (CPAs, financial planners) and educational seminars bring the best-fit clients, supported by clear website content and email follow-up. Local search matters too, especially for probate questions after a death.

### How should a divorce lawyer advertise without sounding aggressive?

Lead with the reader's situation and plain answers, use real team photos, and avoid promises about outcomes. A calm, professional tone appeals to the many clients who want a fair resolution, and it stays clear of misleading-claims rules.

### Can estate planning attorneys pay referral partners?

Generally no. ABA Model Rule 7.2(b) bars giving anything of value for a recommendation, though non-exclusive reciprocal referral arrangements with disclosure to the client are allowed. Check your state's version of the rule.

### How long should an email nurture sequence be for estate planning leads?

Five or six emails over the first month, then a useful monthly email, works well for most firms. Many estate planning prospects take months to act, so steady, helpful contact matters more than frequency.

### How do I get reviews from family law clients who value privacy?

Ask gently after the matter resolves, mention that first names or initials are fine, and never offer incentives. Accept that some clients will decline, and never reveal case details in your replies.
