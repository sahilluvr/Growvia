Most plumbing companies live on the phone. A pipe bursts, someone searches, the first company that answers gets the job. That work is valuable, but it's lumpy. A wet week fills the board; a dry week leaves trucks parked and technicians on the clock with nothing to do.

The plumbers who stop riding that rollercoaster usually have one thing in common: a handful of **business customers who send work every month**. A property manager with 400 units has leaking faucets, running toilets and water heaters failing somewhere every week. A home builder needs rough-in and finish plumbing on every house. A real estate office needs pre-sale repairs done fast. One of those relationships can be worth more than a hundred one-off emergency calls.

This guide shows how to find those businesses, what to say to them and how to turn a first job into a standing arrangement. It complements our [plumber marketing guide](/blog/plumber-marketing-guide), which covers emergency searches, Local Services Ads and reviews.


## Why business accounts change the economics of a plumbing company

A homeowner with a burst pipe is a valuable job, but it's usually one job. The same family might not need a plumber again for years, and when they do, they may search again and call whoever answers first. A property manager is different. Every unit in their portfolio has toilets, faucets, supply lines, water heaters and drains, and something fails somewhere almost every week. When you become the plumber they call, you're not winning a job; you're winning a share of every plumbing problem across hundreds of homes.

Business accounts also smooth out the calendar. Tenant turnovers, planned water heater replacements and builder schedules can be booked into quiet days, so technicians stay productive when emergency calls slow down. And because a business buyer pays against invoices and remembers good service, you spend less on marketing per job over time. The upfront effort of building relationships is real, but it compounds in a way that paid emergency leads rarely do.

There is one more benefit owners often overlook: business accounts make your company easier to sell or borrow against. A lender or buyer looking at your books sees recurring customers with a history of repeat work, which is far more reassuring than a revenue line that depends entirely on this month's ad spend.

## Who sends plumbers repeat work

Not every business is worth chasing. The best partners have frequent plumbing needs, little patience for unreliable trades and the authority to pick a vendor.

| Partner | Why they need a plumber | What wins them |
|---|---|---|
| Property managers | Constant tenant repair requests across many units | Fast response, clear pricing, photos and invoices they can forward to owners |
| Home builders and remodelers | Rough-in and finish work on a schedule | Showing up on the day you promised, clean inspections |
| Real estate agencies | Inspection repairs before closing, often on a deadline | Same-week availability and a written quote buyers trust |
| Restaurants and cafés | Grease traps, drains, water heaters under heavy use | After-hours service so the kitchen never stops |
| Apartment and condo associations | Shared lines, backflow testing, common areas | Planned maintenance and one point of contact |
| Schools, gyms and offices | Restrooms that get heavy use | Preventive maintenance contracts |

Property managers are usually the best place to start. They're easy to find, they buy every month and they hate switching vendors once they find one that works.

## How to find them in your area

You don't need to buy a list. Every business above has a Google Business Profile, and most have a website with a contact email.

- **Search Google Maps** for "property management", "home builder", "real estate agency" and "restaurant" in each town you serve. Note the busiest ones: more reviews usually means more units or more deals.
- **Check the website.** Property managers often list how many doors they manage and which neighborhoods. That tells you the size of the prize.
- **Look for the right person.** In a small firm it's the owner. In a larger one, look for a maintenance coordinator or operations manager.
- **Use local associations.** Landlord associations, builder associations and chamber of commerce member directories list active businesses that are already networking.

## What to say in the first email

Business buyers get pitched all the time. The emails that get replies are short, specific and easy to say yes to. Lead with the problem you solve for them, not with your company history.

> **Subject:** Plumbing for your Elm Street properties
>
> Hi Dana — I run Rivera Plumbing, 12 minutes from most of your listings. Property managers we work with send us tenant repairs by text and get a photo, invoice and same-day fix in most cases. Would it help to have a backup plumber on file for your next leak or water heater call? Happy to do the first job at our standard rate so you can see how we work.

Notice what's in it: the distance (speed), the process they care about (photos and invoices), a low-risk ask (a backup vendor, not a contract) and an easy first step.

::steps A simple 3-email sequence | Day 1: short intro with one clear offer | Day 4: a useful tip (e.g. a water heater age checklist for their units) | Day 10: a final note — "should I close your file?"

Stop the sequence the moment someone replies. If they say "not now", ask whether you can check back in three months and set a reminder.

## Turning the first job into a contract

The first job is an audition. Make it easy to hire you again:

1. **Confirm in writing** when you'll arrive and send a text when you're on the way.
2. **Photograph the problem and the fix.** Property managers forward these to owners; it makes them look good.
3. **Invoice the same day** with the unit address and a clear description.
4. **Ask what else is coming up**: water heaters near the end of their life, units turning over, annual backflow tests.
5. **Offer a simple agreement** after two or three jobs: a priority response window and fixed rates for common jobs.

Forty businesses may sound small. But if four become regular partners, each sending a few jobs a month, you've built a base that keeps trucks busy in slow weeks.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Pitching homeowners with cold email.** Stick to businesses. Consumer outreach without consent creates legal and reputation problems.
- **Sending one long email to everyone.** Mention their area, their type of property or something from their website.
- **Discounting to win the first job.** Compete on speed and reliability; discounts attract buyers who leave for the next discount.
- **Going silent after the job.** A short check-in every few weeks keeps you top of mind for the next call.

## Qualifying a prospect before you write

Five minutes of research per prospect makes your first email far more effective, and saves you chasing companies that will never hire you.

::check Signs a prospect is worth pursuing | Manages or builds within your service area | Has enough volume to send repeat work | No in-house plumber listed on their team page | Website shows recent activity (new listings, projects or posts) | Reviews mention maintenance or repair response, good or bad

Read the property manager's website for the number of doors and the neighborhoods they cover. Check whether they list a "maintenance request" page; that tells you how tenants report problems and which part of the process you'd plug into. For builders, look at the projects gallery: how many homes are under construction now and where. For real estate offices, note the agents with the most listings; they're the ones who see the most inspection reports.

## Three more emails for your sequence

The first email opens the door. These three keep the conversation going without being pushy. Adjust the details so they're true for your company.

> **Follow-up (day 4) — Subject:** Water heaters in older rentals
>
> Hi Dana — one quick idea. Rentals built in the same few years often have water heaters reaching the end of their life at the same time. If it would help, we can look at the ages on a handful of your units and give you a simple replacement plan, so you budget for them instead of paying emergency rates. Happy to do this at no charge.

> **Value-add (day 10) — Subject:** A one-page tenant guide
>
> Hi Dana — we put together a one-page guide for tenants on what to do in the first five minutes of a leak: where the shut-off valve is, what to photograph and who to call. Several managers send it with their move-in packs. Want a copy with your logo on it?

> **Last note (day 18) — Subject:** Should I close your file?
>
> Hi Dana — I don't want to crowd your inbox. If a backup plumber isn't useful right now, no problem at all. If it is, reply with a good time and I'll call. Either way, the tenant guide is yours to keep.

Each email offers something useful even if they never hire you. That's what makes follow-ups feel helpful rather than annoying.

## Your first conversation with a property manager

When a manager agrees to talk, use the call to understand their process, not to pitch. Good questions to ask:

1. How do tenants report maintenance problems today, and who decides which vendor goes out?
2. What happens after hours and on weekends?
3. What do owners expect to see on an invoice before they'll approve payment?
4. Which problems come up most often across your properties?
5. What has frustrated you about plumbers you've used before?
6. Is there a spending limit you can approve without asking the owner?

Listen for the frustrations. If they say "nobody sends photos" or "invoices come weeks later", those become your promises. Bring a sample invoice, a photo report from a past job (with addresses removed) and your insurance certificate, because many managers need it on file before they can dispatch you.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We already have a plumber." | They're not unhappy enough to switch. | "Totally fair. Many managers keep a second plumber for overflow and after-hours calls. Could we be your backup?" |
| "Your rates are higher." | They're comparing hourly rates, not total cost. | Show how faster diagnosis, fewer return trips and clear invoices lower the total cost per repair. |
| "Send me your information." | Polite brush-off or genuine interest. | Send a one-page summary and ask one specific question in the same email. |
| "Our owners pick the vendors." | The manager recommends; owners approve. | Offer materials the manager can forward: insurance, licenses, sample reports. |
| "We only use vendors on our platform." | They dispatch through a vendor portal. | Ask how to get onboarded and complete it promptly. |

## Getting your company ready before outreach

Property managers and builders look you up before they answer. Spend an afternoon making sure what they find backs up your email. Your Google Business Profile should list commercial and property-management services, show recent job photos and include replies to every review. Your website needs a short page for property managers describing response times, how tenants or managers request service, what your invoices include and the areas you cover. Keep a current certificate of insurance and your license details ready to attach, and prepare a sample photo report with addresses removed. Finally, make sure one phone number and one email are answered by a person during business hours, because nothing ends a new relationship faster than a first call that goes to voicemail.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do plumbers get commercial clients?

Start with businesses that need plumbing every month: property managers, builders, restaurants and associations. Find them on Google Maps, email the decision-maker with a short, specific offer and treat the first job as an audition for a longer arrangement.

### Is it worth working with property managers?

Usually yes. They send steady repair work across many units and rarely switch vendors once they're happy. Expect them to want fast response, clear pricing and good paperwork.

### Should plumbers buy lead lists?

You rarely need to. Local businesses are listed on Google Maps with their websites, and most show a contact email. A short list of well-chosen local partners beats a big purchased list.

### How many follow-up emails should I send?

Two or three spaced over about ten days is enough. Stop as soon as someone replies or asks you not to email again.

### What's the best type of business account for a plumber?

Property managers are often the best start because they send repair work across many units all year. Builders and remodelers are strong too, especially in busy building seasons.

### Do property managers need my insurance certificate?

Usually yes. Many can't dispatch a vendor until a current certificate of insurance and license details are on file, so have them ready.

### How long does it take to land a regular account?

Expect several weeks from first email to first job, and a few jobs before a manager treats you as a regular vendor. Consistent follow-up and flawless first jobs speed it up.

### Should plumbers offer maintenance plans to property managers?

Yes. Scheduled checks on water heaters, drains and backflow devices help managers avoid emergencies and give you planned work.
