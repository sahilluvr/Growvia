Business owners don't usually search "business lawyer" until something is urgent: a contract dispute, a partner leaving, an acquisition offer. Long before that moment, they talk to their accountant, their banker and their financial advisor. Those advisors are asked "do you know a good lawyer?" constantly.

That makes referral partnerships the backbone of most successful small-business law practices. This guide shows which partners to build, how to approach them and how to give as much as you get. For marketing basics, see [law firm SEO](/blog/law-firm-seo-guide) and [AI marketing for lawyers](/blog/ai-marketing-for-lawyers).


## Why referral partners drive business law practices

Business owners rarely shop for a lawyer the way they shop for software. They don't compare ten options online. They ask someone they already trust, and the person they trust most with business decisions is usually their accountant. Bankers, financial advisors and business brokers follow close behind. These advisors see legal needs before owners do: a partnership drifting toward conflict, an acquisition opportunity, a lease renewal with unfamiliar terms, a key employee leaving.

When an advisor introduces you, the owner arrives already inclined to trust you. That shortens the sales cycle and improves the relationship from the start. Referred clients also tend to bring higher-quality matters, because advisors refer when there's real substance, not idle curiosity.

Referral partnerships compound. An accountant with a hundred business clients may refer several matters a year. As you serve those clients well, the accountant refers more, and other advisors hear about you through local networks, chambers and study groups. Over time, a handful of strong relationships can supply most of a practice's new business.

There's also a defensive benefit. When competitors increase their advertising, your referral partners keep sending clients based on trust rather than ad spend, making your practice less exposed to rising marketing costs.

## The first approach

Advisors want to know you'll make them look good and won't step on their relationship.

> **Subject:** A lawyer your clients can call
>
> Hi Daniel — I'm a business attorney in Nashville working with owner-run companies on contracts, partnerships and sales of businesses. When CPAs refer a client, I keep them in the loop and stay in my lane on tax questions. Would a 20-minute coffee be useful to compare how we each help owners?

"Stay in my lane on tax" signals respect. CPAs worry that lawyers will undercut their advice.

## Give before you get

The best referral relationships are two-way. Refer clients to your partners when they need accounting, banking or financial planning. Share useful updates they can pass to their clients, like contract checklists or a summary of new state filing requirements.

## Workshops that bring clients

Co-host short sessions with a CPA or banker: "Selling your business in the next three years", "Partnership agreements that prevent disputes", "What to check before signing a commercial lease". Each partner invites their clients; everyone benefits.

## Your first meeting with a CPA

1. What legal issues come up most often with your business clients?
2. Who do you refer to today, and how is that working?
3. How do you like to be involved when a client has a legal matter?
4. What has gone wrong in past referrals?
5. Which industries do most of your clients work in?
6. Would a joint workshop for your clients be useful?

Bring a one-page practice summary listing the matters you handle, the industries you know and how you coordinate with advisors. Show respect for their role, and be clear about where your work ends and theirs begins.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We refer to someone already." | Existing relationship. | Offer to be an option for matters outside that lawyer's focus or when they're busy. |
| "Lawyers are too expensive for my clients." | Fee concerns. | Explain your fee options, such as fixed fees for common matters. |
| "We worry about losing control of the client." | Fear of being sidelined. | Commit to coordination and keep them informed with client consent. |
| "We don't do referral arrangements." | Firm policy. | Emphasize that you're offering a trusted resource, not a formal arrangement. |
| "Send me your information." | Mild interest. | Send your summary and propose one specific workshop topic. |

## Preparing your practice

Advisors will check your background before referring. Make sure your website has clear practice area pages, attorney bios with relevant experience and information on how you work with advisors. Consider fixed fees for common matters such as formations, contract reviews and lease reviews, which make referrals easier. Prepare a one-page practice summary, a sample engagement letter and a few client-friendly checklists. Respond to referred clients quickly, because referrers judge you on speed.

## Workshop formats that bring clients

**Selling your business.** Co-hosted with a CPA or broker: legal and financial preparation for a sale.

**Partnership agreements.** Preventing disputes with clear terms on ownership, decision-making and exits.

**Commercial leases.** Co-hosted with a commercial real estate broker: what to check before signing.

**Employment basics.** Co-hosted with a payroll or HR provider: contracts, handbooks and common pitfalls.

**Starting a business.** For incubators and coworking spaces: entity choice, founder agreements and contracts.

Keep workshops educational and practical. Owners attend for answers, not pitches, and the ones with real matters will follow up.

## A worked example

Here's how a CPA relationship might develop. The details are illustrative.

You email a small accounting firm that serves restaurants and contractors, offering a sale-readiness checklist. The managing partner replies to your follow-up and meets you for coffee. You discover that several of her clients are thinking about selling in the next few years, and she doesn't have a lawyer she refers to consistently.

She refers a restaurant owner who wants to buy out a partner. You coordinate with her on the financial side, handle the agreement and keep her informed with the client's consent. The buyout closes smoothly. Two months later, you co-host a workshop for her clients on preparing to sell, and three owners book consultations.

Within a year, the firm refers matters regularly, and you refer clients to her when they need accounting help. Her colleagues at a local CPA association hear about the arrangement, and two more firms reach out.

## Keeping partner relationships strong

Check in with each partner every quarter. Share brief, useful updates, such as changes in local business filing requirements, that they can pass on. Refer clients back to partners when appropriate. Thank partners for referrals, without anything that could look like payment. Keep a simple record of referrals in both directions, so you can see which relationships are working and which need attention.

## Delivering referred work so partners keep referring

Every referred client is also a test of your relationship with the referrer. Respond within a business day, explain your process and fees clearly and confirm scope in an engagement letter. Coordinate with the referring advisor where appropriate, with the client's consent, especially on matters that affect tax or finances. Keep the client informed with regular updates and avoid surprises on fees.

When the matter closes, thank the client and, with their consent, let the referrer know it went well. Ask the client for feedback. Advisors judge lawyers by what their clients say afterwards, so a smooth, responsive experience leads directly to more referrals.

## Offering fixed-fee packages

Fixed-fee packages make it easier for advisors to refer and for owners to say yes. Consider packages for common matters such as entity formation, operating agreements, contract templates, commercial lease reviews and annual compliance checkups. Describe exactly what's included and what's not. Owners appreciate predictable costs, and advisors appreciate being able to tell clients "it'll cost about this much" with confidence.

Fixed fees also help you manage your practice. Standardized matters become more efficient over time, and recurring packages, such as annual reviews, create predictable revenue.

## Annual legal checkups for business owners

Offer business clients an annual legal checkup: a review of their entity records, key contracts, employment documents, leases and any upcoming changes such as new partners, locations or sales. Coordinate the timing with their accountant's year-end planning where possible. Checkups catch problems early, deepen relationships and often lead to new matters. They also give advisors a natural reason to introduce you to clients.

## Working with startups and incubators

Startups need help with formation, founder agreements, intellectual property assignments, contracts and fundraising documents. Incubators and coworking spaces host many founders and often look for professionals to run workshops or office hours. Offer short sessions on common legal pitfalls for early-stage companies. Consider fixed-fee startup packages. Founders who start with you often stay as they grow, bringing larger matters over time.

## Handling conflicts and referrals out

Sometimes a referred matter isn't a good fit, or a conflict prevents you from taking it. Respond promptly, explain kindly and suggest another attorney where appropriate. Referrers appreciate lawyers who handle these situations gracefully, and they keep referring because they trust your judgment.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do business lawyers get clients?

Mostly through referrals from advisors who serve business owners: CPAs, bankers, financial advisors and business brokers. Build those relationships deliberately and co-host useful workshops.

### Can lawyers have referral agreements with accountants?

Rules vary by state. Paying non-lawyers for referrals is generally prohibited, and reciprocal arrangements may have conditions. Check your bar's rules.

### What workshops attract business owners?

Practical topics like selling a business, partnership agreements, commercial leases and succession planning.

### How should I follow up with referral partners?

Keep them updated (with client consent), refer clients back when appropriate and check in quarterly.

### Should business lawyers offer fixed fees?

Fixed fees for common matters such as formations, contract reviews and lease reviews make it easier for advisors to refer and for owners to say yes.

### How often should I meet with referral partners?

A short check-in every quarter keeps relationships active, with workshops once or twice a year.

### Can I co-host workshops with accountants?

Yes, joint educational workshops are common. Follow your bar's advertising rules and keep the content educational.

### What should a lawyer's practice summary include?

The matters you handle, industries you know, how you coordinate with advisors, fee options and contact details.

### What should be included in a business legal checkup?

A review of entity records, key contracts, employment documents, leases and upcoming changes like new partners or sales.

### How do business lawyers coordinate with CPAs?

By sharing information with client consent, agreeing on who handles which issues and communicating before decisions are made.

### What matters are good for fixed fees?

Entity formation, operating agreements, contract templates, lease reviews and annual checkups.

### How quickly should lawyers respond to referred clients?

Within one business day if possible. Speed reflects on the referrer.

### Can business lawyers host events with banks?

Yes, educational events are common. Follow your bar's advertising rules and any bank compliance requirements.

### How do I know which referral partners are most valuable?

Track referrals from each partner and how those matters turned out.

### What legal help do startups need first?

Usually formation, founder agreements, IP assignments and basic contracts.

### Should lawyers offer office hours at incubators?

Educational office hours can help founders and build relationships, following bar advertising rules.

### What should I do if I can't take a referred matter?

Respond promptly, explain and suggest another attorney if appropriate.

### How do business lawyers stay in touch with past clients?

With an annual checkup offer and occasional short updates on changes that affect owner-run businesses, following bar rules.

### What should a business lawyer bring to a first CPA meeting?

A one-page practice summary, examples of matters handled, fee options and ideas for a joint workshop.

### Do referral partners expect updates on referred clients?

Many appreciate a short note that the matter went well, shared only with the client's consent.

### What do CPAs want from a business lawyer?

Responsiveness, clear fees, respect for their role and coordination before decisions are made.
