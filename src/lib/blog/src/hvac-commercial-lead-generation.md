Residential HVAC is a seasonal business. The first heatwave and the first cold snap flood the phones; spring and autumn go quiet. Commercial maintenance agreements are how many HVAC companies flatten that curve. A restaurant, a dental office or a small office building needs its equipment checked on a schedule, and the visits can be booked in exactly the months your residential calendar is empty.

This guide covers which businesses to target, how to find them and how to pitch a planned maintenance agreement that's easy to say yes to. For the residential side, see our [seasonal HVAC marketing plan](/blog/hvac-marketing-seasonal-plan).


## Why commercial maintenance is worth building

Residential HVAC demand follows the weather, and the weather doesn't care about your payroll. Commercial maintenance gives you work you control: visits booked months ahead, scheduled when technicians would otherwise be waiting for the phone. It also puts you first in line when equipment fails or needs replacing, because the business already trusts the company that has been on its roof every quarter.

There's a practical benefit for your team too. Planned visits are easier to staff, easier to route and less stressful than emergency calls in a heatwave. Technicians can develop a relationship with each site, learn its quirks and spot problems early. Over time, a portfolio of maintenance agreements also makes your business more valuable, because recurring contracts are worth more to lenders and buyers than unpredictable one-off jobs.

Commercial accounts also widen what your company can sell. A restaurant that trusts you with its rooftop units may ask about kitchen exhaust coordination, a dental office may ask about air quality, and an office manager may ask for quotes when the building's equipment reaches replacement age. Each of those conversations starts from a relationship you've already built on scheduled visits.

## How to build your target list

Pick one or two business types and a tight service radius. Commercial customers care about response time, so a list of 50 businesses within 20 minutes beats 500 across the state.

- Search Google Maps for each business type in the neighborhoods you already drive through.
- Prefer multi-location groups and property managers: one yes can cover several sites.
- Read their websites for hours of operation; it tells you when maintenance visits won't disrupt them.
- Note who signs off. For a restaurant it's the owner or general manager; for an office building it's the property or facilities manager.

## Pitch a maintenance plan, not a sales call

Business owners don't want to think about HVAC. Your job is to make it one decision, once a year.

> **Subject:** Keeping your dining room cool this summer
>
> Hi Sam — we service rooftop units for several restaurants near you. Most breakdowns we see in July start as a dirty coil or a worn belt in spring. We offer a four-visit maintenance plan scheduled before you open, with a priority line if anything fails during service. Would a quick look at your units next week be useful? No charge for the first inspection.

The free first inspection lowers the risk for them and gives you a reason to be on the roof, where you can see the equipment's age and condition.

::flow From inspection to agreement | Free inspection booked | Written report with photos | Simple 3-tier plan offered | Visits scheduled in your slow months | Renewal reminder 30 days before the anniversary

## Pricing that sells itself

Keep the choice simple. Three options work well:

| Plan | Visits | Extras |
|---|---|---|
| Essential | 2 per year | Filter changes, safety checks |
| Priority | 4 per year | Priority response, discounted repairs |
| Complete | 4 per year | Priority response, covered parts up to a limit |

Price per unit, not per building, so a customer with three rooftop units understands the cost. Put the schedule in writing and book the visits in spring and autumn when your residential calendar is lighter.

## Following up the right way

Commercial buyers are busy, not uninterested. A short sequence works:

After a "not now", note the reason. "We have a contract until March" is a lead with a date on it.

## Your first site visit

The free inspection is your sales meeting. Use it well:

1. Ask the owner or manager about recent problems, comfort complaints and any breakdowns in the past year.
2. Inspect each unit and photograph model plates, filters, coils, belts and anything worn.
3. Note the age of each unit; equipment nearing the end of its expected life deserves a frank conversation.
4. Explain what you found in plain language before you leave.
5. Send a written report with photos within a day, ranked by urgency.
6. Include a simple maintenance proposal with the visit schedule already filled in.

The report itself builds trust. Even if they don't sign immediately, they now have documentation from your company, and when something fails, your name is on it.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "Our units are fine." | Nothing has broken recently. | "That's great. Maintenance is about keeping it that way. The inspection is free, and you'll get a written baseline." |
| "Our landlord handles HVAC." | Lease terms decide who pays. | Ask who to contact at the property management company and offer the same inspection. |
| "It's too expensive." | They're comparing to doing nothing. | Show the plan cost per month per unit, compared with typical emergency call-out fees. |
| "We already have someone." | They may have a break-fix vendor, not a plan. | Ask whether they get scheduled visits and written reports. Offer to be the plan provider. |
| "Call us when it's hot." | They think in crises. | Explain that your schedule fills in summer and that maintenance slots are booked ahead. |

## Choosing the right niche for your company

Not every commercial niche suits every HVAC company. Restaurants want after-hours visits and fast emergency response, which suits companies with flexible technicians and an on-call rotation. Medical and dental offices value careful scheduling, cleanliness and documentation, which suits teams comfortable working around patients. Office buildings and property managers want predictable visits, written reports and consolidated billing, which suits companies with good office systems. Gyms and studios care about humidity and ventilation as much as temperature, which suits technicians strong in air quality.

Pick the niche that matches your team's strengths and your service area. Then shape every part of your outreach around it: the examples in your emails, the checklist you send, the photos on your website and the plan you propose. A focused company that says "we maintain rooftop units for restaurants in the north side" is easier to remember and easier to trust than one that says "we do commercial HVAC".

Once the first niche is running well, add a second that shares routes or skills, such as medical offices alongside dental practices, or property managers alongside office buildings.

## Handling equipment replacement conversations

During maintenance visits, you'll see equipment nearing the end of its life. Raise it early and calmly: explain the unit's age, condition and the risks of waiting, and offer to prepare a replacement estimate the client can budget for. Avoid pressure. Many businesses prefer to plan replacements in quieter months rather than face an emergency in a heatwave. Clients who trust your maintenance recommendations usually choose you for the replacement too.

## Get your company ready before you reach out

Commercial buyers check you out before they reply. Make sure what they find supports your email:

- **Google Business Profile**: list commercial maintenance as a service, add photos of rooftop work and reply to reviews.
- **A commercial services page** on your website describing maintenance plans, response times and the areas you cover.
- **Insurance and licensing documents** ready to send, because property managers and larger businesses often require them before a first visit.
- **A sample inspection report** with photos and plain-language findings, so prospects can see what they'll receive.
- **One phone number and email** that a real person answers during business hours.

These basics turn a cold email into a credible introduction. When the restaurant owner looks you up and sees rooftop photos, recent reviews and a clear plan page, the free inspection feels like a safe yes.

## A worked example

Here's how a typical first restaurant account might come together. The details are illustrative, but the sequence is common.

A two-location restaurant group receives your email offering a free inspection before summer. The owner doesn't reply. Five days later, your follow-up about dirty condenser coils lands on a morning when the kitchen felt unusually hot, and the general manager forwards it to the owner with a note: "Should we?" The owner books an inspection for a Tuesday before lunch service.

On the roof, your technician photographs a clogged coil, a worn belt and a filter that hasn't been changed in a long time. You send a short report that afternoon, with three photos and a recommendation ranked by urgency, and attach a Priority plan covering both locations with visits scheduled for April, July, October and January.

The owner signs for the first location only. In July, during the first heatwave, the second location's unit fails. They call you because your sticker is on the first location's equipment and your report is in their inbox. You fix it the same day, and the second location joins the plan. That pattern, starting small and expanding after you prove yourself, is how many commercial portfolios grow.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do HVAC companies get commercial accounts?

Target businesses where downtime is expensive, such as restaurants, offices and medical practices. Offer a free inspection, send a written report and propose a simple maintenance plan with priority response.

### What should an HVAC maintenance agreement include?

A visit schedule, the tasks done at each visit, response times for breakdowns, any repair discounts or covered parts, and the renewal terms. Keep it to one page.

### When should HVAC companies market commercial plans?

Late winter and late summer, so the visits land in spring and autumn. That's when businesses think ahead and when your technicians have time.

### Is cold email effective for HVAC companies?

It can be when it's short, local and offers something useful, such as a free inspection. Send to businesses only, keep a small radius and follow up once or twice.

### How much should HVAC maintenance plans cost?

It depends on your market, the number of units and what's included. Price per unit per visit or per year, keep three simple tiers and make sure your price covers travel and time on the roof.

### Should I offer free inspections to win commercial clients?

Many companies do because it lowers the risk for the business and gives you a written report to anchor a proposal. Keep it short and focused.

### What's the best time to sign commercial maintenance agreements?

Late winter and late summer, so the visits land before the peak seasons and in your quieter months.

### When should businesses replace HVAC units?

It depends on age, condition, efficiency and repair history. A maintenance provider can help plan replacements before failures.

### Do maintenance plans include replacement discounts?

Some do. Clearly state any benefits for plan members in your agreement.

### What should a commercial HVAC report include?

Unit details, photos, findings ranked by urgency, recommended repairs and the next scheduled visit.

### How do HVAC companies handle multi-site clients?

With one schedule, consistent reports for every site and a single contact for the client.

### How long does a commercial HVAC inspection take?

It depends on the number and type of units, but a small business with a few rooftop units can often be inspected in under an hour. Schedule it outside busy hours so the business isn't disrupted and the owner or manager can walk through the findings with you.
