Opening a second location feels like doubling the business. Marketing it often feels like tripling the work. Suddenly you have two Google Business Profiles, two sets of reviews, two phone numbers, a website that only really talks about the first office, and a spreadsheet nobody updates.

Multi-location marketing goes wrong in predictable ways. One location gets all the attention while the others drift. Profiles get duplicated or suspended. Location pages are copies of each other with the city name swapped, so Google ignores them. And the owner can't tell which site is growing and which is quietly losing ground to a local competitor.

This playbook is for dental groups, clinics, home service companies, salons, gyms and franchisees running anywhere from two to fifty locations. It covers how to set up each location so it can rank on its own, how to keep brand standards without making every page identical, and how to report on all of it in one view.

## The core idea: each location competes on its own

Google ranks locations, not brands. When someone in Katy searches "dentist near me," your Katy office competes with the dentists near Katy. Your downtown Houston office's 400 reviews don't help much there.

That changes how you plan. Brand-level work (your website, your name, your ads strategy) supports every location. But each location also needs its own profile, page, reviews and local signals.

::flow What each location needs to rank on its own | Its own verified Google Business Profile | Its own location page on your site | Its own steady flow of reviews | Its own local links and citations | Its own report card

## Google Business Profile for every location

### Who qualifies for a profile

Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) allow a profile for each location that serves customers in person during its stated hours. A storefront office with staff qualifies. A virtual office, a mailbox or an unstaffed storage unit does not. Service-area businesses, like plumbers who drive to customers, can hide their address and list service areas instead, but each profile still needs a real base of operations.

Breaking these rules is one of the fastest ways to lose a profile. If you're tempted to create a "location" in a town where you have no office, build a service area page on your website instead.

### Naming and categories

Use the same real business name on every profile, exactly as it appears on your signage. Don't add keywords or city names unless they're actually part of the location's name. "Bright Smiles Dental" is fine. "Bright Smiles Dental Best Emergency Dentist Katy" breaks the guidelines and invites suspension.

Pick the primary category that fits what that location mostly does. Locations can differ. One clinic may offer physical therapy and chiropractic, another only chiropractic. Categories should match reality at each site.

### Verification at scale

If you run 10 or more storefront locations of the same business, you may qualify for bulk verification. According to [Google's bulk verification help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4490296), you must list every location you manage, and service-area businesses aren't eligible. Smaller groups verify each location individually.

::stats Google Business Profile at scale (from Google's help pages) | 10+ | profiles needed to request bulk verification | 1 | profile per real, staffed location | 0 | profiles allowed for virtual offices or mailboxes

### Ownership and access

Keep the profiles in a business account your company controls, not a former employee's or agency's personal Gmail. Give each location manager manager-level access to their own profile only. Keep owner access with two people at headquarters, so one departure never locks you out.

### What to keep consistent, what to localize

| Element | Keep consistent across locations | Localize per location |
|---|---|---|
| Business name | Exact brand name | Only if the real name differs |
| Categories | Core primary category | Secondary categories by actual services |
| Hours | Format | Real hours, plus holiday hours |
| Phone | Format and call routing rules | A local number for each site |
| Photos | Brand style and logo | Real photos of that office, team and work |
| Description | Brand promise and tone | Neighborhood, parking, nearby landmarks, local services |
| Posts | Brand campaigns | Local events, staff, offers |
| Q&A and reviews | Reply standards | Replies signed by the local manager |

## Location pages that are actually different

Every Google Business Profile should link to its own page on your website, not your homepage. That page is where Google and AI assistants learn what this specific location offers, and where a searcher decides to call.

The common mistake is a template with the city name swapped. Google calls this kind of thing doorway content, and it rarely ranks. A useful location page answers what a person choosing that office actually wants to know.

::compare A thin location page vs a useful one | Thin: city name swapped into a template; generic stock photo; same paragraph as every other page | Useful: real photos of the office and team; parking, transit and landmark directions; services offered at this site; local reviews and the manager's name

### What to put on each location page

- The location's name, address and local phone number, matching its Google Business Profile exactly.
- Hours, including holiday hours, and how to book.
- Services available at this location, with links to your service pages.
- The team: names, roles and short bios, with photos.
- Directions that mention real landmarks, parking and transit.
- A few reviews from customers of this location (follow the platform's rules on reusing reviews, and never use patient details).
- An embedded map and a clear call to action.
- LocalBusiness structured data for this location.

For structured data, use the specific type where one exists (Dentist, MedicalClinic, Plumber and so on) on each location page, with that location's address, phone, hours and geo coordinates. [Google's local business structured data documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) lists the supported properties, and our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-for-local-business) walks through it.

### Site structure

Keep it simple and crawlable:

1. A "Locations" page listing every site, each linking to its page.
2. One page per location at a clean URL, such as /locations/katy.
3. Service pages at the brand level, linking to the locations that offer them.
4. Optional local service pages (for example, "emergency dentist in Katy") only where you have enough unique content to justify them.

## Reviews for every location

Reviews are a ranking factor for each profile separately, and they're what searchers compare. A location with 25 reviews next to a competitor with 300 will struggle, no matter how strong your brand is elsewhere.

::steps Rolling out a review program across locations | Write one ask script and one reply standard for the whole brand | Give each location its own review link that goes to its own profile | Train front desk or techs to ask every customer, at the right moment | Set a monthly review goal per location based on its gap to competitors | Review results monthly and coach the locations falling behind

A few rules apply everywhere:

- **Ask everyone.** Google's policies prohibit review gating, where you only ask happy customers. The [FTC's rule on consumer reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers), effective October 2024, bans buying reviews, fake reviews and suppressing negative ones.
- **Send each customer to the right profile.** A review for the Katy office posted on the downtown profile helps neither.
- **Reply consistently.** Use a brand reply standard but let local managers personalize. Healthcare locations must never confirm someone is a patient or mention treatment in a reply. The HHS settlements with Elite Dental Associates in Dallas ($10,000 in 2019) and New Vision Dental in California ($23,000 in 2022) both came from review replies that disclosed patient information.

Our guide to [getting more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews) has ask scripts and timing.

> **Warning:** don't post one location's reviews on another location's profile or page as if they belonged there, and don't let a central team write reviews "on behalf of" customers. Both can break platform rules and the FTC rule.

## Brand standards without bottlenecks

Multi-location marketing needs a split between what headquarters controls and what locations do. If HQ does everything, local profiles go stale. If locations do everything, the brand drifts and mistakes multiply.

| Job | Headquarters | Location |
|---|---|---|
| Brand, logo, website, location pages | Owns | Sends photos and updates |
| Google Business Profile core fields | Owns | Requests changes |
| Hours and holiday hours | Approves | Updates promptly |
| Review requests | Sets script and tools | Asks every customer |
| Review replies | Sets standard, handles escalations | Replies within two business days |
| Social posts | Brand campaigns | Local posts on a calendar |
| Paid ads | Owns budgets and accounts | Flags capacity and local events |
| Reporting | Builds the report | Reviews own numbers monthly |

Write this down in a one-page marketing playbook every new location manager gets on day one.

## Ads across locations

For paid search, organize by location so you can see what each site costs and returns. In Google Ads that usually means a campaign or ad group per location, with location targeting set to a realistic radius and location assets linked to the right profile. Budget each location by capacity. A location with a three-week wait shouldn't get the same spend as one with open chairs.

## Reporting: one view of every location

Most multi-location owners can name their best location. Few can say which one slipped in the Maps rankings last quarter, or which one has stopped getting reviews. You need a report card per location, in a single view.

::check Monthly report card for each location | Google Maps position for 3–5 key searches | New reviews and average rating vs top local competitors | Website visits and leads from that location page | Lead response time | New customers booked | One action for next month

## Franchise-specific notes

Franchises add a layer: the franchisor owns the brand, and franchisees often own the local relationship.

- **Clarify who owns each profile and page in the franchise agreement or brand standards.** Disputes over profile ownership are common when a franchisee leaves.
- **Give franchisees a kit:** approved photos, post templates, review scripts and reply standards.
- **Use local proof.** Franchisees can win against independent competitors by showing local staff, local involvement and local reviews, not just national brand messages.
- **Watch for duplicates.** When a location changes hands or moves, old profiles often linger. Mark old addresses as moved or closed rather than creating new profiles.

## Local market differences

Locations in different cities face different competition, languages, seasons and rules. A Houston location deals with hurricane and flood season, a Chicago location with frozen pipes and winters, and a Phoenix location with extreme summer heat. State rules differ too: Texas lawyers file most ads with the State Bar, Illinois has a strict biometric privacy law, and Washington has a consumer health data law.

## A 60-day rollout plan

::steps First 60 days for a multi-location marketing reset | Week 1–2 — audit every profile, claim or fix ownership, remove duplicates | Week 3–4 — rebuild location pages with unique content and schema | Week 5–6 — launch the review program with per-location links and goals | Week 7–8 — set up the report card and the first monthly review with managers

If you have more than a handful of locations, start with your weakest two. Fixing them usually shows the biggest gains and builds the template for the rest.

## Common multi-location mistakes

- Pointing every Google Business Profile to the homepage.
- Duplicate location pages with only the city name changed.
- One shared phone number for every location, which confuses customers and Google.
- Keyword-stuffed profile names.
- Creating profiles for places with no staffed office.
- Letting an agency or ex-employee own the profiles.
- Reporting on brand totals only, so struggling locations hide behind strong ones.
- Spending the same ad budget at every location regardless of capacity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does each location need its own Google Business Profile?

Each location that serves customers in person during stated hours, with staff, should have its own verified profile. Virtual offices and mailboxes don't qualify under Google's guidelines.

### Should every location have its own page on my website?

Yes. Each profile should link to a location page with that site's address, phone, hours, team, services and directions. Pages that only swap the city name rarely rank.

### Can I share reviews between locations?

No. Reviews belong to the profile they were left on, and customers should be sent to the profile for the location they visited. Posting one location's reviews as another's can break platform rules and the FTC review rule.

### Can I verify many locations at once?

Businesses with 10 or more storefront locations may qualify for Google's bulk verification. Service-area businesses aren't eligible, and you must include every location you manage.

### How should I report on marketing across locations?

Use a monthly report card per location covering Maps position, reviews, leads, response time and new customers, compared with that location's local competitors.

### How do I keep brand control without slowing locations down?

Have headquarters own the brand, website, profiles and ad accounts, and let locations handle review requests, local posts and timely updates. Write the split down in a one-page playbook.
