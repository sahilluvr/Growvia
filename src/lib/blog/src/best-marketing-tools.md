Most small businesses don't choose a marketing stack. They collect one. An SEO tool after a bad month on Google, an email tool when the list hits a few hundred people, a scheduler when Instagram gets busy, and a CRM because someone said you need one. Two years later you're paying for five logins, copying contacts between them, and nobody remembers which tool sends the follow-up emails.

This guide compares the tools small businesses actually use in 2026. It covers what each does well, what it costs, and who it suits. We'll also show where an all-in-one platform makes sense and where a specialist tool is still the better choice.

## What "all-in-one" should actually mean

"All-in-one" gets stamped on almost every marketing product. A tool earns the label only when it covers the whole path a customer takes, from first search to final reply. For a small business, that path usually looks like this:

::flow How most customers find and buy from a small business | A Google, Maps or AI search | Your website or a social post | A form, booking or WhatsApp message | A quick, personal follow-up | A reply, then a sale

So a real all-in-one tool should help with five jobs:

1. **Get found.** Fix your website's SEO, track rankings, look after your Google Business Profile, and check whether AI assistants like ChatGPT and Gemini mention you.
2. **Capture.** Turn visitors into leads with forms, booking links and chat.
3. **Follow up.** Send emails and WhatsApp messages that go out on time and stop when someone replies.
4. **Show up.** Post on social media consistently, without spending your evenings on it.
5. **Measure.** See what is working in plain English, without building dashboards.

A tool that does one of these brilliantly is a specialist. Specialists are great, but each one adds a login, a bill and a gap where leads fall through.

### 2. HubSpot: best for growing teams that want a deep CRM

HubSpot is the best-known name in the category, and for good reason. Its free CRM is genuinely useful, and the paid hubs cover marketing, sales, service and content with deep reporting and a huge app marketplace.

**Pricing:** free tools, then Marketing Hub Starter at roughly $15 to $20 per seat per month with 1,000 marketing contacts, depending on billing and promotions. The big jump is Professional, at about $800 a month on annual billing with three seats, plus a one-time $3,000 onboarding fee. See [HubSpot's pricing page](https://www.hubspot.com/pricing/marketing) for current numbers.

**Where it falls short:** costs rise with every seat and every 1,000 contacts, and the features many teams want (better automation, SEO tools, A/B tests) sit behind Professional. It's built around a CRM first, so local SEO and WhatsApp aren't where it shines. We cover this in depth in our guide to [HubSpot alternatives](/blog/hubspot-alternatives-small-business).

### 3. Zoho: best value if you already live in Zoho

Zoho offers a separate app for almost everything: Zoho CRM, Zoho Campaigns for email, Zoho Social, Zoho Forms and more. Prices are low, and it's popular in India and across Asia.

**Pricing:** Zoho Campaigns has a free plan for up to 2,000 contacts and 6,000 emails a month, with paid plans from a few dollars a month. Zoho CRM's Standard plan starts around $14 per user per month on annual billing. Check [Zoho Campaigns pricing](https://www.zoho.com/campaigns/pricing.html) for your region.

**Where it falls short:** every capability is its own app with its own settings, so "all-in-one" really means "all from one company". Getting them to work together takes time, and SEO is not its strength.

### 4. Brevo: best for big email lists on a small budget

Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) charges by emails sent, not by contacts stored. That makes it excellent value if you have a large list you email occasionally. It also does SMS and WhatsApp campaigns.

**Pricing:** a free plan with up to 300 emails a day and up to 100,000 stored contacts, then Starter from $9 a month for 5,000 emails. Removing Brevo's logo from emails on Starter is a paid add-on. See [Brevo pricing](https://www.brevo.com/pricing/).

**Where it falls short:** it's a messaging tool. There's no SEO, no rank tracking and no AI-search checks, so you'll still need other tools to get found.

### 5. Mailchimp: the familiar newsletter tool

Mailchimp is still the name most people know for email newsletters, with polished templates and strong e-commerce integrations.

**Pricing:** the free plan now covers just 250 contacts and 500 sends a month. Essentials starts at $13 a month and Standard at $20 for 500 contacts, rising with list size, to about $75 and $100 a month at 5,000 contacts. See [Mailchimp pricing](https://mailchimp.com/pricing/marketing/).

**Where it falls short:** you pay for the size of your audience, so costs climb as your list grows, and multi-step automations need Standard or above. We compare the options in [Mailchimp alternatives](/blog/mailchimp-alternatives).

### 6. Semrush: best for serious SEO research

If SEO is your main channel and you need deep competitor research, Semrush is hard to beat. It has a vast keyword database, backlink data and traffic estimates for almost any website.

**Pricing:** Pro costs $139.95 a month, or about $117 on annual billing, and Guru costs $249.95. Extra users cost $45 a month each on Pro. See [Semrush pricing](https://www.semrush.com/prices/).

**Where it falls short:** it tells you what to do, but it won't send your follow-up emails, answer your WhatsApp messages or post to Instagram. For most local businesses it's more research power than they need.

### 7. Buffer: the simplest social scheduler

Buffer does one thing and does it cleanly: schedule posts across many networks, including LinkedIn, X, TikTok and Pinterest.

**Pricing:** free for 3 channels with 10 scheduled posts each, then $6 per channel per month ($5 on annual billing), or $12 per channel for teams. See [Buffer pricing](https://buffer.com/pricing).

**Where it falls short:** no SEO, email, forms or WhatsApp. And because pricing is per channel, it adds up as you connect more accounts.

## How to choose: five questions to ask

::check Answer these before you pick a tool | Which channel brings most of your customers today? | Who will actually use it each week, and how technical are they? | Do you need WhatsApp, and in which countries? | How big is your email list, and how often do you send? | What's your total monthly budget, across all tools?

- **If search and maps bring your customers**, you need site audits, rank tracking and Google Business Profile tools. Growvia or Semrush.
- **If you send big newsletters**, a volume-based email tool like Brevo or Zoho Campaigns is cheapest.
- **If you have a sales team with a long pipeline**, HubSpot's CRM depth may be worth the price.
- **If you sell over WhatsApp** (common in India, Singapore, the UAE and Africa), choose a tool on Meta's official API with template and opt-in support.
- **If you want one tool you'll actually open every day**, an all-in-one platform usually wins, because the best tool is the one your team uses.

## When Growvia is not the right choice

Honesty helps you choose faster, so here's when we'd point you elsewhere:

- You email a list of 20,000+ people every week. Use Brevo, Zoho Campaigns or Mailchimp for the newsletter.
- LinkedIn or TikTok is your main channel. Use a scheduler like Buffer alongside, or instead.
- You're an SEO agency doing deep backlink analysis for clients. You need Semrush or Ahrefs.
- You have a large sales team with complex deal stages and forecasting. HubSpot or a dedicated sales CRM will fit better.

For everyone else, a local business, a growing D2C brand, a consultant or a small agency, one tool that covers search, leads, follow-up and social usually beats five tools that cover one each.

Not always. Search Console shows the keywords you already appear for, your positions, clicks and impressions, and it's free. Tools like Growvia build reports and keyword ideas on top of it. Semrush is worth it when you need to research competitors' keywords and backlinks in depth.

### Can one tool really replace five?

For most small businesses, yes for the everyday jobs: audits, rankings, forms, follow-ups, WhatsApp, social posts and reports. Specialist tools still win at the edges, such as huge newsletters or deep backlink research. Many teams keep one specialist and move everything else into one place.
