Your dental website has one job: turn a stranger who found you on Google into a booked appointment. Most practice sites don't do that job well. They look fine, but a visitor on a phone can't find the insurance plans, can't tell if you see emergencies today, and gives up before the booking form loads.

The fix is rarely a full redesign. It's usually a dozen specific changes to what a new patient sees first, what questions the page answers, and how easy the next step is.

This guide walks through what a dental website that converts new patients needs: above-the-fold essentials, an insurance section, booking and forms, an emergency path, speed, trust signals, before-and-after rules and accessibility. It ends with a checklist and a teardown of a typical practice home page. It sits inside our broader [AI marketing system for dentists](/blog/ai-marketing-for-dentists), which covers how traffic reaches your site in the first place.

## What a new patient is trying to figure out

Before you change anything, think like the visitor. Someone searching "dentist near me" or "dentist that takes Cigna" is answering five questions, usually in this order:

1. Are you close enough and open when I can come?
2. Do you take my insurance, or what will it cost?
3. Can you help with my specific problem?
4. Do I trust you?
5. How do I book, right now, from my phone?

Every section of your site should answer one of those questions quickly. Anything that doesn't (a long "our philosophy" essay, a slideshow of stock smiles) pushes the answers further down.

::funnel Example: where a typical dental site loses visitors (illustrative) | Visits from Google: 1,000 | Scroll past the hero: 550 | Find insurance or pricing info: 260 | Start the booking form: 70 | Submit a request: 35

Those numbers are a labelled illustration, not a benchmark. Your own analytics will show different drop-offs, but most practices find the biggest losses at the same two points: the first screen and the form.

## Above the fold: the first screen on a phone

Most dental searches happen on phones, so design the first screen for a phone first. In roughly the space of one thumb scroll, a new patient should see:

- **What you are and where.** "Family and cosmetic dentist in Plano, TX," not just a logo and a tagline.
- **A tap-to-call button and a booking button.** Two clear actions, side by side.
- **New patient status.** "Accepting new patients" removes a real worry.
- **Hours or "open today until 6."** Especially evenings and Saturdays if you offer them.
- **One trust signal.** Your Google rating and review count, or "Most PPO plans accepted."

| Above-the-fold element | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | "Welcome to Smile Studio" | "Gentle family dentist in Plano, accepting new patients" |
| Primary action | "Learn more" | "Book a visit" and "Call (555) 010-0100" |
| Insurance | Not mentioned | "In network with most PPO plans. See the list." |
| Hours | Footer only | "Open today 7am–6pm, Saturdays by appointment" |
| Trust | Stock photo of a model | Real photo of the dentist and team |

Keep the hero image light. A huge autoplay video that takes several seconds to load on cellular data costs you more patients than it wins.

## The insurance accepted section

Insurance is the most common reason a dental visitor leaves without calling. They can't tell if you're in network, and they don't want an awkward phone call to find out.

Build a clear insurance section and link to it from the first screen:

- List the plans you're in network with by carrier name. Be precise: "Delta Dental PPO" is not the same as every Delta Dental product.
- Say what you do for out-of-network patients: "We're happy to file claims for out-of-network PPO plans."
- If you don't accept a program (for example Medicaid), say so politely so those visitors don't waste a call.
- For uninsured patients, explain your options: an in-house membership plan, financing partners, or a new patient exam package if you offer one.
- Add a short "Not sure? Send us your plan and we'll check" form.

Keep the list current. A wrong insurance list creates angry callers and bad reviews. Assign one team member to check it every quarter.

## Booking and forms

This is where interest becomes an appointment, and where many sites fall apart.

### Online booking vs request forms

You have two broad options:

- **Real-time online scheduling** connected to your practice-management software, often through tools like NexHealth or your PMS vendor. Patients pick an open slot. This is usually the highest-converting option if your schedule is kept accurate.
- **An appointment request form** that collects contact details and a preferred time, followed by a call back. Simpler and still effective if your team responds fast.

Either way, the patient should be able to reach the booking step in one tap from any page.

### What to ask, and what not to

A marketing contact form should be short. Ask for name, phone, email, preferred days or times, new or existing patient, and a simple reason for visit chosen from a short list (checkup, emergency, implants, Invisalign, other).

Don't put medical history, medications or detailed treatment questions on a general website form. That information is protected health information once it's tied to a patient, and it belongs in HIPAA-compliant intake software that has a business associate agreement with your practice. HHS explains the basics in its [HIPAA guidance for professionals](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/index.html).

::check Dental booking form checklist | 5–6 fields at most | Works with autofill on phones | Clear confirmation message after submit | Instant auto-reply email with what happens next | Spam protection that doesn't block real people | No medical history fields | Lead lands where the team sees it within minutes

Speed of response matters as much as the form. A request answered within the hour feels like great service. One answered tomorrow often goes to the practice down the road. ### Tracking without creating HIPAA risk

Many dental sites have ad pixels and analytics tags firing on every page, including booking flows. HHS issued guidance on online tracking technologies in December 2022 (updated March 2024). A federal court in June 2024 vacated part of it, covering unauthenticated public pages, but tracking on patient portals and appointment flows remains high risk. ## The emergency path

Emergency patients are some of the most valuable visitors you'll get. They're ready to book now, and if you treat them well they often stay for years.

Give emergencies their own path:

- A visible "Dental emergency? Call now" button on every page, at least on mobile.
- A dedicated emergency page that explains what counts as an emergency (severe toothache, knocked-out tooth, swelling, broken tooth), what to do while waiting, and when to go to the ER instead (for example facial swelling with fever or trouble breathing).
- Honest availability: "Same-day emergency visits when available. Call by 2pm for the best chance."
- After-hours instructions: who to call, or what your voicemail says.

::flow The emergency visitor's path | Searches "emergency dentist near me" | Lands on your emergency page | Taps call button | Booked same day

## Treatment pages that answer real questions

General pages convert general visitors. High-value patients (implants, Invisalign, veneers, sedation) want specifics before they call.

A good treatment page answers: who it's for, what the steps are, how long it takes, how payment and financing work, what results to expect (honestly), and who does the work. Include the dentist's training for that treatment and a short consultation form at the end.

These pages also help you rank. Each treatment page targets its own searches, which our [dental SEO guide](/blog/dental-seo-guide) covers alongside location pages and schema markup.

## Speed: the invisible conversion killer

A slow site loses patients before they see anything. Google's [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) tests your pages and reports Core Web Vitals, the measures Google uses for loading, responsiveness and visual stability.

Common causes on dental sites:

- Oversized hero images and background videos.
- Several chat widgets, review widgets and tracking scripts loading on every page.
- Heavy page builders and sliders.
- Cheap shared hosting.

::scale Core Web Vitals, Largest Contentful Paint (Google's thresholds) | Good: 2.5 seconds or less | Needs work: 2.5 to 4 seconds | Poor: over 4 seconds

Start with images (compress them and serve modern formats), then remove scripts you don't use. Our [Core Web Vitals guide](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained) explains each metric in plain English.

## Trust signals that actually work

A new patient is about to let a stranger work in their mouth. Earn that trust with specifics.

- **Real photos.** The dentist, the team, the treatment rooms, the front desk. Stock photos of perfect smiles undermine trust.
- **Dentist bios.** Education, years in practice, continuing education in specific treatments, memberships, and a human detail or two.
- **Reviews.** Show your Google rating and a few recent reviews, with a link to read more on Google. Never edit reviews to change their meaning, and remember the FTC's [rule on consumer reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/endorsements-influencers-reviews) bans fake reviews and review suppression. Our [dental reviews and HIPAA guide](/blog/dental-office-google-reviews-hipaa) covers getting more reviews safely.
- **Clear policies.** Cancellation policy, payment options, and what the first visit includes.
- **Comfort details.** Sedation options, accessibility, languages spoken, parking.

## Before-and-after photos: the rules

Before-and-after photos are powerful for cosmetic work, but they come with rules.

- **Patient authorization.** Full-face photos are one of HIPAA's listed identifiers, and using a patient's photos in marketing generally needs their written HIPAA authorization. Get a signed authorization that specifically covers website and social use, and store it.
- **Truthfulness.** Photos must show real results from your practice, without editing that changes the outcome. State dental boards regulate dental advertising, and many have specific rules about testimonials and results.
- **Typical results.** Don't imply every patient will get the same result. A short note such as "Individual results vary" alongside an explanation of what was done helps set expectations.
- **Specialty claims.** Some states restrict how general dentists describe services that overlap with recognized specialties. Check your state dental board's advertising rules.

The [ADA Code of Ethics](https://www.ada.org/about/principles/code-of-ethics) also addresses truthful advertising. It isn't law, but state boards often take a similar line.

## Accessibility

An accessible site serves more patients and lowers legal risk. Lawsuits and demand letters alleging inaccessible websites under the Americans with Disabilities Act are common against private businesses, including healthcare offices. The Department of Justice's [guidance on web accessibility](https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/) says businesses open to the public should make their websites accessible, and most practitioners use the W3C's WCAG guidelines as the technical standard.

Quick wins:

- Alt text on meaningful images.
- Strong color contrast for text and buttons.
- Forms with visible labels, not just placeholder text.
- Every function usable with a keyboard.
- Captions on videos.
- Phone number and booking visible as text, not just inside an image.

## Teardown: a typical dental home page

Here's a hypothetical teardown. Bright Smiles Dental (fictional) has a 3-dentist practice in a suburban area. Their home page looks modern, but it underperforms.

::compare Bright Smiles home page, before and after (hypothetical) | Before: Full-screen video hero; "Welcome to Bright Smiles" headline; phone number in the footer; insurance on a separate page with no link; 12-field contact form with medical history | After: Static photo of the real team; "Family dentist in Riverside, accepting new patients" headline; call and book buttons at the top; "Most PPO plans accepted, see list" link; 5-field form with instant auto-reply

What changed and why:

1. **Headline.** Now says what, where and that they're accepting patients.
2. **Actions.** Call and book buttons sit in the first screen on a phone.
3. **Insurance.** One link from the top answers the most common question.
4. **Form.** Shorter, with no health information, and an instant confirmation email.
5. **Speed.** Replacing the video with a compressed photo cut load time on phones.
6. **Emergency.** A sticky "Dental emergency?" bar on mobile links to a new emergency page.

None of that required a new website. It took a few days of edits.

## The full checklist

Use this as a working list for your next website review:

- Headline says what you do, where, and that you accept new patients.
- Call and book buttons visible in the first screen on a phone.
- Hours visible near the top.
- Insurance plans listed by name, linked from the first screen.
- Options for uninsured and out-of-network patients explained.
- Online booking or a short request form, one tap from every page.
- Instant auto-reply and a response within the hour during business hours.
- No medical history on marketing forms.
- Tracking tags kept off booking flows and patient portals.
- Emergency page with honest availability and after-hours instructions.
- Treatment pages for your high-value services.
- Pages load fast on mobile and pass Core Web Vitals.
- Real photos, dentist bios and recent reviews.
- Signed authorizations for every patient photo or testimonial.
- Accessibility basics covered.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should be on a dental website home page?

A clear headline with what you do and where, call and book buttons, hours, new patient status, insurance information, and one trust signal such as your Google rating, all visible on the first phone screen. Below that, add treatment links, dentist bios, reviews and an emergency path.

### Should a dental website have online booking?

Real-time booking connected to your practice software usually converts best if your schedule is accurate. A short request form works well too, as long as your team responds quickly.

### Can I put patient intake forms on my dental website?

Only through HIPAA-compliant software that signs a business associate agreement with your practice. General website and marketing forms should collect contact details and a preferred time, not medical history.

### Do I need patient permission for before-and-after photos?

Yes. Using a patient's photos in marketing generally needs a signed HIPAA authorization, and you should check your state dental board's rules on testimonials and results claims.

### How fast should a dental website load?

Google considers a Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less good. Test your pages with PageSpeed Insights and fix large images and unused scripts first.

### Does my dental website need to be ADA accessible?

The Department of Justice says businesses open to the public should make their websites accessible, and website accessibility claims against healthcare offices are common. Following WCAG guidelines is the usual approach.
