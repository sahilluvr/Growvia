Residential cleaning fills calendars one house at a time. Commercial cleaning fills them with contracts: the same office every weeknight, the same clinic three times a week, the same gym every morning before opening. Those contracts are the backbone of a cleaning business that can plan staff, buy supplies in bulk and grow without constantly hunting for new customers.

This guide covers which businesses to target, how to find the decision-maker, how to run a walkthrough that closes and how to keep accounts once you win them.


## Why commercial contracts are the foundation of a cleaning business

Residential cleaning can be profitable, but it's fragile. Clients move, cancel for holidays, switch to a cheaper cleaner a neighbor recommends or decide to clean themselves when money is tight. Every lost client leaves a hole in a crew's schedule that has to be filled with a new booking.

Commercial contracts change that. An office cleaned four nights a week, a clinic cleaned daily, a gym cleaned every morning before opening: each is a predictable block of work that repeats month after month. You can schedule crews around them, buy supplies in larger quantities and plan hiring with confidence. When a contract renews, you keep the revenue without spending anything on marketing.

Commercial work also tends to concentrate geographically. Business districts, medical parks and retail centers put several potential clients within a few minutes of each other, which means less travel time and more billable hours. Once a crew is already cleaning one office in a building, adding the office next door is far more profitable than adding a house across town.

The flip side is that commercial buyers are more demanding. They expect consistency, security, documentation and quick fixes when something is missed. Companies that build systems for those expectations keep contracts for years.

## Running a walkthrough that closes

Walkthroughs win or lose contracts. Do these things every time:

1. **Ask what annoys them** about the current cleaning. Write it down in front of them.
2. **Measure and photograph** the space so your quote is accurate.
3. **Send a written scope within 24 hours** listing every task and how often it's done.
4. **Price monthly, not hourly.** Buyers want to know the number, not do math.
5. **Name the crew lead** and offer a direct number for issues.

## Keeping accounts for years

Contracts are lost slowly: small misses pile up until the client looks elsewhere. Prevent that with a monthly check-in, a quick quality inspection by a supervisor and fast fixes when something is missed. Ask for a review or a referral after three good months; office managers often know other office managers.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Quoting without a walkthrough.** You'll underprice or overprice; both lose.
- **Rotating crews.** Clients notice, and trust drops.
- **Ignoring small complaints.** One missed trash can is how cancellations start.

## Your first walkthrough conversation

1. What does your current cleaner do well, and what do they miss?
2. Which areas matter most to your clients, patients or members?
3. When can the crew work without disrupting staff?
4. Who holds keys or alarm codes, and what are the security rules?
5. Are there any products or methods you need us to use or avoid?
6. How do you want to report issues: phone, text or email?
7. Who approves the contract, and when do you need a decision?

Take notes in front of them and repeat the main concerns back before you leave. Then send a written scope within 24 hours that addresses every point they raised. Being fast and specific is the clearest signal of how you'll perform.

## Handling common objections

| What they say | What it usually means | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "We have a cleaner." | Not unhappy enough to switch. | "Totally fair. If they ever miss something, we can start within a week. May I send a scope so you have a comparison?" |
| "Your price is higher." | They're comparing hourly rates or vague scopes. | Show the task list and frequency side by side with what they get now. |
| "We're locked into a contract." | There's a renewal date. | Ask when it ends and set a reminder 60 days before. |
| "Security worries us." | Keys and access are sensitive. | Explain background checks, key control and that the same crew comes every time. |
| "We'll think about it." | They need approval. | Offer a no-contract first month to lower the risk. |

## Building trust before you reach out

Commercial buyers check credentials. Make sure you have liability insurance and, where relevant, bonding, with certificates ready to send. Add a commercial cleaning page to your website showing the industries you serve, your process, your security practices and photos of real work. Ask a couple of existing commercial clients if you can list them as references. Make your Google Business Profile show commercial services and replies to every review. A business that searches for you should see an organized company, not a side job.

## Specialized offers that set you apart

**Medical-grade cleaning.** Clinics and dental offices need written disinfection procedures, attention to high-touch surfaces and products appropriate for healthcare settings. Training a crew specifically for these sites lets you charge for expertise and win accounts others avoid.

**Gym and studio cleaning.** Equipment wipe-downs, rubber floors, mirrors, locker rooms and odor control require different products and routines. Gyms open early, so pre-dawn crews are often essential.

**Day porter service.** Larger offices and coworking spaces benefit from someone on site during the day to restock restrooms, tidy kitchens and handle spills. It's a premium add-on that keeps you visible to the client.

**Move-out and turnover cleans.** Property managers need units cleaned quickly between tenants. Fast turnarounds and consistent quality make you their first call.

## A worked example

Picture how a first clinic account might develop. The details are illustrative.

You email a small family medicine clinic near one of your existing office routes. The practice manager replies to your follow-up about signed checklists; their current cleaner has been inconsistent and a patient recently complained about the restrooms. You walk through the clinic that week, note the exam rooms, waiting area and two restrooms, and ask about their busiest times and security procedures.

Within 24 hours, you send a scope listing every room, the tasks, their frequency and the disinfection steps, plus a fixed monthly price and an offer of a no-contract first month. They accept. Your crew lead introduces herself on the first night, leaves the signed checklist and texts the manager when they're done.

After three months without a single complaint, the practice manager recommends you to the dental office upstairs. Because your crew is already in the building, adding the second account costs almost nothing in travel and the margin on that contract is excellent.

## Pricing commercial cleaning with confidence

Underpricing is the most common mistake new commercial cleaners make, and it's hard to undo once a client is used to the number. Build your price from the walkthrough rather than from a guess. Estimate the time each visit takes by area and task, multiply by frequency, and add travel, supplies, supervision and a margin that leaves room for the occasional extra hour. Price restrooms, kitchens and specialized areas separately in your workings even if you quote one monthly number, because they take longer than open office space.

Quote a fixed monthly fee and list exactly what it includes. Put anything that isn't routine, such as carpet shampooing, window washing or post-construction cleaning, on a separate price list so the base contract stays clear. Review pricing with each client once a year, and tie any increase to changes in scope or costs you can explain. Clients accept fair, transparent increases far more readily than surprise invoices.

## Keeping crews consistent as you grow

Commercial clients notice when faces change. Assign a crew lead to each account, keep the same team on a site wherever possible and document every site's quirks: alarm codes, which doors to lock, where supplies are kept, special requests. When someone is out sick, the replacement should have that site sheet in hand. Run short quality inspections every few weeks, either in person or with photos, and fix anything missed before the client notices. These habits are what separate companies that keep contracts for years from those that churn through them.

## Communicating with clients after each visit

A short message after each visit, such as a checklist photo or a quick note about anything unusual, reassures clients that the work was done. Flag issues you notice, like a leaking tap or a broken light, so the client can fix them. These small touches show attention to detail and build the trust that keeps contracts renewing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do cleaning companies get commercial contracts?

Target offices, clinics, gyms and property managers on your existing routes. Email a short offer, run a careful walkthrough, send a written scope and fixed monthly price, and offer a short trial.

### How should I price commercial cleaning?

Quote a fixed monthly price based on the walkthrough: square footage, number of restrooms, frequency and any specialist tasks. Monthly pricing is easier for buyers to approve than hourly rates.

### Are medical offices good cleaning clients?

They can be very good: steady, frequent and loyal when standards are met. They expect written checklists and consistent disinfection procedures.

### How do I find out who decides on cleaning?

In small businesses it's usually the owner or office manager. In larger buildings, ask for the property or facilities manager.

### What insurance do commercial cleaners need?

Most commercial clients expect general liability insurance, and many ask for bonding and workers' compensation coverage. Check what's required in your state and have certificates ready.

### Should cleaning companies offer day porter services?

For larger offices and coworking spaces, day porters keep restrooms and kitchens tidy during business hours and can be a valuable add-on.

### How do I win back a client who left for a cheaper cleaner?

Stay in touch politely. Cheaper cleaners often slip on consistency, and a short check-in a few months later can bring the client back.

### How do I find out when a cleaning contract renews?

Ask during the first conversation. If the business is under contract, note the end date and reach out about 60 days before with a fresh scope and price.

### Should I clean during or after business hours?

Most offices and clinics prefer after hours to avoid disruption, while gyms often need early mornings. Ask each client and price for any overnight or weekend work.

### Should cleaning crews report maintenance issues they notice?

Yes. Flagging issues like leaks or broken fixtures helps clients and shows attention to detail.

### How do cleaning companies handle keys and alarm codes?

With a written key control process, limited access for named crew members and secure records.

### What's a good first niche for a commercial cleaner?

Offices near your current routes are a common start; clinics and gyms can follow once checklists are in place.

### How do cleaning companies handle supplies?

Agree whether you or the client provide supplies, and price accordingly in the monthly fee.
