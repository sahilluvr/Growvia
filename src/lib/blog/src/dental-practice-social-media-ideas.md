Most dental practices post on social media the same way: a burst of enthusiasm, three weeks of "National Flossing Day" graphics, then silence until someone remembers the account exists. It's not a lack of effort. It's the lack of a plan that a busy front desk can actually run.

This guide gives you that plan. You'll get five content pillars, 30 dental social media ideas laid out as a month, the patient-consent rules you must follow before posting any patient photo, how to make simple reels, how to use AI for captions without sounding robotic, how often to post, and what to measure.

Social media is rarely the biggest source of new patients for a dental practice. Google Maps, your website and recall usually do more. But social builds familiarity, supports referrals and gives your reviews and team a face. It works best as one part of a wider system, which our [AI marketing guide for dentists](/blog/ai-marketing-for-dentists) lays out.

## What social media can and can't do for a dental practice

Be realistic so you put in the right amount of time.

Social media is good at:

- Showing prospective patients who you are before they book, so the first visit feels less scary.
- Keeping existing patients engaged, which supports referrals and recall.
- Recruiting. Hygienists and assistants check your social profiles too.
- Giving people who find you on Google a second place to confirm you're real and active.

Social media is weak at:

- Producing a steady stream of new patients on its own, without paid promotion.
- Replacing Google Business Profile, reviews or SEO.
- Reaching people actively searching for a dentist right now. They search Google, not Instagram.

::compare What dental social media does well vs poorly | Does well: builds trust before the first visit; supports referrals; shows your team; helps hiring | Does poorly: brings urgent patients; replaces Google Maps; works without consistency; fixes a weak website

So the goal is consistency, not virality. Two or three solid posts a week, every week, beats a daily schedule that collapses in March.

## Five content pillars for a dental practice

Content pillars are the recurring themes you post about. They stop you staring at a blank screen. Use these five and rotate through them.

| Pillar | What it covers | Why it works | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meet the team | Dentists, hygienists, front desk, their stories | People choose people | "Meet Ana, our hygienist of 9 years, who's run 4 marathons" |
| Education | Simple answers to common questions | Builds authority and gets saved and shared | "3 signs your gums need attention" |
| Behind the scenes | Tech, sterilization, office life | Reduces anxiety, shows care | A 15-second clip of your sterilization process |
| Community | Local events, schools, charities, local businesses | Makes you the neighborhood practice | Photos from a school dental health visit |
| Practice news and offers | New services, hours, insurance updates, new patient specials (within your state's rules) | Drives action | "We're now in network with [plan]" |

A healthy mix is roughly 70% helpful or human content (team, education, behind the scenes, community) and 30% promotion. Practices that only post offers get scrolled past.

## Patient consent rules before you post

This section matters more than any idea in the list. Posting patients without proper permission can violate HIPAA and your state dental board's rules.

### What counts as PHI on social media

Under HIPAA, protected health information includes any health information that can identify a patient. Full-face photos are one of the identifiers HHS lists. That means a photo of a patient in your chair, a smile transformation, or even a comment that confirms someone is your patient can be a disclosure.

HHS has acted against dental practices for disclosures on review sites. In 2019 Elite Dental Associates in Dallas paid $10,000 to settle after disclosing patient information while replying to Yelp reviews. In December 2022, New Vision Dental in California paid $23,000 for disclosing PHI in Yelp review replies. Social media replies carry the same risk.

### The rules to follow

- **Get a written HIPAA authorization** before posting any patient photo, video, story or testimonial. Using PHI for marketing generally requires written authorization. HHS explains this in its [guidance on marketing under the Privacy Rule](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html).
- **Make the authorization specific.** It should name the platforms (website, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok), the type of content, and how the patient can revoke it.
- **Store it** with the patient's record, and keep a simple list of who has consented.
- **Watch the background.** Schedules on screens, X-rays on monitors, charts on counters and other patients in the waiting room all count.
- **Never confirm someone is a patient in a comment or reply.** If a patient comments "Thanks for fixing my crown!", reply with a general thank you that doesn't mention treatment, or move to a private, HIPAA-appropriate channel.
- **Kids need a parent or guardian's authorization.**

> Warning: "They tagged us first" is not consent. Even if a patient posts about their treatment publicly, your reply can't add health details or confirm they're your patient. Keep replies general, like "Thank you for the kind words, we love having you in the community!"

If you work with local influencers, the FTC's [endorsement guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/endorsements-influencers-reviews) requires clear disclosure of any free treatment, payment or relationship. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024, also bans fake reviews and fake social media influence such as bought followers or engagement.

## The 30-day dental social media plan

Here are 30 dental social media ideas laid out across four weeks. You don't have to post every day. Pick three to five per week and save the rest for next month. Swap in your own details.

::steps How to run the 30-day plan | Day 1 — Pick the 12–15 ideas you'll actually post | Day 2 — Batch-shoot photos and short clips in one hour | Day 3 — Write or generate captions and review them | Day 4 — Schedule the month | Weekly — Reply to comments and messages within a day | Day 30 — Review what worked and plan next month

### Week 1: Introduce yourselves

1. **Meet the dentist.** A photo and short story: why they became a dentist, where they trained, one personal detail.
2. **Office tour reel.** Walk from the front door to a treatment room in 20 seconds.
3. **What to expect at your first visit.** Step by step: check-in, X-rays, exam, cleaning, plan.
4. **Meet the front desk.** The person new patients talk to first. A friendly face reduces phone anxiety.
5. **Myth vs fact.** "Myth: if your gums bleed, stop flossing. Fact: bleeding often means you need to floss more consistently. Ask your hygienist."
6. **Insurance post.** "We're in network with these plans," with a link to your insurance page.
7. **Local shout-out.** Your favorite coffee shop or lunch spot near the office. Tag them.

### Week 2: Educate and reassure

8. **How to brush properly.** A hygienist demonstrates on a model in a short reel.
9. **Dental anxiety.** What you do to make nervous patients comfortable (sedation options, breaks, headphones).
10. **Behind the scenes: sterilization.** Show the process. Patients rarely see it and appreciate it.
11. **Kids' first visit tips.** When to bring them, how to prepare them.
12. **FAQ answer.** Pick a question your front desk gets every week and answer it in 30 seconds.
13. **Team hobby feature.** A hygienist's garden, an assistant's dog, the dentist's cycling.
14. **Review spotlight.** Share a Google review as a graphic (first name or initials only, no treatment details added by you, and only from reviews the patient posted publicly). Then link to your review page.

### Week 3: Show your expertise

15. **Technology spotlight.** Your intraoral scanner or digital X-rays, explained simply.
16. **Treatment explainer.** "What actually happens during a root canal," with a model or animation, no patient.
17. **Patient story** (only with written authorization). A short video in the patient's own words.
18. **Emergency guide.** "Knocked-out tooth? Here's what to do in the next 30 minutes."
19. **Food and teeth.** Snacks that are kinder to teeth, a local twist on seasonal treats.
20. **Ask me anything.** A story with a question box. Answer the best questions in a later post.
21. **Team training day.** Photos from a continuing education course.

### Week 4: Community and action

22. **Community event.** A school visit, charity drive or local 5K sponsorship.
23. **Before and after** (only with written authorization). Cosmetic result, honest caption, no exaggerated claims.
24. **Staff anniversary.** Celebrate someone's years with the practice.
25. **Seasonal reminder.** Back-to-school checkups, sports mouthguards, or "use your benefits before year end" if your patients' plans reset in January.
26. **Poll.** "Electric or manual toothbrush?" Easy engagement.
27. **Referral thank you.** "The best compliment is a referral. Thank you for trusting us with your friends and family."
28. **New patient welcome.** What makes your practice different, with a book now link.
29. **Bloopers or team fun.** Halloween costumes, a team lunch, a birthday.
30. **Month recap.** Your favorite moments, a thank you, and what's coming next month.

## Making reels without a video team

Short vertical video gets more reach than still images on Instagram, Facebook and TikTok. You don't need a camera crew.

### A simple setup

- A recent phone, held vertically.
- A cheap clip-on microphone if anyone speaks to the camera.
- Natural light from a window, or a small ring light.
- A tidy background. Check for patient information on screens and counters.

### Formats that work for dental practices

::check Easy dental reel formats | 15-second office walk-through | Hygienist answers one question | "Day in the life" of a team member | Before your first visit, what to expect | Myth vs fact with on-screen text | Quick tool or technology explainer

Keep reels short: hook in the first two seconds ("Your gums bleed when you floss? Watch this."), one idea, and on-screen text, since many people watch without sound. Add captions for accessibility too.

## Using AI for captions (without sounding like a robot)

AI writing tools can turn a rough idea into a caption in seconds. Used well, they save the front desk real time. Used badly, they produce generic posts that sound like every other practice.

A good process:

1. Give the AI your specifics: the team member's name, the real story, your town, your tone.
2. Ask for two or three short options, not one long one.
3. Edit for your voice. Cut anything that sounds like an ad.
4. Check every health statement. AI can state things too confidently. Have a dentist or hygienist approve anything clinical.
5. Never paste patient information into an AI tool.

## Posting cadence

How often should a dental practice post? Enough to look active and stay consistent. There's no official magic number, so pick what your team can sustain.

| Practice situation | Suggested cadence | Platforms |
|---|---|---|
| Solo practice, no marketing help | 2 posts per week | Facebook and Instagram (cross-post) |
| 2–4 dentists, a team member owns social | 3–4 posts per week, plus stories | Instagram, Facebook |
| Cosmetic or ortho focus | 3–5 posts per week, reels-heavy | Instagram, TikTok, Facebook |
| Multi-location group | 3 posts per week per location page, shared brand content | Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn for hiring |

Also post to your Google Business Profile weekly. Google posts don't replace Instagram, but they appear where people are actively looking for a dentist. Our [Google Business Profile optimization guide](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-guide) covers posts, photos and updates.

Batching is the secret to consistency. Shoot photos and clips for the whole month in one hour, write captions in one sitting, and schedule everything. Then the only daily job is replying to comments and messages.

## Replying to comments and messages

Social media is a two-way channel, and slow replies look like a closed door.

- Reply to comments within a day.
- Move appointment questions to a call or your booking link.
- Don't discuss treatment details in public comments or DMs on general social platforms. Direct people to call the office or use your secure patient messaging.
- Handle negative comments calmly and generally, then take it offline. Our guide to [responding to negative reviews](/blog/professional-services-negative-review-responses) has templates that work for comments too.

## What to measure

Ignore vanity metrics on their own. A post with lots of likes from people 2,000 miles away won't fill your schedule.

::bars Which social metrics matter for a dental practice (illustrative priority) | New patients citing social: 10 * | Link clicks to booking: 8 | Messages and DMs: 7 | Saves and shares: 5 | Local followers: 4 | Likes: 2

That ranking is our suggested priority, not data. What to track monthly:

- **"How did you hear about us?"** Ask every new patient at booking and record the answer in your practice software.
- **Link clicks** to your booking page from your bio and posts. Use UTM parameters on links so your analytics shows them.
- **Messages** asking about appointments.
- **Saves and shares**, which show content people find useful.
- **Follower growth from your area.** Instagram's insights show top locations.

Review once a month. Keep the formats that drove clicks and messages, drop the ones that didn't, and plan the next 30 days.

## Common mistakes

- **Posting patients without written authorization.** The biggest and most avoidable risk.
- **Only posting offers.** People follow practices for people and useful tips.
- **Stock photos everywhere.** Real team photos build more trust.
- **Inconsistent posting.** A month of daily posts followed by silence looks worse than steady weekly posts.
- **Ignoring comments and DMs.** A slow reply is a missed appointment.
- **Copying trends that don't fit.** A dance trend can work for some teams. If it makes your team uncomfortable, skip it.
- **Medical claims without review.** Every clinical statement should be checked by a dentist.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a dental office post on social media?

Rotate through five pillars: meet the team, education, behind the scenes, community and practice news. Aim for most posts to be helpful or human, with about a third promotional.

### How often should a dental practice post on social media?

Two to four posts per week is sustainable for most practices, plus weekly Google Business Profile posts. Consistency matters more than volume, so pick a cadence your team can keep.

### Can I post patient photos on my dental practice's social media?

Only with the patient's written HIPAA authorization that covers social media use. Full-face photos are a HIPAA identifier, and you also need to check your state dental board's rules on testimonials and before-and-after images.

### Is it OK to reply to a patient's comment about their treatment?

Reply warmly but generally, without confirming they're a patient or mentioning treatment. HHS has settled with dental practices that disclosed patient information in public review replies.

### Which social media platform is best for dentists?

Facebook and Instagram suit most general practices, since they reach local families and adults. Cosmetic and orthodontic practices often add TikTok for short video, and LinkedIn helps with hiring.

### Can I use AI to write dental social media posts?

Yes, for drafting captions and ideas, as long as you add your own details, edit for your voice, have clinical statements checked by a dentist, and never paste patient information into the tool.
