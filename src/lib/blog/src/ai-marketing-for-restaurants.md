Most restaurant marketing advice assumes you have a marketing person. You don't. You have a kitchen to run, a schedule full of holes, food costs that won't stop climbing and a phone that buzzes with delivery tablets all night. All-in-one AI marketing for restaurants should fit into the 30 minutes you actually have between lunch and dinner prep.

A hungry person usually decides in a few minutes, on one screen: Google Maps, a review site or an AI assistant answering "best tacos near me." If you win that screen, keep the guests you already earned and stop renting every order from a delivery app, you're ahead of most places on your block.

## How guests choose where to eat

The path from hungry to seated is short. Guests search, skim photos and reviews, check hours and the menu, then call, book, order or show up. After the meal, you either have a way to bring them back or you don't.

::flow How a guest finds you and comes back | Searches Maps or asks an AI assistant | Checks photos, menu, hours and reviews | Books, orders or walks in | Joins your list and returns

Each stage has a weak point. Wrong hours or a PDF menu that won't load on a phone lose the guest at stage two. A great meal with no way to stay in touch loses stage four. The sections below fix each one.

## Google Maps: your most important storefront

For most restaurants, Google Maps is the biggest source of new guests. When someone searches "ramen near me" or "brunch in Midtown," Google shows a map pack of three places drawn from Business Profiles. Google's [guide to local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says results depend on relevance, distance and prominence, and that review count and ratings factor into ranking.

You can't change your distance from a searcher. You can make your profile more relevant and more prominent.

- **Categories.** Pick the most specific primary category that describes you ("Taco restaurant" or "Ramen restaurant," not just "Restaurant"). Add secondary categories only if they're true, like "Bar" or "Caterer."
- **Hours.** Keep regular hours accurate and add special hours for every holiday. Wrong hours are one of the fastest ways to earn a one-star review.
- **Menu.** Add your menu to the profile and link your menu page. More on menus below.
- **Attributes.** Mark what applies: outdoor seating, dine-in, takeout, delivery, vegetarian options, wheelchair-accessible entrance, good for kids and so on.
- **Order and reservation links.** Make sure the links on your profile go to your preferred ordering and booking pages, not only to a delivery app.
- **Name.** Use your real name. Adding "Best Tacos in Austin" to it breaks Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) and risks suspension.

::check Restaurant Business Profile checklist | Specific primary category | Regular and holiday hours correct | Menu added with current prices | Attributes and dietary options marked | Order and booking links point where you want | Fresh photos of food, room and exterior | Weekly post about a special or event

## Your menu is marketing

Your menu is the page guests check most, and often the worst page on a restaurant's website.

**Put the menu in real text, not only a PDF or photo.** PDFs load slowly on phones and are harder for Google and AI assistants to understand. A text menu on your website lets Google connect your dishes to searches like "birria tacos" or "gluten-free pizza."

**Keep prices current.** Nothing frustrates a guest more than a surprise at the register. Update your website, Business Profile and delivery apps the same day you change prices.

**Label dietary options clearly.** Vegan, vegetarian, gluten-free and spicy markers help guests decide. Only label what's accurate, and note cross-contact risks where relevant.

**Know the rules if you're a chain.** Under FDA menu labeling rules, restaurants and similar retail food establishments that are part of a chain with 20 or more locations must show calorie counts on menus and menu boards, including online menus in many cases. The FDA's [menu labeling requirements page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/menu-labeling-requirements) explains who's covered. Independent restaurants aren't required to, but some state and local rules add their own requirements, so check yours.

## Photos that make people hungry

Guests eat with their eyes before they ever see your room. On Maps, photos are often the first thing they scroll through, and customers add their own whether you like it or not.

- **Shoot your top ten dishes** in natural light, from the angle a guest sees them. A modern phone is fine.
- **Show the room** at a busy, happy moment, plus the exterior so people recognize you from the street.
- **Show people.** Your team, your cooks, your regulars (with permission).
- **Add new photos regularly,** especially when the menu changes or the season shifts.

AI-made images are fine for event graphics, but use real photos of your real food on your menu and profile. Guests notice when a dish looks nothing like its picture.

## Reviews: earn more and reply well

Reviews feed your Maps ranking and your conversion rate. Guests read them before they decide, and they read your replies too.

### Asking for reviews within the rules

You can ask every guest for a review. A small card with a QR code on the check presenter, a line on receipts, or a follow-up email to guests who booked online all work. What you can't do:

- **Pay for reviews or offer discounts in exchange.** Google's policies prohibit incentives for reviews.
- **Only ask happy guests.** Google bans "review gating."
- **Post fake reviews** or have staff review you without disclosure.
- **Threaten or pressure** people to remove negative reviews.

The FTC's [Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, buying positive or negative reviews, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression, with civil penalties per violation. Our guide on [how to get more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews) has more scripts and timing tips.

### Replying like a host

Reply to every review, short and specific. Thank happy guests by mentioning what they enjoyed. For unhappy guests, apologize for the experience, don't argue, and move the conversation offline.

::chat Replying to a tough review | them: Waited 40 minutes for our food and the server forgot our drinks. Not coming back. | you: I'm sorry, that's not the night we want anyone to have. Friday was short-staffed, but that's our problem, not yours. | you: I'd like to make it right. Please email me at manager@[yourrestaurant].com so we can talk. Maria, GM

Be careful with serious claims. If someone says they got sick, don't argue or admit fault in public. Respond calmly, ask them to contact you directly, document what happened and talk to your insurer if needed. If you think a review is fake or breaks Google's policies, flag it rather than fighting in the replies.

## Delivery apps vs direct orders

Delivery apps are useful. They put you in front of people who'd never find you otherwise, and they handle drivers you don't want to manage. But they're expensive, and the customer belongs to the app, not to you.

Marketplace commissions on the big apps are tiered, and the top tiers can reach around 30% of the order before payment processing and promotions. Check each app's current merchant pricing for your exact plan. You also don't get the guest's email or permission to market to them, so every repeat order costs you the commission again.

| Channel | What it's good for | Main cost | Who owns the guest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delivery marketplaces | Discovery by new customers, delivery logistics | Per-order commission, promo fees | The app |
| Direct online ordering on your site | Repeat orders from regulars, pickup | Software fee, card processing, your own delivery if offered | You |
| Phone orders | Regulars, older guests, large orders | Staff time, missed calls at peak | You |
| Reservations platform | Dine-in bookings, guest notes | Subscription or per-cover fees, depending on platform | Shared, check the terms |
| Walk-ins from Maps | New local guests | Your time on your Business Profile | You, if you capture them |

::compare A $40 order through an app vs directly | App order: commission taken from the order; no guest email; the app may show them a competitor next time | Direct order: you keep more of the order after fees; you can invite them to your list; their next order comes straight to you

The practical strategy for most restaurants isn't to quit the apps. It's to use them for discovery and move repeat guests to direct channels:

1. **Make direct ordering easy.** Put "Order pickup" first on your website and Business Profile.
2. **Give a reason to order direct.** A loyalty reward, a direct-only item or a small perk for joining your list works, as long as it complies with your app contracts.
3. **Check your app terms** before adding marketing inserts to app orders. Policies vary, and some restrict it.
4. **Track the share of orders that are direct** every month. It's one of the best measures of whether your marketing is working.

## Build a guest list you own

Your email list is the only marketing channel no platform can take away or charge you more for next year. When a slow Tuesday comes, you can fill seats with one email instead of hoping the algorithm is kind.

Capture emails with consent through:

- **Your Wi-Fi login page,** with a clear checkbox to join your list.
- **A QR code** on tables and check presenters for a birthday club or loyalty program.
- **Online ordering and reservations,** with a marketing opt-in.
- **Your website,** with a simple signup for events and specials.

Then send a short welcome email, a birthday offer, a monthly note about what's new, and targeted promotions for slow nights. Follow CAN-SPAM: honest From and subject lines, your postal address and an unsubscribe link honored within 10 business days. The FTC's [CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) covers the basics.

## Social media without living on your phone

Social media matters for restaurants more than for most businesses, because food is visual and people share where they eat. But you don't need to post three times a day.

A simple plan: three to four posts a week, built on a few recurring themes.

- **The food:** a dish of the week, a new menu item, something coming out of the oven.
- **The people:** your cooks, servers and the story behind a recipe.
- **What's happening:** events, specials, holiday hours, a new patio.
- **Guests:** reposts of guests' photos and tagged stories, with permission.

Short vertical video tends to reach new people on Instagram and TikTok. It doesn't have to be polished. Thirty seconds of the grill at dinner rush is enough. Our [social media content plan](/blog/social-media-content-plan-small-business) has a simple monthly framework.

## Events, slow nights and seasonal promotions

Most restaurants have predictable slow periods: early-week nights, the weeks after New Year's, a summer slump in a college town. Marketing can't fix a bad concept, but it can move guests to the nights you need them. Recurring nights with a clear hook (trivia, a family night, a chef's tasting), seasonal menus and local tie-ins like game days all give you a reason to post and email.

Check your state's alcohol rules before running drink promotions. Rules on happy hours and drink discounts vary widely. Massachusetts, for example, has banned happy-hour drink discounts since the 1980s. Your state alcohol beverage control agency is the authority.

Catering and private events deserve their own funnel, because each inquiry can be worth more than a week of regular tables. A website form that captures date, headcount and budget, plus a fast reply, wins those bookings. ## Show up when guests ask AI assistants

More people now ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity "Where should I get tacos in San Antonio?" or "Best brunch spot near Union Square with outdoor seating." These assistants pull from websites, Google and Yelp reviews, food writing, TripAdvisor, reservation platforms and local news.

What helps a restaurant get named:

- **Clear, consistent information** everywhere: name, address, phone, hours and cuisine on Google, Yelp, Apple Maps, TripAdvisor and your reservation platform.
- **A text menu** on your website that names your dishes.
- **Review themes.** Reviews that mention specific dishes ("the al pastor is the best I've had") teach assistants what you're known for.
- **Mentions in local food coverage,** best-of lists and neighborhood guides.
- **Schema markup** on your website. Schema.org has a [Restaurant type](https://schema.org/Restaurant) with properties for cuisine, menu and reservations, and Google's [local business structured data guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) shows how to add it.

## Your website: fast, clear and accessible

Your website has four jobs: show the menu, show the hours and location, take orders or bookings, and capture guests' emails. Everything else is optional.

- **Hours, address and phone** at the top of every page.
- **Menu in text,** as covered above.
- **Order and book buttons** that go to your direct channels first.
- **Fast on phones.** Heavy video backgrounds and huge images slow pages down. Our guide to [Core Web Vitals](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained) explains what to fix.
- **Accessible.** The Justice Department has published [guidance on web accessibility under the ADA](https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/), and restaurants have faced lawsuits over inaccessible websites and ordering apps. Use readable text, alt text on images and ordering flows that work with a keyboard and screen reader.

## Measure what matters

Restaurants run on thin margins, so measure marketing in covers and orders, not likes. Track these monthly:

- **Covers and orders by channel:** dine-in, direct pickup, delivery apps, catering.
- **Direct order share:** direct orders as a share of all off-premise orders.
- **Repeat visit rate,** if your POS or loyalty program shows it.
- **Email list growth and redemptions** of email offers.
- **Review count, rating and velocity** compared with three nearby competitors.
- **Maps actions:** calls, direction requests and website clicks from your Business Profile insights.
- **AI mentions** for your top two or three "best [dish] near [neighborhood]" prompts.

::funnel Example: one slow-night email for a 60-seat restaurant (illustrative) | Emails delivered: 2,400 | Opened: 960 | Clicked or saved the offer: 180 | Redeemed in the restaurant: 45

Those numbers are made up to show the shape, not a benchmark. If the offer is opened but rarely redeemed, the offer or the timing is wrong. If it's barely opened, look at your subject line and sender name.

## A 90-day restaurant marketing plan

Here's a plan for a single independent restaurant. Each week takes an hour or two.

::steps 90 days to a stronger restaurant marketing engine | Weeks 1–2 — fix Business Profile hours, categories, menu, attributes and photos | Weeks 3–4 — put a text menu on your site and set direct ordering as the first button | Weeks 5–6 — launch list capture via Wi-Fi, QR codes and online orders, plus a welcome email | Weeks 7–8 — start review requests on checks and receipts and reply to every review | Weeks 9–10 — plan four weeks of social posts and one slow-night promotion | Weeks 11–12 — check Maps position, AI mentions and direct order share, then fix the weakest link

A hypothetical example: Rosa runs a 40-seat taqueria in San Antonio. Month one, she fixes her Sunday hours, adds a text menu and points her order link at her own pickup page. Month two, she adds a birthday-club QR code to every table and asks for reviews on receipts. Month three, she emails a Tuesday family-night offer to her new list. None of it is complicated. All of it compounds.

## Common restaurant marketing mistakes

- **Wrong hours on Google,** especially on holidays.
- **PDF-only menus** that don't load on phones.
- **Pointing every "Order" button at a delivery app,** then paying commission on your regulars forever.
- **Never capturing a guest's email,** so every visit is a one-off.
- **Arguing with reviewers** in public.
- **Buying followers or reviews,** which breaks platform rules and the FTC rule.
- **Posting only discounts** instead of the food and the people.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most effective marketing for a restaurant?

For most independents, an accurate, photo-rich Google Business Profile with steady reviews brings the most new guests. An email list you own brings them back, and together they cost far less than relying on delivery app promotions.

### Should my restaurant stop using delivery apps?

Usually not. Use the apps for discovery and delivery logistics, but make direct ordering easy and give regulars a reason to order direct, so repeat orders don't carry a commission every time.

### Can restaurants offer a discount for Google reviews?

No. Google's policies prohibit incentives for reviews, and the FTC's review rule bans buying reviews. You can ask every guest for an honest review without offering anything in return.

### How often should a restaurant post on social media?

Three to four times a week is enough for most restaurants. Consistency matters more than volume, and short videos of real food and people tend to reach the most new guests.

### How do I get my restaurant recommended by ChatGPT?

Keep your details consistent across Google, Yelp and other listings, publish a text menu that names your dishes, earn reviews that mention specific dishes and get mentioned in local food coverage. Then track prompts like "best tacos near me" to see if you're named.
