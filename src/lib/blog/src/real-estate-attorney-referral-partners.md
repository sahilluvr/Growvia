Real estate legal work flows through a small number of professionals. In states where attorneys handle closings, agents and lenders decide which closing attorney a buyer meets. Even where they don't, investors, builders and landlords need contracts, title issues resolved and leases drafted, and they ask the same handful of advisors for a name.

This guide shows how a real estate attorney can become that name: who to reach, what they need and how to stay inside the rules that govern real estate referrals.


## Why relationships decide where real estate legal work goes

Real estate transactions run through a small circle of professionals: agents, lenders, title companies and sometimes builders. Buyers and sellers usually meet their attorney through that circle, especially in states where attorneys handle closings. Even investors and landlords, who hire lawyers directly, usually ask their agent, lender or accountant for a name.

That means a real estate practice grows mainly through professional relationships. An agent who closes a few dozen deals a year can influence a meaningful share of a closing attorney's volume. A loan officer who trusts you can steer files your way across many agents. A title company that values your help with curative work recommends you to clients with complicated titles.

These relationships are also durable. Agents and lenders work under deadline pressure, and once they find an attorney who keeps closings on schedule and explains documents clearly to buyers, they rarely switch. The reputation you build with one agent spreads through their office, and offices talk to each other.

Investor and landlord work adds a second, steadier stream. Investors buy repeatedly, form entities, sign leases and occasionally need help with disputes. Once an investor trusts you, they bring you every deal, and they introduce other investors from their networks and meetups.

## Mind the referral rules

Real estate referrals sit under two sets of rules: your state bar's rules and, for many transactions, federal rules on settlement services that prohibit kickbacks and fees for referrals. In practice: don't pay or accept anything of value for referrals, keep marketing truthful and check your state's specific requirements. When in doubt, ask your bar's ethics hotline.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Anything resembling referral fees.** Avoid gifts or payments tied to referrals.
- **Slow communication in closing week.** It's the moment agents judge you on.
- **Jargon with buyers.** Clear explanations build your reputation with agents.

## Your first meeting with an agent

1. What causes delays at closing most often in your deals?
2. How do your buyers usually feel about closing documents?
3. How do you prefer to communicate during closing week?
4. Which types of deals are most common for you?
5. What has frustrated you about attorneys in the past?
6. Would a short training for your team be useful?

Listen for pain points like slow responses, confusing explanations or last-minute surprises. Your pitch is simply that you prevent those problems.

## Preparing your practice

Agents and lenders will check how you present yourself, and so will their clients. Make sure your website clearly explains closing services, investor services and fees. Keep a buyer-friendly closing guide and a seller checklist ready. Ensure your team can respond to agents and lenders quickly during closing week, ideally with a named contact. Review all outreach against your bar's advertising rules and keep in mind federal and state rules on settlement services.

## A worked example

Here's how an agent relationship might grow. The details are illustrative.

You email a busy residential agent offering a buyer closing checklist. She replies to your follow-up and shares the checklist with two buyers. A week later, her usual attorney is booked during a crowded closing week, and she asks you to handle a file.

You confirm the timeline, walk the buyer through every document in plain English and flag a minor title issue early, which you resolve with the title company before closing. The closing happens on schedule. The agent mentions your checklist and calm closing to her team at their weekly meeting.

Over the following months, three agents from the office send you files. One introduces you to a local investor who buys several rental properties a year, and you set him up with an investor package. Within a year, the brokerage and the investor make up a significant share of your closings.

## Keeping relationships strong

After each closing, send the agent a short note thanking them and confirming everything went smoothly. Check in quarterly with useful updates, such as changes in disclosure requirements or common title issues. Offer team training once or twice a year. Keep referral relationships free of anything resembling payment, and track which partners send work so you can give each relationship the attention it deserves.

## Handling closing week without surprises

Closing week is when agents and lenders decide whether to work with you again. Prepare a closing checklist for every file: contract dates, contingencies, title commitments, lender requirements, payoff letters, disclosures and settlement figures. Review the title commitment early and flag issues immediately, giving the agent and title company time to resolve them. Confirm the closing time, location or remote arrangements and who needs to attend.

Communicate proactively. Send the agent and lender a short status note early in the week and again the day before closing. Walk buyers through documents in plain language before they sign. If a problem arises, explain it clearly and propose solutions. Agents remember attorneys who keep calm under pressure and protect their deals.

## Working with title companies

Title companies are important partners, especially for curative work. Learn the preferences of the title companies you work with most, respond quickly to their questions and help resolve issues such as unreleased liens, estate transfers or boundary questions. Title companies often recommend attorneys who make complex files manageable, and they notice who responds promptly when closings are at risk.

## Serving investors as repeat clients

Investors value speed, clarity and consistency. Offer a standard intake for each deal, a clear fee structure for purchase reviews, entity formation and closings, and lease templates for rentals. Keep a file of each investor's entities and preferences so every new deal starts faster. Check in periodically about their plans. Investors who trust you bring every transaction, and they often introduce fellow investors from their networks.

## Landlord and tenant work as a referral source

Landlords need attorneys for leases, notices, disputes and evictions. Property managers and investors who own several rentals value attorneys who respond quickly and explain options clearly. Offer lease reviews, standard notice templates and guidance on local landlord-tenant rules. Landlords who trust you for these matters often bring you their purchases and sales too. Be mindful of local rules and timelines, which can vary significantly.

## Supporting builders and new construction sales

Builders need consistent purchase agreements, efficient closings and clear communication with buyers. Offer to review and standardize their contracts, coordinate closings for each phase and provide buyers with simple explanations of the closing process. Builders with active communities can become steady sources of repeat closings.

## Staying visible with agents through education

Short educational updates keep you top of mind with agents: changes in disclosure requirements, common contract issues, or tips for handling inspection negotiations. Send them occasionally and keep them practical. Agents who learn from you see you as a resource, not just a vendor.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do real estate attorneys get more closings?

Build relationships with agents, loan officers, title companies and builders. Be reachable during closing week, explain documents clearly and offer to serve as a backup attorney to start.

### Can real estate attorneys pay agents for referrals?

Generally no. Bar rules and federal settlement rules typically prohibit paying for referrals. Check your state's rules.

### What services do real estate investors need from attorneys?

Purchase contract reviews, entity setup, leases, title issue resolution and closings.

### Are educational sessions for agents worth it?

Yes. Practical sessions build trust and keep you top of mind when agents need an attorney.

### Do agents choose the closing attorney?

In many markets they strongly influence the choice, even if the client decides. Being easy to work with during closing week is what earns their recommendation.

### What should a real estate attorney's investor package include?

Purchase contract review, entity setup, closing services and lease templates, priced together for repeat buyers.

### Can real estate attorneys train agents?

Yes. Practical sessions on contracts, disclosures and title issues are valuable to agents and build trust. Check whether sessions can qualify for continuing education credit.

### How do I avoid problems with referral rules?

Don't give or accept anything of value for referrals, keep marketing truthful and check your state bar's and federal settlement rules.

### What should a real estate closing checklist include?

Contract dates, contingencies, title commitment, lender requirements, payoffs, disclosures, settlement figures and attendees.

### How can real estate attorneys prevent closing delays?

By reviewing title early, communicating proactively with agents and lenders, and resolving issues as soon as they appear.

### Do real estate attorneys help with title problems?

Many handle curative work, such as resolving liens or estate issues, often in coordination with title companies.

### Should investors form an entity for rentals?

It depends on their goals and situation. An attorney can explain options, alongside advice from their accountant.

### How do real estate attorneys communicate with buyers?

Through plain-language explanations of documents and the closing process, before and during closing.

### What makes agents recommend an attorney again?

On-time closings, calm communication, early problem-solving and buyers who felt well informed.

### Do real estate attorneys handle evictions?

Many do, following local rules and timelines. Landlords should consult an attorney familiar with local law.

### How can real estate attorneys work with builders?

By standardizing purchase agreements, coordinating closings and explaining the process to buyers.

### What educational topics interest agents?

Disclosure changes, contract issues, inspection negotiations and title problems.

### Should landlords use attorney-drafted leases?

Attorney-reviewed leases help ensure terms comply with local laws and protect the landlord's interests.

### How do real estate attorneys price closings?

Often with a flat fee for standard residential closings, with additional fees for complex issues disclosed upfront.

### How do I stay in touch with agents between deals?

With occasional useful updates, short trainings and a quick thank-you after every closing.

### What should a real estate attorney's buyer guide include?

What happens at closing, what each main document means, what to bring, common costs and who to contact with questions, written in plain language.
