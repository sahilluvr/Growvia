Most independent practices don't have a patient problem. They have a visibility and follow-up problem. People in your area are searching for "back pain doctor near me", "Botox near me" or "physical therapy for knee replacement" this week, and they are booking with whoever shows up first, looks trustworthy and answers fast. An all-in-one AI marketing setup for a medical practice exists to make that practice yours.

Healthcare adds a second problem other industries don't have. The same tools that make marketing easy in retail, like ad pixels, review replies and broadcast texts, can expose protected health information (PHI) if you use them carelessly. Practices have paid federal settlements over a single review reply.

This guide gives you the full system for clinics, chiropractors, med spas and physical therapy practices: where new patients come from, how to show up in Google Maps and AI assistants, how to collect reviews and reply safely, which paid channels make sense, how to build referral flow, and a 90-day plan. ## Rule one: keep PHI out of your marketing tools

Before any tactic, settle where patient information lives. Your EHR, practice-management system and patient-communication platform are built for PHI and come with business associate agreements (BAAs). Your marketing stack usually is not, and should not need to be.

Using PHI for marketing generally requires the patient's written authorization. HHS's [marketing guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html) explains the line: communications about the patient's own treatment, such as appointment reminders and recall notices, are generally allowed, while promoting products or services using patient data usually needs authorization. Our [HIPAA compliant marketing guide](/blog/hipaa-compliant-marketing-guide) walks through the definitions, BAAs, testimonials and the newer state consumer health data laws in plain English.

A simple working rule keeps most practices out of trouble:

- **Clinical and patient-specific** messages (reminders, results, care plans, recall based on a treatment) go through your HIPAA-compliant patient system.
- **General marketing** to people who subscribed through your website, events or social (newsletters, seasonal tips, event invites) can go through a marketing tool. Don't export your patient list into it: even a list of names and emails can be PHI because it shows who your patients are.
- **Public channels** (reviews, social posts, ads) never confirm that a specific person is your patient unless you have their written authorization.

## Where new patients actually come from

Every practice has the same basic patient journey. Your marketing should strengthen each step, not just pour more people into the top.

::flow How a new patient finds you | Need or referral | Search, Maps or AI assistant | Compare reviews and website | Call, form or booking | First visit and follow-up

Most of the leverage sits in the middle steps. Converting more of the searches you already get is cheaper than buying traffic.

For a typical small practice, the order of return is usually: Google Business Profile and Maps, reviews, condition and service pages, referral relationships, reactivation of past patients, then paid ads and social. Med spas are the exception, where Instagram often moves near the top.

## Google Business Profile and Maps: your front door

Google says local results are based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and its [local ranking help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) confirms that reviews and complete business information help.

Here is what matters most for practices:

- **Primary category.** Pick the most specific one that matches your main service (for example "Chiropractor", "Physical therapy clinic", "Medical spa", "Family practice physician"). Add secondary categories only where they are true.
- **Services and descriptions.** List the conditions and treatments you actually handle, in patient language ("sciatica treatment", not only "lumbar radiculopathy").
- **Hours, including holiday hours.** Wrong hours are one of the fastest ways to lose a patient's trust.
- **Appointment link.** Point it to your booking page or contact page.
- **Photos.** Real photos of the entrance, front desk, treatment rooms and team. Never photograph patients without written consent.

## Your website: condition pages, trust and safe forms

### Build pages around conditions and treatments

Patients search by problem. Create one strong page for each major condition or treatment you want more of: "Sciatica treatment in Plano", "Knee replacement rehab", "Laser hair removal". Each page should explain who it helps, what a visit looks like, how many visits are typical (in honest ranges), insurance and cost information, and who provides it, with credentials.

Health content falls under what Google calls "Your Money or Your Life" topics, so expertise and accuracy matter. Have a licensed clinician review each page, and show their name and credentials. Avoid promises of cures or guaranteed outcomes.

### Make booking easy without collecting PHI in the wrong place

The contact form is where many practices accidentally move health information into a non-HIPAA system. If your form goes to a general marketing inbox, ask only for name, phone, email, preferred location and preferred time. Don't add "describe your symptoms" or "reason for visit" fields unless the form runs on a HIPAA-compliant platform with a BAA.

The [website contact form best practices guide](/blog/website-contact-form-best-practices) covers the conversion side: fewer fields, clear next steps and fast replies. Add LocalBusiness or MedicalBusiness structured data to help search engines understand your practice, as explained in the [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-for-local-business).

### Watch what your website tracks

This is the biggest hidden risk for practices. Meta Pixel, Google tags and similar tools can send page URLs, button clicks and form events to ad platforms. HHS issued guidance on online tracking technologies in December 2022 and updated it in March 2024. A federal court vacated part of that guidance in June 2024 (the part about unauthenticated public pages), but tracking on patient portals and appointment flows remains high risk, and state health data laws add more exposure.

The [healthcare tracking pixels guide](/blog/healthcare-tracking-pixels-privacy) explains what the ruling changed, what it didn't, and how to audit your site in an afternoon.

## Reviews: more of them, and replies that never confirm a patient

Reviews are where healthcare marketing most often goes wrong in public. HHS's Office for Civil Rights settled with Elite Dental Associates in Dallas for $10,000 in 2019 after the practice disclosed patient information while replying to Yelp reviews. In December 2022, OCR settled with New Vision Dental in California for $23,000 for disclosing PHI in Yelp review replies. These were dental offices, but the rule applies to every covered provider.

The lesson is simple. Never confirm that a reviewer is your patient, and never mention a visit, condition or treatment in a reply, even when the reviewer brought it up first.

### Getting more reviews within the rules

- Ask every patient, not just the happy ones. Google's policies ban "review gating", which means filtering who gets asked based on how satisfied they seem.
- Never pay for reviews or offer discounts in exchange. The FTC's Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, buying reviews and review suppression, with civil penalties per violation. See the [FTC's rule page](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers).
- Ask at a high point: at checkout after a good visit, or when a patient completes a care plan.
- Send review requests in person (a QR code at checkout) or through your patient communication platform, in general wording. "Thanks for visiting us today" is fine. "Thanks for coming in for your sciatica" is not.

Our [guide to getting more Google reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews) has link, QR code and template details. The [dental reviews and HIPAA guide](/blog/dental-office-google-reviews-hipaa) has reply templates that work for any healthcare practice.

::chat A safe reply to a negative review | them: Waited 40 minutes and the doctor rushed my appointment. Never again. | you: Thank you for the feedback. We take wait times and communication seriously. | you: Our privacy policies mean we can't discuss anyone's care here, but our practice manager would welcome a call at (555) 010-2000. | them: Fine, I'll call tomorrow.

It doesn't confirm the person was seen and moves the conversation offline. For more scenarios, including suspected fake reviews, see [how to respond to negative reviews](/blog/professional-services-negative-review-responses).

## Paid channels: Local Services Ads, Search and social ads

Paid ads are a capacity tool. Use them when you have open slots, and only after your profile, reviews and website can convert the clicks.

### Local Services Ads eligibility

Google Local Services Ads are pay-per-lead ads that appear above regular search ads, with a "Google Verified" badge for businesses that pass screening. In the US, health categories include dentists, chiropractors, orthodontists, physical therapists, optometrists, podiatrists and dermatologists. Availability varies by location, so check [Google's Local Services help center](https://support.google.com/localservices/) for your category and city.

| Practice type | Local Services Ads | Google Search ads | Meta and Instagram ads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chiropractor | Eligible category in the US | Good for "chiropractor near me" and condition terms | Useful for awareness and offers |
| Physical therapy | Eligible category in the US | Good for condition and post-surgery terms | Limited, mostly awareness |
| Dermatology | Eligible category in the US | Good for specific treatments | Useful for cosmetic services |
| Med spa | Not a listed LSA category, check current options | Good for treatment terms like "laser hair removal" | Often the main paid channel |
| General clinic | Depends on specialty | Good for urgent and specialty terms | Limited |

### Healthcare ad policies

Google's personalized advertising policies don't allow targeting based on health conditions, and Meta removed many health-related detailed targeting options in 2022. Target by location and broad interests, and let your ad copy and landing page qualify people.

Never upload patient lists to ad platforms for "custom audiences" without legal review and patient authorization. A patient list from your EHR is PHI.

## Referral marketing: the channel most practices underinvest in

For PT clinics, specialists and many chiropractors, referral sources can outperform every digital channel combined. ### How to build referral relationships

1. **List your top 20 potential referrers.** Physicians, surgeons, chiropractors, massage therapists, personal trainers, midwives, podiatrists, depending on your specialty.
2. **Make a one-page referral sheet.** What you treat, how fast you can see new patients, insurance accepted, how you report back.
3. **Visit in person** or book a short lunch-and-learn with useful clinical content, not a sales pitch.
4. **Close the loop.** Send progress reports (through your secure clinical channel) so referrers see outcomes.
5. **Track referrals by source** every month and thank your top referrers personally.

A caution that matters: the federal Anti-Kickback Statute and the Stark Law restrict giving anything of value to induce referrals of federal healthcare program patients, and states have their own rules. Lunches, gifts and "marketing fees" to referral sources need a compliance check. The [HHS Office of Inspector General's physician education resources](https://oig.hhs.gov/compliance/physician-education/) explain the basics.

## Email, recall and reactivation: the right message through the right system

Your existing patients are the cheapest source of visits. The trick in healthcare is routing each message to the right tool.

::compare Which system sends what | Patient communication platform (with BAA): appointment reminders; recall based on last treatment; care-plan follow-ups; anything mentioning a condition | Marketing tool (no PHI): newsletter to website and event subscribers; seasonal wellness tips; event and workshop invites; new provider or location announcements

A general newsletter, like a PT clinic's "five stretches for weekend gardeners", builds a list of local people who know you. Grow it from website and event sign-ups. If you want to email your whole patient roster, use your patient platform or a vendor that signs a BAA. Make sure contacts opted in, include your address and a working unsubscribe link (CAN-SPAM requirements), and authenticate your domain so your emails land in the inbox. More patients now ask an AI assistant questions like "who's the best chiropractor in Scottsdale for sciatica?" The answers draw on your website, Google Business Profile, reviews and health directories.

To show up more often:

- **Keep facts consistent** everywhere: name, address, phone, hours, providers, insurance accepted.
- **Write clear condition and service pages** with plain answers to common questions.
- **Get listed accurately** on health directories (Healthgrades, Zocdoc, WebMD Care, insurer directories, your professional association's "find a provider" tool).
- **Check what assistants say** about you and your competitors for your main prompts.

## Practice-specific playbooks

### Chiropractors

Chiropractic is one of the most competitive local searches in most US cities. You win on Maps prominence, review volume and condition pages for back pain, neck pain, sciatica and headaches. New-patient offers ("$49 first visit") are common, but free or discounted services can raise issues with Medicare and Medicaid patients, and some states require specific disclaimers. ### Med spas

Med spas sell elective treatments to people who research on Instagram, TikTok and Google. Your marketing must handle before-and-after consent, avoid health claims you can't support (the FTC requires competent and reliable scientific evidence for health claims), and use treatment names accurately. ## A 90-day marketing plan for a medical practice

Here is a sequence that works for most independent practices. Adjust the pace to your team's capacity.

::steps 90 days to a stronger patient pipeline | Days 1–14 — compliance check: audit pixels, forms and review replies; separate clinical and marketing tools | Days 15–30 — fix Google Business Profile, categories, hours, photos and directory listings | Days 31–45 — publish or rewrite your top five condition or treatment pages with clinician review | Days 46–60 — start a review habit at checkout and reply safely to every review | Days 61–75 — build a referral list and visit your top ten referrers | Days 76–90 — launch a monthly newsletter and test LSA or Search ads if you have open slots

The first two weeks matter most, because removing one risky pixel or review reply protects you more than any campaign earns. After that, visibility work compounds.

## The KPIs that matter

Booked first visits pay the bills, not clicks. Track a small set of numbers every month.

::funnel Example: one month for a 3-provider chiropractic practice (illustrative) | Profile and website visits: 2,400 | Calls, forms and bookings: 180 | Qualified new patient inquiries: 95 | First visits booked: 60

- **New patients per month**, by source (Maps, website, referral, ads, social).
- **Cost per new patient** for each paid channel.
- **Inquiry-to-booking rate.** If it's low, your phones or follow-up are the problem, not your marketing.
- **Review count and average rating**, plus how many new reviews you got this month.
- **Plan-of-care or treatment completion** (for chiro and PT), measured in your practice software.
- **Referrals by source**, so you know who to thank.

Ask "How did you hear about us?" on intake and record a general category, never clinical detail.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a medical practice use regular marketing software?

Yes, for marketing that doesn't involve PHI, such as SEO, Maps, social posts and newsletters to people who subscribed on your website. Patient lists, appointment details and anything clinical belong in a HIPAA-compliant system covered by a BAA.

### Do I need patient authorization to send a newsletter?

A newsletter describing your own services generally doesn't need authorization, but if you send it to patients, the vendor handling your patient list needs a BAA. If you use PHI to target promotions for third-party products, HIPAA generally requires written authorization, so check with your compliance lead.

### Is it safe to reply to Google reviews as a healthcare provider?

Yes, if your reply never confirms the reviewer is a patient and never mentions a visit, condition or treatment. Thank them, state your commitment to quality and invite them to call the office.

### Which healthcare practices can use Google Local Services Ads?

In the US, Google's health categories include dentists, chiropractors, orthodontists, physical therapists, optometrists, podiatrists and dermatologists. Availability depends on your location, so check Google's Local Services help center.

### How long does it take to see results from healthcare marketing?

Google Business Profile fixes and review habits often show improvement within a few weeks to a few months. Condition pages and AI-search visibility usually take a few months to build, while paid ads can produce inquiries within days.
