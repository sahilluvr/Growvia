Personal lines keep an agency busy. Commercial lines build its value. A contractor, a restaurant or a dental practice needs general liability, property, workers' compensation and more, and renews every year. Small business owners also tend to stay with an agent who understands their industry.

This guide covers which businesses to target, how to approach them and which referral partners send commercial accounts. It assumes you follow your state's insurance marketing rules and your carriers' guidelines.


## Why commercial lines are worth the effort

Personal auto and home policies are important, but they're increasingly commoditized. Consumers compare prices online, switch for small savings and often buy direct from carriers. Commercial lines are different. A contractor, restaurant or professional firm needs several coverages that interact with each other, contracts that require specific limits and certificates, and an agent who understands the risks of their industry. That expertise is hard to replace with a website.

Commercial clients also tend to stay longer and bring more value. A business that trusts its agent renews year after year, adds coverages as it grows and refers other owners in its industry. The owner's personal policies often follow, creating a complete relationship.

Commercial work also builds an agency's reputation. Agents who specialize in an industry become known within it: contractors talk to other contractors, restaurant owners talk to each other. That word of mouth is powerful and lasts longer than any advertising campaign.

The challenge is getting in front of owners before renewal decisions are made. Most businesses renew with their current agent by default unless someone gives them a good reason to review. That's why timing, specialization and referral partners matter so much.

## Your first coverage review

1. What does your business do, and what are the main risks day to day?
2. When do your policies renew?
3. What coverages do you have now, and what limits?
4. Do your contracts require specific insurance terms?
5. Have you had any claims, and how were they handled?
6. What frustrates you about your current agent?

Use the answers to explain gaps and options clearly. Owners switch when they understand something specific they'd gain, such as faster certificates, better claims support or a coverage gap you identified.

## Preparing your agency

Business owners will research you. Make sure your website explains your commercial focus, the industries you serve and how reviews work. List carriers you work with where appropriate. Prepare a coverage review checklist, a contract insurance checklist and a sample certificate request process. Ensure you can issue certificates quickly, ideally the same business day. Follow your state's insurance advertising rules and your carriers' guidelines in all marketing.

## A worked example

Here's how a contractor relationship might develop. The details are illustrative.

You email a growing roofing contractor offering a free coverage review. He replies to your follow-up about subcontractors and tools, because he isn't sure his policy covers equipment stolen from job sites. You review his policies and find gaps in tools coverage and lower liability limits than some contracts require.

You present options from several carriers before his renewal. He switches, and the first time a general contractor asks for a certificate with specific wording, you issue it the same afternoon. He mentions your quick certificates to other contractors at his supply house.

Over the next year, several contractors contact you, and his bookkeeper starts referring clients who need coverage reviews. His personal home and auto policies move to your agency too.

## Keeping clients long term

Schedule a review before each renewal. Check in when clients mention growth, new vehicles, new locations or bigger contracts. Help clients through claims and follow up afterwards. Keep certificates fast and accurate. Thank referral partners and keep them informed with client consent. Clients who feel looked after renew and refer.

## Explaining coverage in plain language

Most business owners don't read their policies, and many don't fully understand what they've bought until they file a claim. Agents who explain coverage clearly build trust that lasts. During a review, translate each coverage into a real situation: "If a customer slips on a wet floor, this is the part that responds." "If your van is in an accident while an employee is driving, this is what applies." "If a fire closes the shop for a month, this covers lost income." Point out exclusions and limits in the same way, so there are no surprises later.

Put a one-page summary in writing after each review, listing coverages, limits, key exclusions and recommended changes. Owners keep these summaries, refer to them when contracts arrive and forward them to their accountants and partners. Clear explanations are also the best defense against the price-only conversation; once an owner understands what each coverage does, comparing quotes on price alone feels risky.

## Making certificates effortless

For contractors and many service businesses, certificates of insurance are a daily need. General contractors, property managers and clients ask for proof of coverage before work begins, often with specific wording for additional insureds. Agents who issue certificates slowly cost clients jobs.

Build a simple certificate process: a dedicated email address or form for requests, a standard turnaround commitment (same business day where possible), and saved templates for frequent certificate holders. Keep a list of each client's regular certificate holders so repeat requests take minutes. Contractors talk about agents who make certificates easy, and that reputation spreads through supply houses, job sites and trade associations.

## Helping clients through claims

The claim is where loyalty is won or lost. When a client has a loss, contact them quickly, explain the process, help gather information and stay involved until the claim is resolved. Even when the carrier handles the adjustment, the agent's presence matters. Afterwards, review whether coverage worked as expected and whether changes are needed. Clients who feel supported during a claim rarely shop their insurance again, and they often tell other owners about the experience.

## Working with contractors' contract requirements

Contractors increasingly face detailed insurance requirements in contracts: specific limits, additional insured wording, waivers of subrogation and primary and noncontributory language. Many contractors sign contracts without checking whether their policies match. Offer to review insurance sections of contracts before they sign, explaining any gaps and whether endorsements are available. This service prevents disputes and lost jobs, and it makes you an indispensable part of their bidding process.

## Annual review rhythm

Set an annual review rhythm with each commercial client: a check-in 60 to 90 days before renewal, a review of changes in the business, a market comparison where appropriate and a written summary of the renewal. Mid-year, send a short note asking about new employees, vehicles, locations or contracts. These touchpoints catch changes early and demonstrate ongoing value. Clients who see their agent regularly are less likely to respond to competitors' offers.

## Growing within a niche

Once you have a few clients in a niche, become active in that industry. Attend trade association meetings, sponsor local industry events or share short articles on common risks. Ask satisfied clients for introductions to peers. Niche reputation builds slowly but compounds: owners in the same industry trust an agent their peers recommend.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do insurance agents get commercial clients?

Pick a niche such as contractors or restaurants, offer free coverage reviews before renewal and build referrals with accountants, lenders and real estate agents.

### When should I contact a business about insurance?

Sixty to ninety days before their policies renew. Ask for the renewal date and set a reminder.

### Who refers commercial insurance clients?

Accountants, bookkeepers, business bankers, real estate agents, business attorneys and payroll providers.

### Why are certificates of insurance important for contractors?

Many jobs require proof of coverage before work starts. An agent who issues certificates quickly helps contractors win and start jobs.

### How early should insurance agents contact businesses before renewal?

Sixty to ninety days before renewal gives time to review coverage and compare options.

### Should insurance agents specialize in an industry?

Specializing helps you understand risks, speak the owner's language and build a reputation within that industry.

### What should a coverage review include?

Current policies and limits, contract requirements, gaps, claims history and options from carriers you represent.

### How do insurance agents get referrals from accountants?

Offer clear, plain-language reviews for their clients, respond quickly and keep them informed with client consent.

### What is a certificate of insurance?

A document showing that a business has certain insurance coverage, often requested by clients, general contractors or landlords before work begins.

### What does "additional insured" mean?

It's a way of extending certain coverage under a policy to another party, such as a client or general contractor, often required by contracts. Exact terms depend on the policy.

### How often should businesses review their insurance?

At least once a year before renewal, and whenever they add employees, vehicles, locations, equipment or new types of work.

### Do small businesses need cyber insurance?

Many businesses that store customer data or rely on computer systems consider it. A review can explain options and what's typically covered.

### How can agents compete with online insurance providers?

By specializing, explaining coverage clearly, issuing certificates quickly and supporting clients through claims, which online channels often can't match.

### What information do agents need for a commercial quote?

Typically business details, operations, revenue, payroll, employees, vehicles, property, loss history and current coverage. A checklist makes this easier.

### What is a waiver of subrogation?

A contract provision where one party agrees not to pursue recovery from another for certain losses. Whether a policy can accommodate it depends on the policy and endorsements.

### Should agents review contracts for contractors?

Reviewing insurance requirements in contracts helps contractors avoid gaps and disputes, within the agent's role.

### How often should agents contact commercial clients?

At least before each renewal and mid-year, plus whenever the business changes.

### How do agents become known in an industry?

By serving several clients well, attending industry events and sharing useful information.

### What changes should businesses tell their agent about?

New employees, vehicles, locations, equipment, contracts or types of work.

### How do agents handle a client's claim?

By contacting the client quickly, explaining the process, helping gather information and staying involved until the claim is resolved.

### Should commercial clients bundle policies with one agent?

Bundling can simplify management and sometimes pricing, but it depends on the coverages and carriers available.

### How do insurance agents choose a niche?

By looking at their experience, carrier appetite in their market and industries with enough local businesses to support growth.

### How do agents follow up after a coverage review?

Send a written summary, options with clear comparisons and a reminder before the renewal date.
